## Icon Composer for Beginners Group Lab (id: wwdc2026_8012, 61.7 min)

Basis: transcript (frames not viewed yet). Local automatic transcript made with Whisper, it is not Apple's official one; proper names and terms may carry hearing errors. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/8012/.

Central thesis: In a group question-and-answer lab led by Mike Stern (head of the Design Evangelism team), Apple designers and engineers argue that the app icon should be born from a simple idea, drawn with simple, elegant vector shapes, and that Icon Composer exists to transform that single, resolution-independent expression into an icon that adapts on its own to sizes, platforms, appearance modes and the system's own Liquid Glass material. The author's role is to provide a clean layered construction and well-thought-out annotations; the system takes on the adaptation and rendering, and the author refines whatever is needed by testing at real size.

The design process Apple describes:
Demonstration of Icon Composer (Liam, with the Photos app icon):
1. Drag layers coming from other design tools into the left column and organize them on the z axis, in groups and layers.
2. Configure the background on the canvas: solid color or gradient, or automatic mode, which uses system light in light mode and system dark in dark mode.
3. Select each layer and adjust appearance (color, opacity, blend modes, fill, including replacing the SVG) and composition (position, visibility), deciding whether it goes into Liquid Glass or not.
4. Select each group and adjust the glass properties: specular, blur, refraction, translucency and shadows.
5. Review the variations by platform (iOS, macOS, watchOS) and by mode (dark, mono, clear and tint), adjusting the color fills to optimize legibility.
6. Use the preview panel: solid or custom backgrounds, overlaid icon grid, size controls and comparison with OS26's backward-compatible rendering (term uncertain in the automatic transcription).

Workflow for drawing an icon from scratch (Lance, with additions from other participants):
1. Have an idea: a visual metaphor that represents what the app wants to communicate, which can also reference part of the app's interface.
2. Draw the icon in a design tool, thinking about the composition and about how the layers are drawn and organized.
3. Export the layers as individual files, at the full size of the canvas.
4. Drag the layers into Icon Composer, which works as a second stage: annotate the icon, control how color applies to each element for the appearance modes and control how Liquid Glass applies to each layer.

Verification on the device (answer about the most common mistakes):
1. Treat the icon like code: build, design and then test, without skipping the test.
2. Evaluate at all sizes and presentations, from smallest to largest, using the "waterfall" feature that, according to the speaker (with hesitation), Icon Composer has to show the common presentation sizes all at once, and alternating backgrounds.
3. Put the icon in a group on the home screen, where it is at the smallest possible size on the iPhone screen (guidance that the speech says predates Icon Composer).
4. Go outside with the iPhone, look at the icon with the arm moving under daylight and among dozens of other icons chosen at random, and ask whether it has presence and stands out.
5. Adjust color or shape to accentuate what makes the icon unique.

Updating an icon made for iOS 26 to the iOS 27 look (Lance and others):
1. Open last year's file in the updated Icon Composer and see the new rendering applied automatically, without touching annotations or artwork.
2. Review the specular highlights, especially with overlapping layers, check what the automatic setting does and switch to inside or outside according to the layers' colors.
3. Fine-tune the shadows and configure the refraction when there are overlapping layers.
4. Review the translucency manually, because the reduction Apple applied to its own icons does not happen automatically.

Integration in Xcode (John):
1. Drag the Icon Composer file into the project, alongside the Swift files, as a common resource.
2. Check the target association suggested by Xcode in the import dialog.
3. In the target editor, make sure the app icon name matches the file, usually "app Icon", without the "dot Icon" at the end.
4. Remove the old icon from the asset catalog, if it exists. There was no change to this process compared to the previous year.

Process for beginners with no design experience (several participants):
1. Define how to represent the app, how you want people to remember it and what essence to capture in a small space.
2. Close the computer and sketch by hand on paper with a pencil, without needing to be a good artist.
3. Spread out and stick up the sketches, look at them and ask friends what they feel, like the raw storyboards of Pixar movies.
4. Alternatively, write what the app does and what is unique about it, or talk to someone, record it and listen back looking for objects and metaphors used; sometimes the app's own name is the metaphor.
5. Only after settling what you want to communicate, move on to rendering and tools, asking how much can be simplified without losing the meaning.
6. Think about the layered construction and the composition.

Principles stated and why:
- Use simple, powerful vector shapes, simple and elegant geometries, and not fine, complex details. Why: the icon appears anywhere from a tiny rectangle in Settings or a Mac menu to a huge billboard, and complex detail is hard to adapt to that range of conditions; that is why some of the best logos in the world are the ones built with the most simplicity and still communicate with strength.
- Do not overdo complexity, especially with the new glass features. Why: these effects are delicate and powerful; refraction looks good because it scales well, and glass is very sensitive to the number of shapes, their size and how they overlap, so minimal movements on the refraction control change the result a great deal.
- Draw at the right size and export the layers at the full size of the canvas. Why: the layers arrive in Icon Composer already in the correct position, it saves time and avoids surprises on the device, where the icon needs to work small.
- Prefer scalable vectors (SVG) over bitmaps, although bitmaps are accepted. Why: moving from a raster world to a resolution-independent representation solves the problem of the icon that goes from tiny to huge, and the effects, composition and adaptation to the rendering modes are nearly impossible with a set of bitmaps delivered ready-made. For certain brand styles or app types, the bitmap is still the right choice.
- See the icon the way the customer will see it. Why: on a large Mac screen the icon appears much larger than its natural size.
- A living icon that adapts, instead of a static set of pixels. Why: the icon belongs to many people and audiences (whoever draws it, the engineering team or the product it is part of), it is visual and marketing identity and lives in an ecosystem; the user is also a stakeholder. Light mode, dark mode, clear adaptation and harmony with the system's design language are hard with static pixels, and a cart of bitmaps executed one by one does not evolve along with the system. The load is split: the author provides a simple, elegant expression, and Apple adapts and renders it with quality as the device's conditions change.
- Bring the interface's design language and material quality to icons fully, not superficially. Why: the material needs to work with each element, of different sizes and colors, and that requires giving the author precise control over the glass; since icons coexist in a shared space, a shared language makes the experience more harmonious.
- A single file for all platforms and modes. Why: before, it was necessary to make separate icons for watch, iOS and macOS and, with dark, clear and tint modes, imagine individual PNGs for each one, which sometimes did not work well in another context.
- Consistency between icons. Why: it makes it possible to guarantee, for example, the same dark background in dark mode across both Apple and third-party icons. One participant compares it to having a high-level API like SwiftUI instead of low-level system programming, from which more interesting things can emerge and adapt over time.
- Clear mode glass in icons does not respond to the system's Liquid Glass transparency control in iOS 27; it stays constant, by deliberate decision. Why: in the system's glass there is some notion of foreground and background content, but an icon can have anything, and it was necessary to guarantee a consistent, legible experience in every case; also, on iOS the icon deals mostly with different wallpapers, not with system glass underneath.
- Adapt the flat brand to the medium, as has always been done in graphic design. Why: a logo appears on a form, a business card, letterhead, a physical product, a car's logotype, motion in an ad, and in each medium the identity is executed in the most natural way so that it keeps coming through; the icon, as the app's face, is sometimes wearing sunglasses and a hat and remains recognizable as long as it connects clearly to the brand's other representations.
- Do not rely on color to convey meaning. Why: people with color blindness, such as red-green color blindness, may not perceive the color; the recognizable shape, with highlights, shading and real contrast, also needs to work in clear and tint modes. A good "shape algebra" or shape design language comes before colors, gradients and glass effects, so that the icon is immediately recognizable to everyone, which is presented as brand design rule number one.
- Maximize the dynamic range in mono mode annotations. Why: very dark darks and very light lights are transformed according to the color the user picks for icons from the wallpaper, and that sustains the contrast.
- Keep at least part of the foreground element white or nearly white. Why: some tint colors chosen by the user are naturally light in luminance, such as yellow, and that helps guarantee contrast; Apple does this in its own files.
- Prefer the backgrounds provided by the system and watch the luminance of your own gradients, especially in mono mode. Why: system backgrounds give maximum contrast and are already optimized for the new glass rendering.
- Keep a single visual identity across all system versions, without giving up the icon's fundamental shape. Why: the brand's current visual identity is the most important thing, and Apple delivers the icon with full quality and the correct corner radius also in old presentations, such as "I was 17" (term uncertain in the automatic transcription) and earlier ones, and on the product page on the App Store.
- Let the container shape guide the composition by platform. Why: the watchOS circle does not have the same layout or asymmetry possibilities as the rounded rectangle, and a circle sits well centered inside another circle.
- Simplify without losing the meaning. Why: not everything fits at small size; a piano app does not need all 88 keys in the icon, but one key might be too few.
- Good automatic defaults. Why: the automatic specular highlight should work without the author needing to touch it, by taking into account the color of the layer and the background and choosing inside or outside on its own.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when spoken:
- Canvas background: solid or gradient; automatic mode with system light in light mode and system dark in dark mode, backgrounds already optimized for the glass. New: automatic gradients, where you set the brand color and the app generates a background gradient.
- Layers and groups: Liquid Glass properties apply at the group level; separate layers into distinct groups to configure them independently. Imported layers become glass by default; it's possible to turn this off so they stay as flat graphics, useful for raster art that's already pre-rendered (cases like Camera and Preview) or something like a watermark, which wouldn't make sense to be glassy.
- Specular: light drawn around the edge of the object that gives definition. This year it's sharper and contains dark parts that help define the shape. Automatic by default; inside draws inside the shape and works well when the foreground is darker than the background; outside works better when the background is darker than the foreground.
- Blur: blurs the layers and gives the sensation of frosted, refractive glass.
- Refraction: two-dimensional control; the vertical axis controls the height of the refraction (the radius gets rounder as it goes up) and the horizontal axis controls the intensity, bringing more of the artwork into the shape. There's an inverse refraction, which wraps the edges in the opposite direction. The 2D control accepts modifier keys to lock one of the values. For the Photos icon, the result that worked both large and small landed in the middle of the range.
- Translucency: more transparent at the bottom and retaining color at the top, compared to the new Siri glass. This year Apple reduced translucency in many of its own icons so they'd be sharper and more legible, an adjustment that depends on the color and size of the layer; it's still used, for example, in the Podcasts icon to show the refraction between layers.
- Shadows: now they're "ring shadows," with more definition at the edges, reinforcing the material. Chromatic shadows pick up the color of the layers that bleeds into the background; neutral shadows are desaturated versions. Shadows also help show that one element passes underneath another.
- Blend modes, as described in the talk: normal replaces the pixel below without its contribution. The upper group is called additive and darkens: darken, multiply and plus darker (said as "plus L, or plus D"; term uncertain in the automatic transcription), which keeps the vividness of the color below and helps sell the glass, but does little against a dark background. The next group is called subtractive and lightens the object relative to the background: lighten, screen and plus lighter (said as "plus L"; term uncertain in the automatic transcription), which is like a screen that keeps the vividness, while screen becomes more desaturated. The modes give more color intensity and control over how the glass layers blend, since imported layers arrive opaque. Numeric example stated: red 255, 0, 0 and green 0, 255, 0 combined with screen would give 255, except in HDR. Red glass overlaid on yellow glass in the real world would tend toward a darker amber orange, something close to what multiply or plus darker, perhaps with an opacity adjustment, can help achieve. Plus lighter and plus darker are used extensively in the glass of system controls, with very similar math.
- Mono mode: a single grayscale annotation covers light clear, dark clear and all tint modes; the annotations need to meet contrast requirements.
- Tint mode: the tint color is exposed in Icon Composer, from desaturated to vivid; optimize for light colors like yellow while keeping the foreground white or nearly white.
- Complex shapes in tint mode: detail that's too fine or too many elements around it also hurt the contrast.
- Interlaced overlap (the Apple Developer app icon, where the pencil, brush and ruler pass over one another in a cycle): slice the elements into several individual layers and reassemble them so one shape appears to pass behind another, using shadows; seen from the side, the construction tells a different story than seen from above. Compared to an icon in the "MCS Hesher-esque" style (term uncertain in the automatic transcription).
- From flat brand to glass: expose layers that in the logo were rubber paths or subtractions; for example, eyes on top of a face instead of cut out of it. In the Podcasts icon, rubber paths became solid shapes to look like stacking. Simple shapes call for different properties than typographic marks, thin strokes or details.
- Templates for four applications: Figma, Sketch, Illustrator and Photoshop; the Illustrator one has a recommended script to position the vectors and keep the position relative to the canvas. Export as SVG or PNG. The design resources include a sample file with layers to compare last year's rendering with this year's.
- Preview: solid background or your own image to see refraction on the "chiclet" and contrast, an overlaid icon grid, size controls and comparison with the backward-compatible rendering.
- Composition by platform: move and scale elements in the composition panel; in Maps, the asymmetric layout of the rounded rectangle becomes, on the watch, the position marker centered in the circle.
- Legacy icons: without an Icon Composer file, the system automatically segments old icons into basic documents and renders them; in iOS 27 the foreground elements become more prominent and less translucent than in iOS 26, and for anyone who prefers the earlier, more translucent look, the talk says it's time to create the version in Icon Composer, which gives full control over the appearance.
- An iOS 26 icon made in Icon Composer adopts the iOS 27 look automatically, without recompiling the app.
- Getting it: it comes with Xcode or as a direct, standalone download; the Icon Composer site, cited from memory as "developer.apple.com slash Icon Composer," with a caveat from the speaker, has a download link, a mini tour and a comparison of the Photos icon in iOS 26, unadjusted in iOS 27, and optimized.
- Device Hub (also said as "Device's app"; term uncertain in the automatic transcription): simulates the appearance of icons in light, dark and appearance variants without compiling on the device, also useful for Watch.
- Usability improvements since the version released with iOS and macOS 26: selection annotations in the interface, snapping to guides and grids, copying and pasting attributes from one icon to another.
