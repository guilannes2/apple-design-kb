## Design with SwiftUI (id: wwdc2023_10115, 18.6 min)

Basis: transcript (frames not viewed yet). Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10115/.

Central thesis: Two designers from the Apple Maps team argue that SwiftUI is a design tool, not just a development framework, because designing directly on the device reveals hidden details, makes it possible to design how the interaction feels, test the design in real scenarios and present it with demos that explain themselves. All the designs for the new watchOS 10 Maps app were made in SwiftUI, directly on the device, which gave control over every detail, from spacing and text styles to animations and interactions.

The design process Apple describes:
1. Treat SwiftUI as a design tool: write declaratively in Xcode and see every change appear instantly in the visual canvas.
2. Get the details right by building on the device: start with a minimal prototype (a simple map on the watch), test it, fix what comes up (the zoom speed via the Digital Crown), add one layer at a time (points of interest, then interface controls, then the walking radius), bring that work back to the device, explore variations of a detail in separate prototypes and, finally, integrate those prototypes.
3. Design the interactions: prototype the feel of the interaction with gestures and also with real hardware sensors, adjusting animations, thresholds and haptic response until it feels right.
4. Test in realistic scenarios: take the design out into varied conditions, feed the components with real and diverse data, and create one-off, parameterized design tools to run through many variations.
5. Present with demos on the device: bring several demos together in a single SwiftUI app and take them to design reviews as often as possible, gathering feedback on the experience.
6. Ship what was designed: since the designer works with the same material as the engineer, the prototype can become an app submitted to the App Store.

Principles stated and why:
- Common things should be easy and complex things should be possible. Why: no time is lost recreating common interface elements and there is no limit when it is time to create something new. It is also what allows someone who does not code, like Will himself, to learn and design productively in a short time.
- SwiftUI balances two types of tool: it lowers the floor for simple tasks and raises the ceiling for creating new things. Why: tools that are only easy work for the simple and not the sophisticated; tools that are only powerful are hard to learn and bad at the basics.
- Declarative writing: you declare what you want to create (a Button, an Image). Why: this model relies on the intuition designers already have about how interfaces are put together.
- Modern interfaces are dynamic and require many decisions. Why: states like the image loading or the button pressed are what make the product feel complete; they stay hidden in static tools and show up right away in a dynamic tool.
- Building on the device gives a more complete understanding of what is being made. Why: the parts start to interact, the experience can be evaluated as a whole and it becomes clear what works and what still needs polishing.
- Interactions give the sense of how the design feels, not just how it looks. Why: this is why it helps that SwiftUI's animations are performant, easy to use and fully interruptible; and everything feels native because the work happens in a genuinely native environment.
- Design is also how the thing functions, and it is necessary to discover where it breaks. Why: it is easy to get carried away by ideal scenarios, with pretty photos and short labels; testing in the real world shows how the design holds up under everyday use.
- Demos on the device explain the design by themselves. Why: they build consensus, save meetings, reduce project risk and team confusion, and make the review spend its time evaluating instead of explaining.
- The tools of creative work have an enormous impact on the final experience. The reason given: using SwiftUI in the process would lead to a final product that is more thought through, complete and polished.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when spoken:
- The talk gives no numeric interface values (spacing, font size, opacity, duration, threshold or spring constant). The only numbers stated are contextual: watchOS 10, compass prototype ready in a few hours, dozens of Apple frameworks.
- Typography: on a line of text, adjust font size, weight, content and color, with immediate update in the canvas; modifiers to change the text format with specific fonts.
- Style and layout via modifiers: drop shadows, borders, aspect ratio and padding.
- Ready-made system components and behaviors: color picker, push transition. System elements adapt on their own across iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch and Mac, and in most cases the code can be shared.
- Hardware and frameworks as design material: haptic feedback, audio, cameras, gestures, accelerometer; MapKit for dynamic maps, WeatherKit for live weather, RealityKit for augmented reality objects.
- States that need to be designed: how the image appears while loading and how the button appears when pressed.
- Digital Crown and zoom: test several sensitivity values in the prototype until the zoom speed feels natural.
- Progressive detail on scroll: the walking time gains more detail as the Crown is turned, solved with a counter (ticker) animation.
- Split-screen search scrolling: the goal is immediate response without excessive sensitivity. The platter follows slow movements of the Crown; when crossing a threshold, a new platter enters quickly with haptic response. Fast movements use a firmer spring animation so the interaction feels agile. This way the scrolling feels right both slow and fast.
- Compass: prototype with an enlarged compass using the watch's internal sensors to test transitions and make the cardinal direction precise.
- Outdoor legibility: observe how brightness and sunlight affect the reading of the cartography and the interface, which often leads to adjusting contrast levels across the whole app.
- Charts with real data: in the elevation chart, adjust the Y-axis scale so that flat routes appear as expected, and not like a mountain climb.
- Parameterized design tool: for the walking radius, which needs to render well over a very dynamic surface like the map, vary line width, opacity values and blend modes across different map environments.

Examples cited:
- Maps on watchOS 10 with a more map-centered experience: a simple map on the watch immediately revealed that the zoom via the Digital Crown was too fast. It teaches that testing on the device exposes problems that a static screen does not show.
- Walking radius around the current location, indicating what is nearby and how long it takes on foot: it called for more detail on scroll, several options were prototyped and the preferred one, the animated counter, was born in a separate prototype before being integrated. It teaches how much complexity hides in an apparently simple design, and that it only becomes obvious when you prototype it.
- Location button on the new home screen, which activates a compass showing the cardinal direction the person is facing: using the real sensors made the compass precise and refined a microinteraction.
- Search for a place, like a restaurant, in a split screen with map and results list: a scroll that would be hard to do in other tools. It teaches how to adjust the feel separately for slow and fast movements.
- Elevation chart for people who walk or cycle, with the route's elevation range and real-time progress: with San Francisco's hilly routes it worked as expected; with New York's flat routes it looked much more extreme than it was. It teaches testing with real and diverse data.
- Contrast adjustment because of brightness and sun: Maps is used outdoors in very varied conditions.
- Mini walking radius tool: it allows running through far more variations than would be efficient in a static tool, and tools like this answer specific questions or solve complex problems.
- Design reviews with several demos in a single app (walking radius, search results, cards with business information): everyone wanted to try it and give feedback, and the time went to evaluating, not explaining.
- The microwave analogy, easy but limited, versus the industrial espresso machine, powerful but hard for the basics: SwiftUI would sit in the balance between the two.
