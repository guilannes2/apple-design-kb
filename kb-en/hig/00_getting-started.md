# Getting started

## Design principles (slug: design-principles)

What it governs: the eight fundamental principles that run through the whole Human Interface Guidelines and serve as the basis for weighing design decisions on any Apple platform: Purpose, Agency, Responsibility, Familiarity, Flexibility, Simplicity, Craft and Delight.

Why: Apple starts from the premise that the most successful and lasting designs are born from a deep understanding of how people think, feel and interact with the world. The principles are not fixed rules with a single correct application, but tools for arbitrating between competing priorities throughout the design process.

Do and avoid, by principle:

Purpose (do something meaningful)
- Start with intent: identify what matters most to the person you're designing for.
- Focus on what makes the product genuinely useful; constantly ask whether the design still serves the purpose.
- Prioritize the most important features aligned with real use, instead of spreading effort thin.
- Investigate existing solutions and avoid recreating them; define what differentiates your product.

Agency (letting people act their own way)
- Stay out of the way: take people straight to the task or the content.
- Give freedom to explore the interface without locking the person into fixed flows or modes; when a guided flow is necessary, make it easy to skip or escape it.
- Help the person recover from errors: make actions reversible and the recovery of previous states easy and cheap in time.

Responsibility (acting in people's best interest)
- Be fully transparent about what the product does and why, from the first interaction.
- Give a clear justification when asking for permission, and be clear about what is collected and how it is used.
- Collect only what's necessary for the product to work, anticipate forms of misuse and put protections against abuse in place.

Familiarity (building on what people already know)
- Use concepts that people already know from the real world and from other software.
- Keep visuals and interactions consistent: once a behavior is established, apply it throughout the design.
- Give clear feedback: show when controls are available, when content changes, and use system patterns for alerts and choices.

Flexibility (adapting to diverse contexts and needs)
- Design for everyone, treating accessibility as a priority from the start.
- Preserve the person's context as the design adapts across platforms and configurations, keeping content and controls in consistent, predictable positions, with natural animations in transitions.
- Consider multiple input methods (voice, touch, keyboard, and so on).
- Treat each platform with the same intent and level of care.

Simplicity (being clear and direct)
- Include only what's necessary; simplicity is not minimalism, it's focus on what's useful.
- Be concise: choose exactly the words necessary for a concept or control label.
- Establish clear hierarchy, with recognizable controls and consistent structure.

Craft (caring for every detail)
- Be deliberate in every decision, pursuing quality visuals, animations, text and audio.
- Experiment and iterate: prototype early, discard what doesn't work, test under real conditions.
- Keep the interface up to date with the platform's most recent capabilities and standards; design is an ongoing commitment, it doesn't end at launch.

Delight (making it human)
- Identify the emotion you want to inspire (energize, calm, excite) and let it shape the design.
- Create defining moments: every interaction, even a button or an error message, is an opportunity to express character.
- Don't confuse delight with decoration: don't let the pursuit of delight get in the way of the product's central purpose.
- Delight emerges from the sum of everything: freedom to act, safety to explore, familiarity and flexibility.

Exact specifications: there are no numbers, measurements or technical values in this article; it is purely conceptual.

Platform differences: none; the principles are declared as cutting across all Apple platforms.

Links to other articles: no direct citation of other HIG articles within the text (only one resource video, "Principles of great design").

## Designing for iOS (slug: designing-for-ios)

What it governs: the fundamental characteristics of the iPhone (screen, ergonomics, input methods, usage patterns) and the recommended practices for making an app or game feel "at home" on iOS.

Why: Apple starts from the observation of how people actually use the iPhone (in the pocket, in the hand, on the move, in short or long sessions switching between apps) to justify each recommended practice; the intention is for the design to reflect the real circumstances of the device's use, not an abstract ideal of interface.

Do and avoid:
- Limit the number of controls on screen to help focus on the task and the primary content; make secondary details and actions discoverable with minimal interaction.
- Adapt without friction to changes in appearance: device orientation, Dark Mode and Dynamic Type, letting the person choose the configuration that works best for them.
- Position controls thinking about how the device is held: it is easier to reach controls in the middle or at the bottom of the screen, which is why it's especially important to allow swiping to navigate back or start actions on a list row.
- With the person's permission, integrate information available via platform capabilities (payments, biometric authentication, location) in a way that improves the experience without asking the person to type data in manually.

Exact specifications: no number, measurement or specific value is given in this article (the characteristics are described qualitatively, such as "viewing distance of no more than a foot or two").

Platform differences: the article is specific to iOS; lists as system features specific to iOS: Widgets, Home Screen quick actions, Spotlight, Shortcuts, Activity views.

Links to other articles: cites Apple Design Resources and the iOS Pathway (developer documentation) as related resources.

## Designing for iPadOS (slug: designing-for-ipados)

What it governs: the fundamental characteristics of the iPad and the recommended practices for an app or game to take advantage of the large screen, the multiple input modes and iPadOS multitasking.

Why: the logic is the same as the iOS article, adapted to the scale and flexibility of the iPad, large screen, multiple combined input modes (touch, keyboard, Apple Pencil, voice) and usage sessions that range from quick actions to hours of immersion, require a design that takes advantage of the space without sacrificing ease of reach for controls.

Do and avoid:
- Take advantage of the large screen to elevate the content that matters, minimizing modal interfaces and full-screen transitions; position controls where they're easy to reach, but without getting in the way.
- Use the viewing distance and the input mode to calibrate the size and density of the displayed content.
- Allow Multi-Touch gestures, physical keyboard or trackpad, or Apple Pencil, and consider unique interactions that combine multiple input modes.
- Adapt without friction to changes in appearance (orientation, multitasking modes, Dark Mode, Dynamic Type) and transition naturally to run on macOS.

Exact specifications: none. The text describes the usage distance as "typically within about 3 feet" of the device, but does not give interface measurements.

Platform differences: system features cited as specific to iPadOS: Multitasking, Widgets, Drag and drop. The text explicitly mentions the app's transition to run on macOS as something to support.

Links to other articles: cites Apple Design Resources and the iPadOS Pathway.

## Designing for macOS (slug: designing-for-macos)

What it governs: the characteristics of the Mac (large screen, stationary use, multiple input modes, multiple open apps) and the recommended practices for an app to feel at home on macOS.

Why: the Mac is used in a stationary way, with a longer viewing distance and several windows and apps open at the same time; Apple derives the recommended practices from this use reality (more space, more input precision, more time of concentration).

Do and avoid:
- Take advantage of large screens to show more content with fewer nested levels and less need for modality, while keeping a comfortable information density.
- Let the person resize, hide, show and move windows according to their work style; support full-screen mode for a distraction-free context.
- Use the menu bar to give easy access to all of the app's commands.
- Support high-precision input modes for pixel-perfect selections and edits.
- Handle keyboard shortcuts to speed up actions and allow keyboard-only workflows.
- Support customization: customizable toolbars, configurable windows with the most-used views, choice of colors and fonts in the interface.

Exact specifications: no technical interface number; the typical use distance is described qualitatively as "about 1 to 3 feet".

Platform differences: macOS-specific system features cited: menu bar, file management, going full screen, Dock menus. Mentions the possibility of extending the workspace by connecting additional screens, including the iPad.

Links to other articles: cites Apple Design Resources and the macOS Pathway.

## Designing for tvOS (slug: designing-for-tvos)

What it governs: the characteristics of Apple TV (large screen at a distance, remote control and other inputs, immersive use) and the recommended practices for experiences that "feel at home" on tvOS.

Why: people typically stay several feet away from the TV (often 8 feet or more) and use a remote control, a game controller, voice or apps on other devices; Apple derives from this the emphasis on the focus system, on edge-to-edge art and on fluid animations for a cinematic experience that is legible at a distance.

Do and avoid:
- Support powerful and enjoyable interactions through the fluid, familiar gestures of the Siri Remote.
- Embrace the tvOS focus system, letting it smoothly highlight and expand items on the screen as the person navigates between them.
- Deliver edge-to-edge art, subtle and fluid animations, and immersive audio, for a cinematic experience that is clear, legible and captivating even at a distance.
- Make multi-user support easy: easy, infrequent login, handling of shared login, and automatic profile switching when the current viewer changes.

Exact specifications: the typical use distance is described qualitatively ("often 8 feet or more"), with no other interface numbers.

Platform differences: tvOS-specific system features: integration with the TV app, SharePlay, Top Shelf, TV provider accounts. Mentions picture-in-picture as a way to follow an app or an alternate video simultaneously.

Links to other articles: cites Apple Design Resources and the tvOS Pathway. Change log entry (change log) from September 14, 2022: refinement of the multi-user support guidance.

## Designing for visionOS (slug: designing-for-visionos)

What it governs: the characteristics of Apple Vision Pro (infinite 3D space, immersion, passthrough, spatial audio, eye and hand input, ergonomics and accessibility) and the recommended practices for creating comfortable, familiar spatial experiences.

Why: on visionOS, the device brings the content to the person instead of forcing the person to move to the content, and the person depends entirely on the device's cameras to see everything, real and virtual; that is why Apple treats visual and physical comfort as the top priority, and recommends finding the minimum level of immersion that serves each key moment of the app, instead of assuming that everything needs to be fully immersive.

Do and avoid:
- Take advantage of the unique capabilities of Apple Vision Pro (space, spatial audio, immersion), integrating passthrough and spatial eye and hand input so that they feel native to the device.
- For each key moment of the app, choose the appropriate minimum level of immersion; not every moment needs to be fully immersive.
- Prefer windows for contained, UI-centered experiences, with familiar controls; the person can freely reposition windows and the system uses scale to keep content legible near or far.
- Prioritize comfort: display content within the person's field of view, positioned relative to the head, avoiding the need for the person to turn their head or change position to interact.
- Avoid visual content that is overwhelming, abrupt, too fast, or that has no stationary frame of reference.
- Support interactions with hands at rest, in the lap or at the side of the body.
- If supporting direct gestures, make sure the interactive content is not too far away and that the person does not need to interact with it for extended periods.
- Avoid encouraging excessive movement during fully immersive experiences.
- Help people share activities by displaying spatial Personas of other participants, giving the sense of being together in the same space.

Exact specifications: the text does not carry numerical measurements (neither degrees nor meters); the only unit cited is qualitative (13 years of age as the minimum requirement for fitting/using the device, mentioned in the safety notice).

Platform differences: specific to visionOS. Its own concepts described: Shared Space (multiple apps side by side by default), Full Space (a single app, which can show 3D content blended with the environment, open a portal to another place, or enter another world), passthrough control through the Digital Crown. Accessibility technologies cited: VoiceOver, Switch Control, Dwell Control, Guided Access, Head Pointer.

> IMPORTANT (safety notice cited in the text): Apple Vision Pro should not be used while driving a vehicle or operating heavy machinery, nor while moving through potentially dangerous environments such as balconies, streets or stairs; the device is designed to be fitted and used only by people 13 years of age or older.

Links to other articles: cites Apple Design Resources, the visionOS Pathway and "Creating your first visionOS app". Change log: February 2, 2024 (link to the Apple Vision Pro user guide), September 12, 2023 (update to the opening art), June 21, 2023 (new page).

## Designing for watchOS (slug: designing-for-watchos)

What it governs: the characteristics of the Apple Watch (small screen on the wrist, quick and frequent use, Digital Crown, Always On) and the recommended practices for "glanceable" (at a glance) and specialized interactions.

Why: since the watch is used on the wrist, in quick glances throughout the day (interactions that often last less than a minute), and people frequently use complications, notifications and Siri more than the app itself, Apple recommends a streamlined design, with a shallow navigation hierarchy and actionable content anticipated to the person's needs.

Do and avoid:
- Support quick, glanceable, single-screen interactions that deliver critical information concisely and allow targeted actions with one or two gestures.
- Minimize the depth of the navigation hierarchy; use the Digital Crown for vertical navigation (scrolling or switching between screens).
- Personalize the experience by proactively anticipating the person's needs, using device data for actionable content relevant now or very soon.
- Use complications to deliver relevant data and graphics, potentially dynamic, directly on the watch face, visible every time the wrist is raised and tappable to go straight to the app.
- Use notifications to deliver timely, high-value information, allowing important actions without opening the app.
- Use background (color) to convey useful supporting information, and materials to illustrate hierarchy and sense of place.
- Design the app to work independently, complementing notifications and complications with additional details and functionality.

Exact specifications: no interface numbers; usage distance is described qualitatively as "usually no more than a foot" (no more than a foot away).

Platform differences: specific to watchOS. System features cited: Complications, Notifications, Always On, Watch faces. Specific inputs cited: Digital Crown, Action button, GPS, blood oxygen and heart function sensors, altimeter, accelerometer, gyroscope.

Links to other articles: cites Apple Design Resources and the watchOS Pathway. Change log of June 5, 2023: reinforcement of the guidance on glanceable experience and focus, and emphasis on the importance of the Digital Crown in navigation.

## Designing for games (slug: designing-for-games)

What it governs: how to create or adapt a game for the Apple platforms, covering entry into the game, readability on every screen, intuitive interactions, inclusion and adoption of Apple technologies (Game Center, iCloud, haptics, Spatial Audio).

Why: Apple organizes the guidance around the player's experience from installation (how long until they can play) to inclusion and integration with the ecosystem; the recurring logic is to reduce friction (less waiting, good defaults, teaching through play) and maximize who can play (accessibility, alternatives to physical controllers, avoiding stereotypes).

Do and avoid, by section:

Jump into gameplay
- Let the person play as soon as installation finishes; include as much playable content as possible in the initial install while keeping the download time to 30 minutes or less; download additional content in the background.
- Provide good default settings based on device information (resolution, automatic recognition of accessories and controllers, the person's accessibility settings); support the platform's most common interaction methods.
- Teach through the game: integrate setup and onboarding into a playable tutorial; if there is a written tutorial, offer it as an optional reference, not as a prerequisite.
- Defer requests (for sensors, personal data such as hand tracking) until the right moment, integrating the request into the scenario that requires it; ask for a rating or review only after the person has already spent quality time with the game.

Look stunning on every display
- Make sure text is always legible, with good contrast against the background and using at least the minimum size recommended on each platform.
- Make sure buttons are always easy to use, respecting the minimum button size recommended per platform.
- Prefer resolution-independent textures and graphics; if that is not possible, match the game's resolution to the device's. In visionOS, prefer vector art that continues to look good when the system scales it dynamically by distance and angle.
- Integrate the device's features (rounded corners, camera) into the layout, relying on the safe areas provided by the platform when possible.
- Make sure the game's menus adapt to different screen aspect ratios and remain legible and easy to use on every device (and in both orientations on iPhone and iPad, if supported); prefer dynamic layouts with relative constraints over fixed layouts.
- Design for the full screen experience.

Enable intuitive interactions
- Support each platform's default interaction method, paying special attention to control size and menu behavior when porting from a pointer-based context to a touch-based one.
- Support physical game controllers, but also offer alternatives, since not every player can use a physical controller.
- Offer touch game controls that embrace the touchscreen experience on iPhone and iPad, allowing direct interaction with game elements and virtual controls overlaid on the content.

Welcome everyone
- Prioritize perceivability: make sure the game's content can be perceived by sight, hearing or touch; avoid relying only on color to convey an important detail, and offer descriptive captions in cutscenes.
- Help players customize the experience (type size, control mapping, motion intensity, sound balance), taking advantage of Apple's native accessibility technologies.
- Give players tools to represent themselves: support the spectrum of self-identity in avatars, names and descriptions.
- Avoid stereotypes in stories and characters; review the game to identify and remove biases and stereotypes, and treat references to real cultures and languages with respect.

Adopt Apple technologies
- Integrate Game Center for cross-device discovery, connecting with friends, progress, achievements, leaderboards, challenges and multiplayer.
- Support GameSave to allow resuming the game exactly where it left off on another device, via iCloud.
- Support haptics (Core Haptics) so the player can feel the action, optionally combining it with custom audio.
- Use Spatial Audio to immerse the player in the game's soundtrack, adapting automatically to the current device.
- Take advantage of other Apple technologies (augmented reality, machine learning, HealthKit, location, camera, microphone) for unique game mechanics.

Exact specifications:
- Recommended initial download time: 30 minutes or less.
- Default and minimum text size by platform: iOS/iPadOS 17 pt (default) / 11 pt (minimum); macOS 13 pt / 10 pt; tvOS 29 pt / 23 pt; visionOS 17 pt / 12 pt; watchOS 16 pt / 12 pt.
- Default and minimum button size by platform: iOS/iPadOS 44x44 pt (default) / 28x28 pt (minimum); macOS 28x28 pt / 20x20 pt; tvOS 66x66 pt / 56x56 pt; visionOS 60x60 pt / 28x28 pt; watchOS 44x44 pt / 28x28 pt.
- Screen aspect ratios cited as examples to support: 16:10, 19.5:9, 4:3.

Platform differences: table of default and additional interaction methods by platform: iOS (default: Touch; additional: Game controller), iPadOS (default: Touch; additional: Game controller, keyboard, mouse, trackpad, Apple Pencil), macOS (default: Keyboard, mouse, trackpad; additional: Game controller), tvOS (default: Remote; additional: Game controller, keyboard, mouse, trackpad), visionOS (default: Touch; additional: Game controller, keyboard, mouse, trackpad, spatial game controller), watchOS (default: Touch; additional: none). Support for physical game controllers exists on every platform except watchOS. On macOS, iOS and iPadOS, full screen hides other apps and part of the system UI; on visionOS, a game in Full Space can completely envelop the person.

## Designing for iPhone Duo (slug: designing-for-iphone-duo)

What it governs: how to design for the iPhone Duo, a two-screen device (inner and outer) with a central hinge, covering device anatomy, poses, dynamic layouts, reserved regions, split views, arrangement views and the vertical positioning of toolbars, tab bars and navigation controls.

Why: the iPhone Duo has a variety of screen sizes and possible poses (folded, open, standing), so Apple prioritizes an adaptable layout over custom layouts for each pose; the central logic is to use size classes and containers that adapt automatically, instead of reinventing the app at every resize, preserving familiarity and the relative position of controls as the person moves the device between poses.

Anatomy
- The device has an outer screen (outer) and an inner screen (inner). With the device closed, the person interacts with the outer screen, and the system positions toolbars and tab bars on the side to maximize vertical space for content. Controls remain on the side also when the device opens in landscape, to keep the experience consistent between the screens.
- A central hinge supports various ways of holding and positioning the device, and also affects the space available for content as the device folds.
- The outer front camera sits in the corner, always visible, vertically aligned with the side controls. The inner camera sits behind the screen and stays hidden until activated.

Device poses
- People hold and rest the iPhone Duo in various ways: partially folded like a book, resting on a surface, or standing on its edges.
- Supporting the various poses does not mean designing a custom layout for each one: instead, use size classes so the app adapts naturally as it changes size. A compact width layout for the outer screen and a regular width layout for the inner screen provide the basis for every pose.

Do and avoid:
- Build the app to resize: use size classes, layout margins and safe area insets to arrange controls and content; avoid fixed widths and dependencies specific to one screen.
- Create a consistent experience between the screens: keep the functionality and state of elements the same between the two screens, preserving the app's information hierarchy, but showing an additional level of hierarchy on the larger inner screen when it makes sense for the content (example cited: Mail shows the email list OR an email when closed, and both side by side when open).
- Keep the same functionality across device poses: controls can overflow and content can change position or size as the interface adapts, but give access to the same controls and content regardless of how the person holds or views the device.
- Follow the system's vertical layout for toolbars, tab bars and navigation controls: since the outer screen is wider and shorter than that of other iPhones, the system moves controls to the side to preserve vertical space and reflect the screen's asymmetry; on the inner screen, controls remain on the side in landscape to preserve a continuous vertical height.
- Make the game playable in every device pose: it can lock to portrait or landscape orientation, but must fill the screen as the pose changes; keep text and control sizes as consistent as possible when resizing; prefer changing the screen's proportions (aspect ratio) rather than using letterbox or pillarbox; if that cannot be avoided, add art in the padding area so the experience looks full screen.

Dynamic layouts
- Account for a variety of hardware and software configurations; build layouts with layout margins and safe area insets, avoiding fixed widths or anything tied to a specific screen.

Reserved regions, beyond the standard safe areas:
- The outer front camera region: always present, expands into the Dynamic Island during Live Activities; when controls are on the side, the system automatically accounts for this region.
- The inner front camera region: present only when the camera is active; when inactive, the camera is not visible; when active, the UI moves away to indicate the camera's presence.
- The fold region (folding region): conditional, depending on device use; when partially open, this region divides the inner screen into multiple usable regions, excluding the central region as the screen folds.
- Many system components automatically adapt to reserved regions (alerts, context menus, sheets move to account for the fold; larger components such as Split views adapt column width and margins to match the inner screen's symmetry). For custom components, reserved region APIs exist to reposition content away from these regions.
- Adapt the layout when the device folds: prefer a layout container that adapts automatically (example cited: the Notes split view, which adjusts the width of each pane to remain clearly visible as the device folds); in a grid layout, prefer an even number of columns so content divides cleanly.
- Avoid extreme layout changes as the person folds the device: move only what is necessary to keep elements visible and easy to tap; controls that disappear or change drastically become harder to find and follow.

Split views
- On the inner screen, the split view expands; on the outer screen, it collapses to a single pane, the same way it adapts between regular and compact environments on other iPhones. When built with standard components, the split view automatically adapts to reserved regions, adjusting width and margins according to the fold.

Arrangement views
- An arrangement view is a layout container that holds two views inside it (a primary and a secondary) and organizes them dynamically according to screen size, orientation and reserved regions.
- Two types: split (divides the area horizontally when the arrangement is wider than it is tall, and vertically when it is taller than it is wide) and overlay (positions the primary view over the secondary one; when the screen is partially folded, the views move to occupy each side, otherwise the primary sits over the secondary).
- It is possible to limit which axes a split arrangement uses, and to collapse the secondary view in an overlay arrangement when it is not necessary to show it.
- Consider an arrangement view when the layout already looks like one: a layout that positions two views side by side or one above the other (such as an HStack or VStack) translates directly to a split arrangement; a layout that overlaps one view onto another (such as a ZStack) translates to an overlay arrangement.
- Keep navigation outside arrangement views: they organize content but do not handle navigation, so navigation containers (navigation split views, tab views) should sit around, not inside, the arrangement view.

Vertical controls
- On the iPhone Duo, toolbars, tab bars and navigation controls that normally sit at the top and bottom of the screen move to the side, preserving vertical space for content and keeping controls within reach. Exception: the inner screen in portrait, which has enough vertical space to keep standard horizontal bars.
- Controls on the side include system and app elements: Dynamic Island, status bar, toolbar (including navigation buttons) and tab bar.
- When two apps share the inner screen with Split View multitasking, each one positions its controls on its own outer edge (the app on the left has controls on the left).
- Because controls on the vertical axis stay aligned to the hardware, they keep the same position relative to the camera on the outer screen, and remain on the same side in right-to-left languages.
- Account for asymmetry in layouts: since controls sit on one edge, the space for content is asymmetric; use safe areas to ensure controls do not cover content, including controls on the opposite edge (such as when two apps share the inner screen).
- Keep controls consistent across device poses: since not every pose positions controls vertically on the side, and the available space varies, it is important to keep the controls' relative positions as similar as possible so the person does not need to relearn where the actions are.
- Follow the standard positioning order for toolbar items: reserve the top of the vertical axis for primary navigation controls (such as Back or Close), followed by prominent actions (such as Done); keep the remaining items in their original groupings, with vertical space between items coming from the top bar and the bottom bar to keep them distinct.
- Prioritize the most used toolbar items to keep them easily available: items overflow from bottom to top by default; it is possible to assign a visibility priority to each item to change that order, starting with whole groups and then individual items within a group.
- Preserve the most used actions first (such as Compose in Mail or New Note in Notes), and keep visible for longer the controls that convey important status (such as items with badges).
- In general, do not override the standard positioning of bars: the position of controls on the vertical axis is one of the central patterns of the iPhone Duo.
- Consider using the full width of the screen for interfaces where bars are not necessary, especially visual and immersive layouts that do not scroll, as long as nothing conflicts with the Dynamic Island or the status bar (example cited: Calculator occupies the full width of the screen). It is possible to combine both approaches, letting a background image or header occupy the full width while scrollable content has an inset.
- Group related toolbar items instead of spacing them manually: groups created with ToolbarItemGroup (SwiftUI) or UIBarButtonItemGroup (UIKit) provide space between items and other groups automatically, and adapt as the available space changes.
- Position controls near the content they affect: when controls belong to a content area different from the back edge (trailing edge), keep them with that area instead of moving them to the side (example cited: controls that affect the email list in Mail sit above the leading pane, not on the side).
- Provide a title and a symbol for every toolbar item that is not text-only: this lets the system choose the right representation for the context; include a title even when the item shows a symbol, because the system uses the title in overflow menus and expanded forms.
- Keep text-based buttons to a minimum: labels that include text sit in a horizontal bar, so prefer a symbol whenever possible.
- When space is limited, preserve the toolbar or the tab bar according to the experience the view offers: in navigation-focused experiences, move toolbar items to the overflow menu to keep the tab bar and primary destinations accessible (default bar compression behavior); in task-oriented experiences, minimize the tab bar to preserve the toolbar actions central to completing the task.
- Use the system overflow menu: if the app has its own overflow menu, move those actions to the system menu so the person finds everything in one place; reserve the ellipsis symbol (ellipsis) for overflow, and give other menus a distinct symbol.

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

1. Apple derives practice from observation of real use, not from abstract aesthetics: each platform article (design-principles aside) starts by describing display, ergonomics, inputs and interaction patterns before getting to the "best practices", the design rule is always justified by how the person's body and attention relate to the device (designing-for-ios, designing-for-ipados, designing-for-macos, designing-for-tvos, designing-for-visionos, designing-for-watchos).

2. Automatic adaptation is preferred over manual customization by context: instead of asking for a specific layout per pose, size or device, the recurring guidance is to use mechanisms that adapt on their own (size classes, safe areas, dynamic layouts, arrangement views on iPhone Duo; Split View and multitasking on iPadOS; window resizing on macOS) (designing-for-ipados, designing-for-iphone-duo, designing-for-macos).

3. Consistency of position and behavior across contexts is treated as a usability requirement, not a luxury: keeping controls in the same relative place across screens, poses or modes keeps the person from having to relearn the interface (designing-for-iphone-duo, designing-for-watchos, designing-for-visionos).

4. Permission and data collection are treated as a design decision, not just a policy one: the timing and the justification of a permission request are part of the UX flow, including in games (designing-for-games, design-principles in the Responsibility section).

5. Each platform has a characteristic "attention time unit" that shapes the guidance: iPhone (minutes to more than an hour, switching between apps), iPad (from quick actions to immersive hours), Mac (minutes to hours of concentration), Apple TV (immersive hours, but with picture-in-picture), Apple Watch (interactions of less than a minute, several times a day), visionOS (immersion adjustable by the moment), the duration and frequency of use directly justify the recommended information density (designing-for-ios, designing-for-ipados, designing-for-macos, designing-for-tvos, designing-for-watchos, designing-for-visionos).

6. Physical comfort is raised to an explicit design principle, not an implicit one, especially where the risk is greater: visionOS dedicates a whole section and a formal safety warning to comfort and to preventing use in dangerous contexts (designing-for-visionos), which does not appear with the same intensity in any other platform in the group.

7. Minimum text sizes and hit target sizes are standardized per platform, not universal: the same concept (legibility, reachability) results in different numbers per device, reflecting the distance and interaction method typical of each screen (designing-for-games, which tabulates text and buttons per platform, in direct coherence with the ergonomics described in designing-for-ios, designing-for-ipados, designing-for-macos, designing-for-tvos, designing-for-visionos, designing-for-watchos).

8. The eight principles of design-principles work as a "grammar" that reappears, paraphrased, in each platform article: Agency and error recovery appear as "let people resize/hide/show windows" and "recover from mistakes" on macOS; Flexibility appears as support for multiple input modes across every platform; Simplicity appears as "limit controls on screen" on iOS and "minimize hierarchy" on watchOS (design-principles, designing-for-ios, designing-for-macos, designing-for-watchos).

9. Onboarding and permissions are deferred until the moment of real need, instead of front-loaded: both designing-for-games ("defer requests until the right time") and the Responsibility principle in design-principles treat the timing of the request as part of the design, not a technical detail.

10. Standard system components carry accessibility, layout adaptation and navigation policies "for free": using the system standard (toolbars, split views, size classes) is repeatedly presented as the path that automatically solves adaptation and accessibility, while customization requires reimplementing those guarantees manually (designing-for-visionos, designing-for-iphone-duo, designing-for-ipados).

11. Inclusion is treated as part of the design, not as a compliance add-on: designing-for-games dedicates a whole section ("Welcome everyone") to perceivability, personalization, self-representation and removal of stereotypes, echoing the Flexibility principle ("design for everyone") from design-principles.
