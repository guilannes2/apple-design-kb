None of them carries its own guidance, principle, rule or specification text: each file has only the title, the source link, sometimes a one-line sentence describing the category, and the list of child articles of that section. The substantive content of each topic (Boxes, Alerts, Buttons, Typography, etc.) is in the individual articles, covered in other groups of this survey.

## Human Interface Guidelines (slug: human-interface-guidelines)
Collection index page, but it is the root page of the entire HIG, and so it carries a bit more structure than the others in this group. It opens with the sentence "The HIG contains guidance and best practices that can help you design a great experience for any Apple platform." and organizes three highlighted blocks before the list of topics:
- "Design fundamentals": points to Design principles, Designing for iPhone Duo and Designing for iOS, with the note "For more, see Getting started."
- "Foundations of design": points to Accessibility, App icons, Color, Layout, Materials and Typography, with the note "For more, see Foundations."
- "New and updated": lists recent additions and updates to design guidance, pointing to Branding, SharePlay, Layout, Menus, Siri and Snippets, with the note "For more, see What's new."

After these three blocks, the page lists the first-level topics of the HIG: Getting started, Foundations, Patterns, Components, Inputs and Technologies. It carries no technical specification, only the document's navigation architecture.

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

1. The HIG is structured as a tree, with layers of pure index before any concrete rule: the root page (human-interface-guidelines) points to six top-level categories, each category points to dozens of individual articles. The rule itself always lives in the leaves, never in the index nodes (every slug in this group).
2. Apple separates "what I can do" from "how to do it right": Getting started and Foundations come before Patterns and Components in the reading order suggested on the root page, signaling that principle and target platform need to be resolved before choosing a component (human-interface-guidelines, getting-started, foundations).
3. Patterns is treated as a layer separate from Components: one covers user tasks and behaviors (Drag and drop, Onboarding, Undo and redo), the other covers the interface controls the platform already provides ready-made (patterns, components).
4. Within Components, Apple subdivides by interface function, not by platform: Content, Layout and organization, Menus and actions, Navigation and search, Presentation, Selection and input, Status and System experiences are functional categories that span iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS and watchOS at the same time (components and the seven subcategories listed in it).
5. The Technologies list mixes platform services (iCloud, Sign in with Apple, In-app purchase), hardware and sensors (NFC, Tap to Pay on iPhone), health and research frameworks (HealthKit, CareKit, ResearchKit) and even a generative AI item (Generative AI) at the same hierarchical level, showing that the HIG treats "technology to integrate" as a broad category and not just UI APIs (technologies).
6. Inputs is organized by human input modality, not by device: gestures, voice, gaze (Eyes), digital crown, remote control and motion sensors all enter as "ways of controlling the app", which reflects the philosophy of designing for the interaction and not for the specific hardware (inputs).
7. Getting started reserves a section per platform (Designing for iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS, watchOS, games and even iPhone Duo), confirming that Apple treats each platform as a design context with its own particularities before getting into a specific component or pattern (getting-started).
8. The "New and updated" section on the root page exists as a mechanism for tracking changes in guidance over time, signaling that the HIG is a living, versioned document, not a static manual (human-interface-guidelines).
9. Within Foundations, items such as Accessibility, Inclusion, Right to left and Privacy appear side by side with purely visual elements such as Color, Typography and Materials, at the same hierarchical level, which indicates that accessibility and inclusion are not treated as an appendix, but as a design foundation at the same level as color and typography (foundations).
10. System experiences groups operating system surface formats that live outside the app's main window, such as Widgets, Live Activities, Complications, Controls and Notifications, treating them as their own design family, distinct from the components that stay inside the app (system-experiences).
