# wwdc2019 (part 1)

## Mastering the Living Room With tvOS (id: wwdc2019_211, 48.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 40 of 40 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/211/.

Central thesis: the Apple TV must deliver a "content first" experience, in which the content appears immediately upon opening the app and the barriers between the viewer and what they want to watch are removed, to end the choice paralysis of traditional TV grids.

The design process Apple describes: the design team started from the observation that current TV is tedious, with row after row of content, and that this "paralyzes through excess of choice". The guiding question was how to get people to what they want to watch faster, removing barriers to entry. From there, the team redesigned the Top Shelf using concrete examples (the redesigned Top Shelf itself and the TV app) to demonstrate each principle instead of just stating it.

Principles stated and why:
- Remove barriers to entry: the Top Shelf became interactive and immersive because the goal is for "something to already be playing" when the app opens, reducing hesitation.
- Reduce the number of steps for the task: every swipe or click on the remote adds cognitive load, so interactions should be reused (e.g. the swipe up gesture used both in the Top Shelf and in the TV app's playback), so the person doesn't need to learn something new.
- Avoid sensory overload: chaotic and distracting interfaces get in the way of focus. The analogy used was eleven people talking at the same time, impossible to understand.
- Diversify content types and break the grid: mixing modules of different proportions among the standard 16:9 rows gives visual breathing room and highlights important items.
- Create hierarchy by reducing the canvas to simple, non-competing elements: in the Top Shelf, content has maximum priority and occupies the screen, the dock and the picking row come after, with less emphasis.
- Metadata is useful but secondary: it only appears when the person shows interest (resting their thumb on the remote), so it doesn't compete with the content right away.
- Pagination dots and chevrons give a sense of place: they communicate that you're looking at an item within a set and indicate swipe directions, preventing the person from wondering "is this clickable?" or "how do I go back?".

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- In the redesigned Top Shelf, upon entering, the person stays on a poster image for 5 seconds, then a trailer starts, without forcing audio at this initial moment (audio can be invoked later by explicit user action).
- There are two Carousel styles in the Top Shelf API: Actions Carousel (focus on the image/video in full screen, with title, context title and Play and More Info buttons) and Details Carousel (adds rich metadata: summary, genre, duration, media options, including support for 4K HDR, closed captions and audio descriptions, and a list of named attributes, such as Starring and Director).
- Recommended number of items in the Top Shelf Carousel: between 5 and 10, so as not to overload with noise.
- Carousel images must be 1920 by 1080 points, full screen, available at 1x (1080p) and 2x (4K) resolution.
- Standard safe area for tvOS applications: 90 points of padding on the side edges (leading/trailing) and 60 points on the top and bottom edges; it is acceptable for content to extend beyond this area, but the system may hide it or make it non-interactive.
- Carousel videos must be 1080p HD, delivered via HTTP Live Streaming (HLS), and be 2 to 5 minutes long, since the Carousel is for preview, not for a complete viewing experience.
- The API offers four UI styles in the Top Shelf: Carousel Actions, Details Carousel, Sectioned and Inset Content.
- Code architecture: TVTopShelfContentProvider (root object, responds to the system's asynchronous content requests), TVTopShelfContent (protocol with three concrete classes, one per UI style) and TVTopShelfItem (classes per style, including Carousel Item, Item Collection, Sectioned Item, Content Item).
- New TVCollectionViewFullScreenLayout and the TVCollectionViewFullScreenCell cell, with two views: Masked Background View (receives the opaque image) and Masked Content View (receives UI elements); the cell occupies 1920 by 1080 points.
- Two central concepts of this layout: masking (Masked Amount 0 when full screen edge to edge, 1 when inset) and parallax (Parallax Factor determines how much the Masked Background moves slower than the Masked Content, creating depth).
- Best practices for the FullScreenLayout: avoid starting animations or video before the cell reaches the center, avoid multiple videos playing at the same time (causes frame loss), avoid excessive use of visual effects or group opacity animations, and release large images from memory when not in use.
- TVUserManager (new in tvOS 13) provides an identifier for the current user and notifies when it changes; the app is responsible for mapping this identifier to the app's internal profile (usually saved in user defaults), with several identifiers possibly pointing to the same profile.
- New Tab Bar in tvOS 13 moves along with the content (instead of disappearing when it loses focus); the tabBarObservedScrollView property controls whether it scrolls with the content or stays fixed at the top; leadingAccessoryView and trailingAccessoryView allow inserting brand logos next to the Tab Bar.

Examples cited: the Top Shelf redesign (shows how content first applies in practice); the TV app (source of reused interaction patterns, such as the show/hide menus gesture during playback); Preference Panel and the TVUserManager confirmation dialog (show how to ask for consent to associate a user identifier with a profile without repeating the question later).

Short quotes:
"We've become paralyzed by choice and robbed by indecision."
"It's not to make people wait in frustration... it's to throw open the doors, let them in, and let the content shine."

## Apple Design Awards (id: wwdc2019_104, 49.3 min)

Basis: transcript and 52 of 52 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/104/.

Central thesis: the Apple Design Awards exist to publicly recognize and celebrate apps and games whose design is both beautiful and functional, evaluated on design, technology and innovation.

The design process Apple describes (judging criteria, not a building process): winners are selected by evaluating three areas. Design: visual strength, efficiency and ease of use, quality of gameplay, attention to detail and polish, internal coherence. Technology: efficient use of Apple's newest technologies, fast and responsive apps that take full advantage of device resources. Innovation: brilliance, novelty of ideas, platform differentiation, things that simply were not possible before.

Principles stated and why: the presentation argues that "beauty" in design is not just appearance, it is also function, each component "deeply considered" and "carefully crafted"; judging by appearance alone is not enough, "it has to work as beautifully as it looks". A great design is described as coherent and cohesive, that helps people relate to each other in new ways.

This video does not describe interface construction techniques (layout, typography, etc.); it is an awards ceremony with qualitative descriptions of nine winning apps and games.

Examples cited (2019 winners) and what each one teaches, according to the narration:
- Ordia (Loju): platform game with simple drag-and-drop controls; uses color to communicate the role of each element (green/yellow friendly, pink danger), has a colorblind mode tested even at monochrome scale, and Taptic Engine feedback "just right".
- Flow (Moleskine): iPad drawing app that uses Apple Pencil 2, iOS drawing APIs and Metal to simulate realistic tools (mechanical pencil, felt-tip pen, markers, calligraphy); has 1,400 unique color names, and optional sound effects reinforce the interaction with the tool.
- The Gardens Between (The Voxel Agents): game whose main mechanic is controlling the flow of time (not the movement of the characters), with a custom soundtrack and "natural" haptics reinforcing the narrative.
- Asphalt 9 Legends (Gameloft): hyper-realistic arcade racing, its own engine with Metal 2 integration, 60 frames per second, more than 50 vehicles and 70 tracks, Touch Drive system that automates acceleration and braking to focus the player on curves and shortcuts.
- Pixelmator Photo: iPad photo editing app; trained a custom Core ML model on more than 20 million image pairs for the ML Enhance feature, automatically controlling more than 32 different adjustments (white balance, hue, saturation, etc.); uses Metal and Core ML.
- ELOH (Broken Rules): physics and geometry game with no onboarding or instructions, no scoring or time limit, with 99 levels; the soundtrack is composed by the player themselves while playing.
- Butterfly IQ (Butterfly Network): handheld portable ultrasound (cost cited as US$ 2,000, versus more than US$ 60,000 for a traditional device) that moves the ultrasound hardware to a microchip and the processing to an iOS device; the AI guides the user to obtain a diagnostically useful image and allows manually correcting measurement points, such as the cardiac ejection fraction.
- Thumper (Drool): rhythm game with its own engine in Metal at 60 frames per second, support for real-time portrait/landscape orientation switching, custom gesture system to detect swipes, haptic feedback to reinforce impacts.
- HomeCourt (NEX Team): basketball app that uses the iPhone camera with Core ML and the Vision framework to automatically track shots, makes and misses, and the position from which the ball was thrown, without needing anyone to track it manually.

Short quotes:
"It has to work as beautifully as it looks, and great design reflects the broader definition of beauty."
"Great design draws us in."

## Visual Design and Accessibility (id: wwdc2019_244, 10.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/244/.

Central thesis: three features, Dynamic Type, Reduce Motion and Differentiate Without Color, make apps more visually accessible, and for many users they are not a preference, but the difference between being able to use the device or not.

The design process Apple describes: the presentation follows the three features in sequence, explaining the human problem each one solves before showing the API. For Dynamic Type, the question is how to ensure that large text stays legible and the UI stays balanced. For Reduce Motion, the question is how to adapt the experience for the one third of people with some degree of sensitivity to motion. For Differentiate Without Color, the question is how to convey information without depending only on color, for people with color blindness.

Principles stated and why:
- Dynamic Type, four principles: make as much text dynamic as possible (if the text can grow, it should grow); use as much of the screen width as possible; do not truncate text as it grows, keeping the same amount of text as the standard UI; scale glyphs next to the text so the UI stays balanced.
- Reduce Motion: when enabled, the app should adapt its animations; the logic is that a small subset of people can have their sensitivity triggered just by looking at a screen with motion.
- Auto-play video previews: give the user control over automatic video playback, because for some this sparks curiosity and for others it can be annoying or cause anxiety.
- Prefer Cross-fade Transitions: give even more granular control over the type of motion in screen transitions.
- Differentiate Without Color: when color blindness makes colors look similar, the app should add an additional indicator (shape, for example) anywhere color is the only way to convey information; and, when possible, the ideal is to not depend on color alone from the start.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- iOS offers 11 different text styles for Dynamic Type, from titles to captions and footnotes.
- To use Dynamic Type in UILabel, UITextField and UITextView: use the preferred font of one of the 11 text styles and set adjustsFontForContentSizeCategory to true, so the view updates even with the app in the foreground.
- Since iOS 11, it is possible to map custom fonts to the 11 text styles, scaling with UIFontMetrics when a font is defined for the style, and falling back to the system's default font when it does not exist.
- New Environment Overrides panel in Xcode lets you drag a Dynamic Type slider and see the result instantly in the simulator.
- The new accessibility setting is in Settings, Accessibility, Motion, Auto-Play Video Previews, enabled by default; there is a public API to check whether it is enabled, and the app should listen for the change notification.
- Differentiate Without Color is checked with a call to "should Differentiate Without Color" and a notification for when the setting changes.

Examples cited: "peanut status" app created by the presenters themselves, used to demonstrate green/yellow/red circles that, with Differentiate Without Color enabled, become a green check, yellow question mark and red X; App Store, whose featured game trailers play automatically when scrolling the screen, used as an example of auto-play; Settings app, used to show the standard lateral transition that becomes a dissolve with Prefer Cross-fade Transitions; the game Causality, whose gameplay is indicated by color on the screen, and which offers a color-blind mode with symbolic flags above players and targets.

Short quotes:
"If the text can grow, it should grow."
"You should not rely on color alone to signal meaning."

## Introducing SF Symbols (id: wwdc2019_206, 39.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 28 of 28 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/206/.

Central thesis: SF Symbols is a library of more than 1,000 vector symbols designed to behave like text (same weights and scales as the San Francisco font), eliminating the complexity of managing fixed images for each size and UI context.

The design process Apple describes: the team redesigned all the system symbols in iOS 13 (rounder and heavier, to become more visible) and then extended that language to create SF Symbols, designed from the start to "match" text at any point size, because symbols appear side by side with text of varying sizes throughout the UI.

Principles stated and why:
- Symbols must be specified in typographic points, like text, so they align naturally on the same baseline as the text next to them.
- There are three scales (small, medium, large) because the same symbol point size sometimes needs more or less emphasis, depending on the available space; the symbol's point size does not change between scales, only the scale does.
- Each scale keeps the weight matched to the text and the stroke thickness adjusts to the weight, it is not simple linear scaling; all scales are optically centered on the text's cap height.
- Symbols come in weights that match the San Francisco font's weight family (from ultra-light to black), each weight drawn manually.
- Symbols work with Dynamic Type and are localizable (example given: the Voice Memos time control reflects the language's numeral system, English versus Arabic).
- Prefer centering (vertical and horizontal) over edge alignment when positioning symbols next to text; build layouts from the smallest element to the largest; let the image size change, do not cache or rasterize symbols unnecessarily.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Export source: SVG template from the SF Symbols app itself, containing all weights and scales named by layer, editable in design tools and used directly by Xcode as the source artifact.
- To minimally support a custom symbol you need to provide regular at medium scale; to support the bold text feature, also provide bold; to support all Dynamic Type styles, include medium and bold.
- The UIImage(systemName:) API to use system symbols with two lines of code; UIImage(named:) prioritizes a custom symbol over a non-symbol image of the same name, and never returns a system symbol.
- By providing two images with the same name (an old bitmap, a new symbol), the same code runs on iOS 12 (picks up the bitmap) and iOS 13 (picks up the symbol), with no version check.
- SymbolConfiguration lets you configure scale (small/medium/large), point size with optional weight or scale, or match a font via SymbolConfiguration(font:); configurations are immutable, and combine with the applying method.
- preferredSymbolConfiguration on UIImageView applies the configuration when rendering the symbol, with no effect on images that are not symbols.
- Symbol sizes are expressed in font points (typographic), not in screen dimensions; that is why you should not manually constrain a symbol image's width and height, this can decenter the symbol and is less performant; the recommendation is to leave the symbol at its natural size.
- baselineOffsetFromBottom (UIImage) expresses the baseline offset from the base of the image, an optional property (CGFloat?) because not every image has a baseline; withBaselineOffsetFromBottom creates a new image with a defined baseline, making regular images behave like symbols for layout purposes.
- Buttons in UIKit already come with a predefined symbol configuration: system buttons use "body large" (reacts to Dynamic Type); regular buttons use medium scale by default; PreferredSymbolConfiguration(forState:) lets you change this.
- Bar buttons use a large configuration in non-compact size classes (portrait on iPhone) and automatically switch to medium in a compact size class (landscape), without needing to provide a second image.
- Symbols have no intrinsic color: the rendering mode of regular images usually resolves to "always original", but for symbols it resolves to "always template", using the tint color (black when no tint color is defined).
- withConfiguration applies a configuration (for example, 34 points, bold weight) to an image to redraw it larger and heavier, useful when drawing with draw(at:) instead of draw(in:).
- NSTextAttachment(image:) creates a "smart" text attachment that inspects the surrounding text (font size, weight and color) to complete the symbol's configuration automatically.
- withTintColor applies color efficiently without needing to rasterize the image manually; there is an optional parameter to change the rendering mode at the same time.

Examples cited: a slideshow app with tortoise and hare images, used to demonstrate UIImage(systemName:); a fictional coffee app with a custom "teacup", used to demonstrate the whole flow of SVG template export, import into Xcode and use of UIImage(named:); Wi-Fi networks list, cited as a reference for a system button with a symbol (the info button); a comments app, used to demonstrate vertical center alignment versus baseline between avatar and text.

Short quotes:
"They're specified in typographic points, like text."
"For symbols, you have to let go of describing the image using a dimension."

## Designing iPad Apps for Mac (id: wwdc2019_809, 30.3 min)

Basis: transcript and 25 of 25 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/809/.

Central thesis: bringing an iPad app to the Mac is not just running the same code, it is understanding and resolving the real differences between a system designed for touch and a system designed for keyboard and mouse, keeping what already works (Auto Layout, drag and drop, multi-window) and adapting architecture, toolbars, layout, typography, color, gestures, icons, contextual menus and the menu bar.

The design process Apple describes: first, ensure a solid foundation in the iPad app (complete Auto Layout, drag and drop support, support for multiple windows), because this foundation determines what already comes "for free" when running on the Mac (split views become split views, the file browser and the activity view become the platform equivalents, iOS edit menus and contextual menus become Mac contextual menus). Then, map the structural differences between the two systems and decide, point by point, whether the direct translation is enough or whether it is worth rethinking the navigation (for example, turning tabs into a sidebar).

Principles stated and why:
- iOS was designed for touch, Mac for keyboard and mouse: touch requires larger targets that are easier to reach while in motion; mouse and trackpad provide physical stability and greater precision, allowing smaller controls and higher information density.
- Controls on the bottom, left or right edges make sense on iPhone/iPad because people hold the device and reach those areas with their thumb; on the Mac nobody holds the screen, so placing controls on those edges "offers no ergonomic benefit at all".
- Sidebars are a powerful navigation tool on the Mac because they accommodate many grouped and labeled options, giving additional context; using a sidebar can flatten the hierarchy of an app with tabs.
- The sidebar background should be translucent, never filled with a solid color, image or pattern, because translucency and the change in vibrancy (glyphs, selection highlights) signal which window has keyboard focus.
- Toolbars create a top-down flow of information, which is the norm in Mac apps; actions in the toolbar should not change depending on the app area, only enable/disable; actions relevant only in specific areas should go into a contextual action menu, not the toolbar.
- Actions at the bottom of the screen (common on iPad) are problematic on the Mac because windows are draggable and the bottom edge can end up off screen or behind the dock.
- On the Mac, color should be more neutral because people tend to have many windows, files and folders open at the same time; excessive color would fragment and overload the experience. At the same time, translucency integrates the app into the ecosystem because it lets the user's wallpaper influence the appearance.
- Selection highlight colors are a personal preference on the Mac; using a highlight color different from the system's feels out of place and confusing.
- Menu bar structure should be stable: commands are not added or removed after the app is opened, only enabled/disabled, so people learn where commands are even when unavailable.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- The base font on macOS is 13 points; on iOS it is 17 points; to maintain consistency, the system scales the content area by 77% (13 divided by 17) when the iPad app runs on the Mac; when creating mockups, recreate this 77% scale (for example, by placing the content area in a smart object in Photoshop).
- macOS does not support Dynamic Type: it always uses the "large" size of the setting, just scaled by 77%.
- Very small iOS text sizes (footnote, caption one, caption two, and even the Mac's own "mini" size) can become hard to read on the Mac; it may be necessary to increase the text a little.
- App icons on the Mac, by default, appear as a square image masked by a continuous curve shape, with a subtle shadow; to go further, Mac icons have unique silhouettes (not just squares with rounded corners) and are often rendered in 3D with realistic lighting and materials; on 1x displays, icons in the Finder are only 16 pixels in height by width, so it is worth creating icons with pixel hinting at the smaller sizes.
- UIKit gestures remap automatically to trackpad or mouse events: tap becomes mouse-down, long press becomes mouse down and hold, pan becomes mouse drag, swipe becomes drag in the corresponding direction; pinch and rotate on the trackpad use the cursor position as the center (on iPad they use the midpoint between the touches); screen-edge swipes are not remapped.
- Mouse hover events can reveal additional information (example: the Stocks app shows the price of a specific point when hovering the mouse over the chart, equivalent to the tap on iOS).
- Six tips for contextual menus: avoid overloading with too many options; be succinct (one-word labels, verbs that indicate the action); command order is meaningful, most important items at the top, related items grouped together; use separators to make relationships explicit; use submenus to control menu size and hide less relevant actions.
- Menu bar: every app has a menu bar; Apple recommends listing in writing all the possible actions in the app and which objects they apply to, then fitting them into the standard macOS menus (App, File, Edit, Format, View, Window, Help) and creating custom menus when there are object types specific to the app with specific actions (example: Mail has a Mailboxes menu and a Messages menu because the actions do not overlap between the two object types).
- Assign keyboard shortcuts to the most used commands, always following the precedent of the standard shortcut list in the macOS HIG, because shortcuts work on both the Mac and the iPad.

Examples cited: Finder, used to illustrate the contextual action menu depending on selection (with a file selected versus nothing selected); Stocks app, to illustrate hover revealing information; Mail app, to illustrate a custom menu by object type (Mailboxes, Messages); Keynote app, to illustrate a custom menu organized by workflow (align, distribute, group, move slide objects).

Short quotes:
"Every area of the screen is just as easy to reach as every other area."
"Mac interfaces should be more neutral. They shouldn't compete with the content that they present to people."

## Introducing Core Haptics (id: wwdc2019_520, 29.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 18 of 18 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/520/.

Central thesis: Core Haptics is an event-based audio and haptic rendering API that lets the developer design their own haptic patterns synchronized with audio, instead of relying only on UIFeedbackGenerator's predefined patterns.

The design process Apple describes (in this session, mostly technical, more engineering than visual design process): the session follows a decision sequence: first decide whether the use case calls for UIFeedbackGenerator (communicating a simple intent: selection, impact, notification, leaving modality composition to the system) or Core Haptics (when the developer wants to be "their own sound and haptic designer", with fine timing control to synchronize with Core Animation or AVAudioEngine). From that decision, the implementation flow follows fixed steps: create the content (pattern), create an instance of the haptic engine as soon as the app knows it will need haptics, create a player associated with a pattern and an engine, start the engine, start the player (in immediate mode or scheduled with an absolute timestamp), and optionally receive a callback when the player finishes.

Principles stated and why:
- Core Haptics does not replace UIFeedbackGenerator; UIKit, in fact, is built on top of Core Haptics, so both share the same low-latency performance.
- Designing your own haptic patterns takes more time, but it is worth it when it allows something that would not be possible otherwise and differentiates the app.
- A single pattern can generate an infinite number of audio and haptic variations through dynamic parameters adjusted in real time.
- Separating content from code (AHAP files) makes it easier to share, edit and version haptic patterns across people and tools.

Concrete interface-building techniques (API/data level, with exact numbers cited):
- Core Haptics works on all iPhones equipped with Taptic Engine starting with iPhone 8, with the guarantee that the pattern will feel the same across those products.
- Fundamental content block: CHHapticEvent, with a type and a time, and optional parameters; overlapping events mix together; events are grouped into a pattern.
- Four event types: HapticTransient (like a gavel, momentary and instantaneous), HapticContinuous and AudioContinuous (longer, like bowing a string instrument, with more modulation controls, for example resonance), and AudioCustom (custom audio played in sync with the haptic).
- Two central event parameters: HapticIntensity (analogous to audio volume, from 0 to 1, from no output to maximum output) and HapticSharpness (a new concept, with no physical or audio analog, from a round and organic feeling at 0 to a crisp and precise feeling at 1); examples given from iOS 12: the flashlight button on the lock screen is an example of high sharpness, the App Switcher's swipe up gesture is an example of low sharpness.
- Dynamic parameters adjust intensity and other parameters of active and future events of a pattern already running, starting from a specific timestamp; example given: reducing intensity to 0.3 starting at 0.5 seconds, for example when a character speaks in a game.
- AHAP (Apple Haptic Audio Pattern) is a schema on top of the JSON format, with a version key, an array of event dictionaries, each event with a time in seconds relative to the start of the pattern and a type; HapticContinuous and AudioContinuous events also require a duration in seconds.
- Typical code flow: import CoreHaptics; declare CHHapticEngine as a member variable of the view controller; assign a closure to stoppedHandler (called if the engine stops due to an audio session interruption or app suspension); start the engine; create the pattern and the player at the moment of need (for example, when a ball collides with the wall); start the player with CHHapticTimeImmediate to play as soon as possible, without needing to keep a reference to the player ("fire and forget").

Examples cited: simple physics app with a ball that collides with the edges of the screen (used to demonstrate intensity and sharpness proportional to impact speed); Swift Shot (AR sample code enhanced with haptics modulated by the speed of pulling the slingshot); the iPhone 7's haptic home button, the Apple Watch Series 4's haptic crown and UIDatePicker, cited as existing examples of audio-haptic duality at Apple.

Short quotes:
"You're going to feel the tension building up as you stretch it back, and then a very satisfying thunk as you release."
"So the application can simply fire and forget it."

## What's New in iOS Design (id: wwdc2019_808, 27.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 23 of 23 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/808/.

Central thesis: in iOS 13, the iOS design system was completely revised around three axes (Dark Mode, new card-style modal sheets, and the new context menu control), keeping iOS recognizable but with more internal consistency, more color options for information hierarchy, and accessibility on equal footing with the other goals.

The design process Apple describes: the design system is defined as "a set of pieces, like colors, fonts and glyphs, that work together in a logical and consistent way". The goals that guided the redesign were stated explicitly: keep iOS familiar and intuitive; increase internal consistency among native apps; offer a range of color options to articulate information hierarchy (more important elements stronger, less important ones receding); put accessibility on the same level as the other goals (full support for Bold Text and Increase Contrast); and keep the system simple and easy to implement.

Principles stated and why:
- Semantic color describes the purpose of a color, not its exact value, which allows it to be dynamic (for example, a background color can be black in Dark Mode and white in Light Mode), avoiding the need to keep two parallel lists of colors.
- When thinking about Dark Mode, it is more useful to imagine that the lights were dimmed than that everything was inverted; that is why colors are not simply inverted between modes (for example, the background of table rows is lighter in both modes).
- Fill colors (fill) and most separator colors are semitransparent so they contrast well against variable backgrounds; the six fully opaque system gray colors, on the other hand, should be used when transparency would cause undesired results, such as optical illusions in overlapping grid lines.
- There are two parallel sets of background color, "base" and "elevated", because direct shadows work well to separate overlapping light interfaces, but are less effective in dark interfaces; that is why lighter background colors (elevated) signal that an interface is in the foreground.
- Vibrancy (instead of solid color) should be used over system materials, because solid color can become "murky" and illegible depending on the background.
- If a custom control recreates what UIKit already offers for free, that consumes time and effort for little benefit; but custom controls remain necessary when UIKit does not cover the case (the example given is a star rating indicator).
- Modals are for switching modes, they should not be used just because the animation or visual style is appealing; a good use of a modal sheet is when the app changes from a viewing mode to a distinct creation/editing mode (example from Calendar).
- Every action available in a context menu must remain accessible somewhere else in the main interface, because context menus are powerful but people may not think to access them.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- The iOS 13 semantic color palette includes primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary variants of label color (for titles, subtitles, placeholder text and disabled text, respectively) and of system background color (system background is pure white in Light Mode and pure black in Dark Mode).
- Recommended color contrast for tint colors: aim for a contrast ratio of 4.5 to 1 or higher; tint colors become lighter in Dark Mode and darker in Light Mode, and have high-contrast variants for when the Increase Contrast setting is active.
- Four system materials with different levels of translucency: thick, regular (the default, works well in most cases), thin and ultra-thin; the choice depends on how much separation from the background is appropriate for the content.
- Navigation bars are now drawn by default without background or shadow when using a large title; the background and shadow reappear (fade in) when content scrolls underneath the bar.
- SF Symbols includes more than 1,500 symbols in this version, with built-in baseline values for correct vertical alignment with text, small/medium/large scale variants, and nine weights per symbol, matching the weights of the San Francisco font; symbols become bolder when the Bold Text setting is active, because they are vector-based.
- Modal sheets in iOS 13 have by default the new card-style appearance (card-style), which reveals a preview of what is behind; they can be dismissed by pulling down at any point on the screen; if the content has already been scrolled, the first pull down scrolls back to the top, and an additional pull dismisses the modal; this gesture can be blocked when there is a mandatory decision to be made in the modal, showing instead an action sheet asking how to proceed.
- Contextual menu replaces the old Peek and Pop (which depended on 3D Touch and did not work on iPads and some iPhones); the gesture to open it is tap and hold, it works on any touch device; it is made up of a menu of commands and an optional preview of the selected item; on iPhone, preview and menu are stacked in portrait; on iPad, they are side by side when there are more than three commands.
- Contextual menus on iOS include glyphs next to each command and label destructive actions in red; they can be hierarchical, leading to a secondary submenu.

Examples cited: app Contacts, used to show the switch between "base" (alone) and "elevated" (in modal presentation inside Phone) background color; Mail on iPad, used to show how the background color changes between base and elevated depending on whether the app is alone, in slide-over, in side-by-side split view, or composing an email in a sheet; app Settings, used as an example of a navigation bar with large title and no background; app Calendar, used as an example of correct modal sheet use (viewing events versus creating/editing an event) and of a child view for event detail within the same viewing mode.

Short quotes:
"A rational and consistent design system helps people to learn and use apps with ease."
"Modals are for switching modes. Don't use them just because you like the animation or the visual style of them."

## Designing Audio-Haptic Experiences (id: wwdc2019_810, 26.0 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/810/.

Central thesis: sound and haptics, when designed together and in sync with the visual, create a new dimension of experience, guided by three principles: causality, harmony and utility.

The design process Apple describes: the designers always start from the question "what would this object be in the physical world and how would it sound and feel?" before designing sound and haptics. The process is demonstrated with the real case of the Apple Pay confirmation: first they asked what sounds people associate with payment and what the interaction of paying is like; they tested several sound candidates (one sounded "too happy and frivolous", another "matched the checkmark animation but was too harsh") until arriving at the chosen one; then they designed the haptics, initially trying to imitate the sound's waveform, but discovering, through experimentation, that two simple taps worked better. The process for the Apple Watch Series 4 crown started from research on real mechanical watches and objects like bicycle hubs as sound inspiration, before designing the sound itself; the motion team then adjusted the animation to "snap" (snap) into the sound and the haptics.

Principles stated and why:
- Causality: for feedback to be useful, it must be obvious what caused it; the sound and haptic experience must match the qualities of the objects involved, the dynamics of the action (a strong or weak hit) and the environment, because straying too far from that correspondence sounds "strange" and "doesn't work" (demonstrated by deliberately inverting the sound of a ball kick).
- Harmony: things must "feel the way they look and sound"; in the real world, audio, haptics and visuals are naturally in harmony through the cause-and-effect relationship, but in the digital world this work has to be done manually, in an additive way (visual, then audio, then haptic); synchronization between the three senses is where "the magic happens" and its absence completely breaks the illusion (demonstrated by introducing latency on purpose).
- Utility: add audio and haptics only when there is clear value and benefit to the experience, with restraint; often the right decision is not to add sound and haptics, so as not to overload people and dilute the value of what is really important.

Concrete interface-building techniques (sound and haptics):
- Two primitive haptic building blocks: transient (short, compact, like a tap or impact) and continuous (extends over a set duration).
- Two controllable design dimensions: haptic intensity (amplitude) and haptic sharpness (from a "round and organic" feel at low values to a "precise and crystalline" feel at high values), applicable to both transient and continuous.
- The "ghost effect" technique: when chaining several transient events in quick succession (the example given is a sequence of four taps), people usually don't notice the first event, perceiving the sequence as if it had three; this can be used deliberately as a priming effect, "waking up the skin" before the main event, as in the case of an important third-party notification on watchOS.
- Contrast technique between similar experiences: to distinguish a left-turn navigation cue from a right-turn one on watchOS, when the sounds are similar but different, the haptics can double the taps (from single to double) to reinforce the contrast between the two sides.
- Sound-haptic pairing recommendation: a chime with an abrupt attack pairs well with a transient of high sharpness; a softer sound pairs better with a continuous that rises and falls in intensity, but these are not rigid rules, the Apple Watch alarm is cited as a case where reversing the expected order (haptic first, creating anticipation, then the sound) worked better.

Examples cited: soccer ball kick (illustrates causality); Apple Pay sound and haptic confirmation (illustrates the full decision process of causality and harmony); sphere falling and colliding with the bottom of the screen (illustrates harmony and synchronization between visual, audio and haptic); Apple Watch Series 4 crown (illustrates harmony using a mechanical watchmaking reference); ARKit virtual timer app (illustrates utility, feedback only where there is value); Apple Watch alarm (illustrates experimentation outside the general sound-haptic pairing rule); third-party notification on watchOS (illustrates the "ghost effect" as priming); left/right navigation cues on watchOS (illustrates contrast between similar experiences); full-screen effect in Messages (illustrates synchronization at a special moment).

Short quotes:
"For feedback to be useful, it must be obvious what caused it."
"Harmony requires great care and attention from you, but when done well, can create very delightful and magical experiences."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

In this 2019 batch, Apple is clearly pushing two simultaneous axes: a "system" axis (SF Symbols, Dark Mode, semantic colors, the new card-style for modals, contextual menus) that seeks consistency across platforms and less manual work for the developer, present in wwdc2019_206, wwdc2019_808 and wwdc2019_809; and a "senses" axis (audio, haptics and motion as part of the design, not as an addition), present in wwdc2019_520, wwdc2019_223, wwdc2019_810 and also in wwdc2019_244 (motion and color accessibility).

A recurring pattern is "give away for free whatever is possible, but allow fine control": SF Symbols (wwdc2019_206) and the automatic mapping from iOS to macOS (wwdc2019_809) treat system automation as the default and customization as a deliberate exception, the same reasoning appears in wwdc2019_808 when it advises against recreating controls that UIKit already offers.

There is a repeated emphasis on "less is more" as a design principle, not just an aesthetic one: reducing interaction steps to lower cognitive load on tvOS (wwdc2019_211), not overloading with audio and haptics beyond what is necessary (wwdc2019_810, wwdc2019_223, wwdc2019_520), and not overloading context menus or the Top Shelf with too many items (wwdc2019_809, wwdc2019_211).

Accessibility appears treated as a first-class requirement, not a complementary one, both explicitly (wwdc2019_244, on Dynamic Type, Reduce Motion and Differentiate Without Color) and embedded in other sessions (wwdc2019_808 cites full support for Bold Text and Increase Contrast as a design goal of the new color system).

Content duplication note: wwdc2019_223, wwdc2019_520 and wwdc2019_810 share most of their text with each other (223 is essentially the merge of 520 with 810), which suggests that the same pair of sessions (design and development of Core Haptics) was cataloged under more than one id in this corpus.
