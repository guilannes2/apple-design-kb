## Iterative UI Design (id: wwdc2016_805, 41.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 31 of 31 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2016/805/.

Presented by Ryan Olshavsky and Jonathan Cho, designers on the iWork team (Numbers, Pages, Keynote).

**Central thesis.** Good design is not easy or immediate, but there are concrete methods, defining the app, sketching from what is already known, and generating and critiquing several alternatives, that make it more likely to arrive at the right design.

**The design process Apple describes.** In the talk, the presenters describe a process in three major stages, illustrated with a fictional food delivery app for the Apple cafeteria ("Caffé Macs"):

1. Define the app. First, list every imaginable feature without judging anything (there is no good or bad idea at this stage). Then, separate the audience: identify who the user is by asking (mentally, as in an interview) what that person prefers, values and does, and turn those answers into customer goals. In parallel, define app goals, that is, how the app should look and feel, leaving out purely business goals (profit, ROI), because they do not guide design decisions. Finally, cross the feature list with the customer goals and the app goals to decide what goes in and what is left for later.
2. Design from what is already known. Instead of waiting to have the whole app mapped out, start with the screen that will certainly be needed (in the example, the menu screen), using a screenshot of an existing app as a reference for layout, color and typography.
3. Generate alternatives and then critique them. After having a first "good enough" version, repeatedly ask "what could we do differently?": vary row size, information density, white space, typography, image proportion, containers (cards), use of space (edge-to-edge), extremes (a photo filling the whole screen) and navigation schemes (swipe between items). Only after having several alternatives (eleven, in the example) does the team take a third-party stance and critique each option, going from obvious eliminations to a final comparison between two options.
4. Repeat the same cycle (start from what is known, iterate, critique) to design the usage flow (workflow) of the chosen feature, not just the static screen.

**Principles stated and the why of each.**
- "You are not the user": designing only for your own preferences introduces biases that may not serve anyone else.
- The audience cannot be "everyone": that is too vague to produce useful conclusions about what to prioritize.
- Customer goals and app goals guide the design; business goals (profit, cost) do not, because they describe what the company wants after launch, not the experience itself.
- Never judge ideas during the generation phase: judging too early kills alternatives that could reveal something better.
- Do not settle for the first idea: "you can't know if your design is right if you try only one idea".
- A shorter workflow does not need to cut functionality: you can keep everything by moving what is optional to a secondary element (in the example, a tray), instead of forcing everyone to go through it.

**Concrete interface-building techniques cited, with exact numbers when spoken.**
- Reference screen created at 375 by 667 points, a resolution cited as that of the iPhone 6.
- Separator lines 1 point thick.
- Use of the system color picker with the eyedropper tool to capture the exact color of a reference element on the screen.
- System font identified as San Francisco, with visual adjustment of weight (perceived as semi-bold) and size by direct comparison with the reference screenshot, without measuring in design software.
- Use of an overlaid shape as a mask to crop an image to exactly the desired size.
- Keyboard shortcut Command, Control, Shift, 4 to copy a crop of any part of the screen directly to the clipboard.
- Multiple selection of repeated rows or photos, with automatic uniform alignment and spacing in Keynote.
- Explicit concern for readability for people with visual impairment or color blindness when testing a large typography variant overlaid on photos.
- In the purchase flow, example values cited in the prototype: 10 dollars (initial order, no confirmation) and 11.68 dollars (order summary in the cart model).

**Examples cited and what each one teaches.**
- The Messages app was used as a reference for typography and header for the first version of the menu screen, showing that reusing visual conventions from existing apps speeds up the design without sacrificing familiarity.
- Eleven layout variations of the menu screen (labeled A to L), all designed in Keynote, illustrate the range of alternatives worth generating before choosing: changes in density, typography, photo proportion, absence of photo, grid instead of list, cards, edge-to-edge and swipe navigation between dishes.
- In the two presenters' live critique, the winning design (option "K") won by balancing photo and descriptive text, while more extreme options (no photo, too much photo, cut-off text, pagination of 12 to 15 items) were discarded for compromising the menu's function.
- The redesign of the order workflow, from an abrupt "Order" button, to a shopping cart model with summary and confirmation, to, finally, direct purchase from the menu screen itself with a tray, teaches that reducing the number of perceived steps (without removing functionality) improves the feeling of fluidity.

**Short quotes.**
"good design is not easy."
"you can't know if your design is right if you try only one idea."

## Designing for Apple Watch (id: wwdc2015_802, 54.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 40 of 40 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2015/802/.

Presented by Mike Stern, User Experience Evangelist at Apple.

**Central thesis.** Designing for Apple Watch requires a different mindset than the one used for iOS or Mac, organized around three foundational themes, personal communication, holistic design and light interaction, plus the role of beauty and charm.

**The design process Apple describes.** In the talk, the presenter does not describe a single step-by-step process, but guides toward deciding, for each type of app communication (notifications, glances, screens), questions such as: is this information relevant now, given the person's location and time context? Is this preference explicit (the person set it) or can it be inferred implicitly from behavior (e.g. reordering by frequency of use)? Is this action critical to the flow (should it stay inline) or is it contextual/a display preference (can it go into the Force Touch menu)? Does this interaction fit within a window of a few seconds?

**Principles stated and the why of each.**
- Personal communication: since notifications physically tap the person's wrist, they are much more noticeable than on iPhone, so excessive frequency generates irritation and leads the person to turn off the app's notifications.
- Relevance by context: the two contextual factors cited for deciding when to notify are location and time.
- Explicit preferences (settings) are direct, but rarely explored by people; implicit preferences (learned from observed behavior) complement this without requiring manual configuration.
- Holistic design: the bezel color was chosen to match the display, so that a black background in the app visually blends into the watch and allows content and controls to go all the way to the edge.
- The Force Touch menu is for contextual actions (related to the screen or to the app as a whole) and for display mode preferences, but it should not contain "critical path" actions (necessary for the person to move forward), which need to stay visible on the main screen.
- The Digital Crown allows smooth scrolling, so it is not necessary (or desirable) to show everything at once on the screen; Pickers get an outline when there is more than one on the screen at the same time, to indicate which one responds to the crown's rotation.
- The Taptic Engine should be used with restraint because haptic patterns cannot play simultaneously (the system discards the ones that do not fit), and because the meaning of the patterns only holds if all apps use them consistently.
- Light interaction: since interaction time on Watch is measured in seconds, not minutes or hours, the app needs to focus on the essential and, at times, deliberately cut functionality.

**Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when spoken.**
- Internal interaction time target cited as about five seconds or less.
- Glances are assembled by combining a top template, chosen from 12 available, and a bottom template, chosen from 24 available.
- Nine types of haptic feedback in total; the ones explicitly named in the talk were: notification, direction up, direction down, success, failure, start (for activity), stop (for activity) and a more discreet click haptic for dial increments.
- In the Fitness app, the workout session begins with a three-second countdown before starting the recording.
- For the New York Times app, the text cites that reading a full story would take "way more than two to five seconds" (more than two to five seconds), hence the short synopsis format.
- In watchOS 2, animatable properties at runtime cited include height, width, insets, alignment, background color, tint color and opacity.

**Examples cited and what each one teaches.**
- Invoice2Go triggers a clock-in reminder notification based on location (arrival/departure from the workplace), showing a relevant notification anchored in place context.
- American Airlines notifies the person about the time to leave for the airport, an example of relevance anchored in time context.
- Fitness reorders the list of workout types by frequency of use and remembers the last goal chosen (e.g. "no goal"), without requiring explicit configuration, an example of learned implicit preference.
- Stop watch, Weather, Calendar and Messages use the Force Touch menu to free up main screen space for content, instead of cluttering it with controls.
- Activity puts the action of changing the daily move goal in the menu, available on any screen of the app, avoiding repeating this control on every page and avoiding the confusion of seeming specific to one page.
- Messages changes the menu content according to context (conversation list versus open conversation), showing that the menu should reflect the current screen.
- Fitness shows the decision to save or discard a workout inline (not in the menu), since it is a mandatory action to move forward.
- The phone app shows the voicemail playback button inline, because the chance of the person wanting to tap it is close to 100%.
- Stocks uses the Digital Crown to scroll the detail screen instead of squeezing everything in, keeping the main values large and legible.
- ReSound, a Bluetooth hearing aid app, uses animation instead of simple sliders to express each adjustment (noise reduction, speech attenuation, wind cancellation).
- Pace Maker, an Apple Design Award winner, animates buttons by changing size and shape in sync with the music when applying effects, instead of just highlighting or outlining the active button.
- Toby, a virtual "pet", uses Force Touch to call the character back to the watch ("Here, Toby"), as an example of personality and charm even on a small screen.

**Short quotes.**
"Apple Watch is not a miniature iPhone."
"Sessions are about convenience."

## Apple Watch Design Tips and Tricks (id: wwdc2015_805, 51.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 37 of 37 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2015/805/.

Presented by Mike Stern and Rachel Roth, User Experience Evangelists at Apple. A direct continuation of the previous session (wwdc2015_802): it quickly reaffirms the three themes (personal communication, holistic design, light interaction) and then moves to a list of ten common pitfalls observed in real Apple Watch apps.

**Central thesis.** There is a recurring set of ten common errors in Apple Watch app design, and each one has a practical, specific fix.

**The design process Apple describes.** The session is structured as a numbered list of pitfalls, each followed by the diagnosis of the problem and the recommended fix:

1. Slow apps: load progressively (show what is already ready and complete the rest later, without blocking navigation), keep placeholders in place of content that has not arrived yet (so the page height does not change suddenly) and order the content so that what takes longer to load sits further down the page, outside the initial view. Also optimize graphics through compression.
2. Apps that are too complex: remember that "Apple Watch is not a miniature iPhone" and include only the subset of functionality that makes sense for quick reference, or extend the iPhone experience in a new way.
3. Tedious navigation: choose between the page model (a "flat" collection of equivalent items, few screens) or the hierarchical model (larger or more complex structures), without mixing the two in the same app; avoid overusing modal sheets, which do not show the time and are meant to signal a disruptive exit from the main flow; keep the hierarchy to at most two levels (three at the limit).
4. Confusing labels: the text at the top of the screen is the title of the current screen, never a "back" button, because there is no room for both at the same time and knowing where you are matters more than seeing the name of the previous screen; in modals, the close button needs to look clickable (not a title).
5. Using the Force Touch menu as primary navigation: since the menu does not show visual structure cues (hierarchy arrow, pagination dots), using it to navigate leaves the person not knowing where they are or that navigation is even possible.
6. Buttons that do not look like buttons: title color and chevrons (iOS conventions) do not work well on the Watch because of the reduced space and because title color is already used to reinforce branding; the fix is to wrap tappable elements in rounded rectangles or circles, and never use these shapes on content that is not clickable.
7. Inadequate touch targets: since the person may be in motion, touch targets need to be generous.
8. Legibility: use the system font (SF Compact) and the system text styles to take advantage of Dynamic Type, and always test on the physical watch, not just on the computer's large screen.
9. Unnecessary backgrounds and padding: the watch's own black bezel already works as visual "padding", so vibrant background colors and extra spacing make the screen look even smaller.
10. Icons that are hard to recognize: since Watch icons are circular, small and without a text label, it is necessary to simplify as much as possible while keeping the original color scheme, creating a complementary concept when the iPhone icon does not fit well in the circle.

**Principles stated and the why of each.** The principles coincide with the ten items above; the common thread stated by the presenters is that, on screens this small and with interactions this brief, each element needs to communicate its function (tappable or not, title or button, hierarchical or page navigation) without relying on iOS conventions that do not fit the available space or time.

**Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when stated.**
- Image compression: a photo at the highest JPEG quality setting took up about 125 K; at the highest compression setting (lowest quality), it dropped to about 25 K, around 20% of the size of the maximum-quality image, with no perceptible difference in quality in the context of use.
- PNGs: the standard 24-bit palette (millions of colors) can be swapped for an 8-bit palette (up to 256 colors); from around 200 colors or fewer, perceptible dithering/posterization already appears; the swap produced a file size reduction described as almost ten times smaller.
- Circular controls: at least 80 by 80 pixels on the 42 millimeter watch, or 75 by 75 pixels on the 38 millimeter one.
- Rectangular buttons: never shorter than 53 pixels in height on the 42 millimeter, or 50 pixels on the 38 millimeter; general practice across the system tends to use 80 pixels in height or more.
- Side-by-side buttons: up to two with short labels work well; three with text is not recommended; three with only numbers, single characters or icons still works, but they end up close together; never more than three side by side.
- Typography: five system text styles named specifically, Headline, Body, Caption 1, Caption 2 and Footnote, with automatic adjustment of size, leading (vertical space between lines) and tracking (space between letters) via Dynamic Type.

**Examples cited and what each one teaches.**
- A hypothetical Apple Retail stores app illustrates progressive loading with a photo placeholder, so as not to make the person wait for the photo to see the rest of the content.
- Calendar on the Watch deliberately shows a small subset of the iPhone Calendar's functions (no search, no creating events, no managing calendars), reinforcing the principle of focusing on the essential.
- Camera on the Watch becomes a remote viewfinder for the iPhone's shutter, an example of extending functionality instead of porting everything.
- Weather (page model) and Stocks (hierarchical model) illustrate the two possible structural models and why each one fits the type of content they show.
- Maps on iOS illustrates how color and position on the screen signal interactivity in that system, a contrast used to explain why this convention does not carry over well to the Watch.
- Yelp (red) and Foursquare (pink) show how page title color reinforces app identity even on screens with similar content; the same reasoning is repeated with Timers and Runtastic Six Pack.
- Chipotle uses one large button to "order a burrito", the app's primary action, going beyond the recommended minimum size.
- Hailo, Citymapper and Rules! illustrate ways to maintain brand personality without relying on a colored background: accent color, a loading illustration with its own sense of humor, and button texture inherited from the iPhone app.
- Citymapper, Nike+ Running and Procreate reused the iPhone icon almost unchanged on the Watch, because they were already simple enough shapes for the circular format.
- Yelp removed the text from its own icon and relied on color and shape to be recognizable.
- The Iconfactory team redesigned the Twitterific bird, turning it from profile to facing forward and simplifying the beak and eyes, to work better in the Watch's small circle.
- Fanatee (the game Letter Zap) removed the text from the icon, but kept the green and gold colors, preserving brand recognition.
- Apple's own Camera icon and the Sky Guide icon (an astronomy app) exemplify "complementary" icons: they are not copies of the iPhone icon, but they keep enough color and style to look like "siblings".

**Short quotes.**
"Bigger is better when it comes to everything on the Watch."
"The bezel is the illusion of padding."

## Ingredients of Great Games (id: wwdc2014_602, 56.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 34 of 34 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2014/602/.

Introduction by Geoff (not identified by last name in the transcript) and main presentation by Allan Schaffer, Game Technologies Evangelist at Apple.

**Central thesis.** Beyond technology (OpenGL ES, Metal, Game Center, SpriteKit, SceneKit), Apple Design Award winning games share ten recurring design and business ingredients, from onboarding to the core loop, to ongoing engagement, and an eleventh bonus ingredient: keep innovating.

**The design process Apple describes.** The session presents a numbered list of ten items (plus one bonus), each with a diagnosis of a common problem and a practical recommendation:

1. Remove friction from the first contact: load only what is necessary to reach the first interactive screen, using techniques such as Grand Central Dispatch to take work off the main thread and Time Profiler to investigate load time; defer content downloads so as not to block the player; defer or eliminate registration (unless the game depends on a server, in which case the Vendor ID provided by Apple can be used as a unique identifier); do not force configuration decisions that the player does not yet have enough information to make; do not ask for a rating before the person has even played.
2. Be a good teacher: bring the player straight into the game, teach the primary interaction first, introduce new interactions one at a time, give hints in context, and also teach the mechanics that support the core loop (not just the controls).
3. Tune the core loop: the core loop is described as the player's action, the reward received when pursuing a goal, and the resulting expansion or growth that motivates pursuing more reward; the loop needs to be easy to express in words, each phase needs to feed the next, and each iteration needs to be meaningful; some of the best games overlap more than one core loop at the same time.
4. Design for touch, do not port controls from another platform: instead of overlaying virtual buttons or D-pads (indirect controls, which force the player to look at their own finger), redesign the core loop around direct manipulation (tapping, dragging, swiping, drawing paths), always reinforced by instant visual, audio, and tactile feedback.
5. Foster engagement: understand the target audience and treat design as fractal, meaning the same patterns of coherence need to repeat from the core loop to progression and narrative; overlay layers of progression with multiple paths and goals; in free-to-play games, deliver value to both paying and non-paying players, without forcing monetization; encourage replay, linking "try again" to the core loop, "see what comes next" to progression, and "see how it ends" to narrative; plan new content (seasonal, regional, ongoing) before launch, because the most engaged players consume content fast.
6. Use background transfers via NSURLSession (introduced in iOS 7): put only the content of the first hours of gameplay in the initial bundle, at full quality, and download the rest later, in the background, without sacrificing image quality or cutting localizations to fit the cellular download limit; the transfer runs outside the app's process and continues even if the app is terminated; follow the iOS Data Storage Guidelines; this technique applies to assets, never to code changes, which require a new submission to App Review.
7. Optimize graphics performance: the most common problematic pattern is making many draw calls with few triangles each (the "bind, draw, bind, draw" cycle), especially in 2D and isometric games; use Xcode's tools (GPU performance graphs, GPU frame debugger, OpenGL ES analyzer instrument) to locate the real bottleneck before optimizing blindly; SpriteKit and SceneKit automatically absorb much of this optimization; Metal is for those who need many more draw calls and direct control of the frame.
8. Make a good gameplay video: starting with iOS 8 and OS X Yosemite, it is possible to attach to the App Store an "app preview" of up to 30 seconds, in H.264/MPEG4 format, captured directly from an iOS device connected to a Mac (which sees the iPhone as a video camera); the video must show real gameplay, not function as an advertisement, and it is recommended to have one version for iPhone and another for iPad.
9. Localize: first the presence on the App Store (name, description, keywords, screenshots), then the content of the game itself, separating text from art so as not to have to duplicate large images per language; consider "culturalized" content by region when it makes sense.
10. Aim for the state of the art: take advantage of the capabilities of the latest hardware and support the most recent versions of iOS, with the recommendation to support the current version (N) and the previous one (N minus 1); it is possible to keep the base SDK updated and still support an older deployment target, handling capability differences at runtime.
11. Bonus ingredient, "go to 11": keep innovating and keeping up with the platform's new capabilities, instead of considering the work finished after the ten items.

**Principles stated and the why of each.** They coincide with the list above; the common thread is that successful games treat each of these fronts, first contact, teaching, core loop, controls, engagement, technical distribution, performance, store presentation, global reach, and continuous updating, with the same intention and care they devote to the choice of graphics technology.

**Concrete techniques and exact numbers cited.**
- 75 billion app downloads cited as general platform context (not specific to games).
- 130 million monthly active users on Game Center, in the month prior to the talk.
- App preview of up to 30 seconds, in H.264/MPEG4.
- App Store available in 155 countries and 40 languages.
- Recommended language list: EFIGS (English, French, Italian, German, Spanish), Japanese, Korean, Traditional and Simplified Chinese, Brazilian Portuguese, Russian, Turkish, and Arabic.
- Recommendation to simultaneously support two versions of iOS, the most recent (N) and the previous one (N minus 1); at the time of the talk, this meant targeting iOS 7 and 8.
- In the graphics optimization example shown in Xcode's tools, 59% of the cost of the most expensive shader in the frame was concentrated in shadow projection.

**Examples cited and what each one teaches.**
- "Paddle Mania 3000", a fictional game used as a counterexample, stacks up all the friction mistakes from the first item on the list: monolithic loading, blocking download, registration screen, paddle configuration without enough context, and a request for a rating before even playing.
- Threes, Leo's Fortune, DEVICE 6, Blek, and Monument Valley, Apple Design Award winners, are cited as the set of games from which the ten ingredients were extracted.
- Cut the Rope is the onboarding case study: it teaches the goal first, then the rope-cutting interaction, then the bubble-popping one, and makes explicit the possibility of trying again because getting it right through trial and error is central to the game's core loop.
- Ridiculous Fishing, by Vlambeer, illustrates the core loop with three overlapping mechanics (lowering the line while avoiding fish, reeling it up while collecting fish, throwing the fish into the air to score), whose points buy better bait and longer line, closing the loop.
- Monument Valley, Blek, Threes, Infinity Blade, Cut the Rope, and Where's My Water are cited in sequence as examples of design for direct touch (tapping, twisting, dragging, swiping, drawing a path), each reinforced by instant visual and audio feedback, and, in the case of Where's My Water, also described as tactile due to the response to the finger's movement.

**Short quotes.**
"technology alone is not enough."
"Go the extra mile."

## Prototyping: Fake It Till You Make It (id: wwdc2014_223, 57.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 39 of 39 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2014/223/.

Note on duration: the durations of the card titles were corrected on 15/09/2026 based on the measurement of the video file, which matches the Apple server; the earlier indication of 0.0 min or of missing duration came from an error in the collection, not from the site.

Presented by Jeff Bernstein, with Linda Dong, Julian Missig and Mark Hauenstein, members of an internal Apple prototyping team that works across all of the company's products.

**Central thesis.** Fast and deliberately "fake" prototyping, evolving from static drawings to animation and then to real interaction, serves to test ideas and to generate new ideas before spending time and money building the real product, following the cycle "make fake apps, show people, learn from their feedback".

**The design process Apple describes.** The session demonstrates three stages of growing fidelity, all applied to the same fictional app, "Toast Modern" (discovery of artisanal toast in San Francisco):

1. Prototype with images (Keynote). Before drawing, answer three questions: what needs to become more real (the one element actually being tested), what can stay fake (practically everything else) and where people will use this (test on the physical device, in the real context of use). The process always starts with a large quantity of hand sketches, without holding on to a single idea; then the options that communicate the information well, are not overly complex, and represent directions distinct from each other are chosen (in the example, four: list, grid, map, gallery). Each sketch is then recreated on a real screen using a screenshot of an existing app as a template: block out areas with shapes, capture exact colors with the color picker's eyedropper, mask images into shapes (e.g. circle), use special characters as temporary icons, and always check at the zoom level matching the device's real physical size.
2. Prototype with animation (Keynote). After showing the static screens to real people and collecting feedback, use Keynote transitions and "builds" to simulate the relationship between two screens over time (for example, between a list and a map). The choice of transition communicates something about the relationship between the screens: "move in from bottom" suggests a temporary modal overlay; "move out horizontally" suggests navigating back; a "flip" suggests there is a setting behind the screen. The Magic Move transition interpolates position, size and opacity of elements that Keynote identifies as the same across the two slides, creating an almost automatic animation when the same images repeat between the screens; to avoid the "fade to gray" effect in the middle of the transition, the elements that need to stay visible must exist in both slides. Test the animation in both directions (there and back) by duplicating the first slide as a third slide, with no extra work.
3. Prototype with interaction (Core Animation and Objective-C). Three steps: put the image on the device, break the image into separate parts (to apply interaction to specific areas), and move the images when touched. The dragging behavior is implemented by capturing the touch position on every move event, calculating the difference (delta) between the previous position and the current one, and adding that difference to the position of the corresponding image layer, creating the sensation of dragging. The prototype code does not need to be good or reusable, just quick to put together, because its function is to support a decision, not to become a final product.

After each stage, show the prototype to people from the app's real target audience (without defending or debating the result) and ask: does the person know how to do this specific thing? Is it easy? How can we improve this, openly? Then, the team asks itself: what worked, what did not work, what people did not understand or where they got stuck, and what new ideas this suggests, before restarting the cycle.

**Principles stated and the why of each.**
- Prototyping serves two reasons: testing ideas, to save time and money by building the right thing, and generating new ideas, to improve the final experience.
- The more prototypes between the initial idea and the final app, the better the result tends to be.
- The path goes from fake to real gradually, adding elements of reality little by little, instead of trying to get everything right at once.
- You do not need to know how to program in order to prototype: designers and managers should also draw and animate before asking an engineer for code.
- Prototype code is disposable by definition: the stated intention is to "hack it together", not to produce something reusable.

**Concrete interface-building techniques cited.** The session did not bring pixel, point or interaction-time measurements as UI specification (unlike the Apple Watch videos in this same group); the only numbers cited are illustrative of the prototyping exercise itself: a screen took about 10 minutes to be put together in Keynote from the reference screenshot, and the value of 4 dollars appears as a hypothetical joke example ("not everyone wants to pay 4 dollars or more for a slice of bread").

**Examples cited and what each one teaches.**
- "Toast Modern", the fictional artisanal toast app, structures the entire session and serves to show the three fidelity phases (image, animation, interaction) applied to the same product problem.
- The iPhone Music app was used as a layout reference to build the first version of the toast list screen, showing how to make use of the structure of an existing app to speed up the design.
- The OS X Maps app was captured full screen and cropped in Keynote to serve as a "fake image" of a map, without worrying about real latitude and longitude coordinates.
- The four concepts tested on paper and then in Keynote (list, grid, map, gallery) were narrowed down, after testing with real people, to two favorites (list and map), with the feedback indicating that the star icons made it clear how to find better-rated toast, but that nearby toast was hard to understand without a map.
- The prototype that combined list and map on the same screen (splitting the space in half) brought a problem that was only noticed in testing with people: it was not clear whether the toast shown on the map were the same ones in the list, flagged as something to resolve before investing in building the real version.

**Short quotes.**
"Make fake apps, show people and learn from their feedback."
"just hack it together as quick as you can."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- The cycle "build fast and cheap, show it to real people, ask what worked and what did not work, iterate" appears, in nearly identical words, both in the screen design of a food app (wwdc2016_805) and in the low-fidelity prototyping of a restaurant discovery app (wwdc2014_223), suggesting that this cycle is taught as Apple's general method for arriving at a correct design, regardless of platform or fidelity stage. IDs: wwdc2016_805, wwdc2014_223.
- Keynote repeatedly appears as a real design tool inside Apple, not just for presentation: used to draw static UI pixel by pixel (wwdc2016_805) and to prototype transition animation between screens, including via Magic Move (wwdc2014_223), which indicates that the barrier to entry for "doing design" at Apple is deliberately low, requiring no specialized tools or code.
- The motto "Apple Watch is not a miniature iPhone", repeated almost word for word in two different sessions about the same product (wwdc2015_802, wwdc2015_805), and the parallel warning against porting indirect controls from another platform to touch games (wwdc2014_602), show a broader principle: each platform must be designed from its own physical and interaction-time constraints, not by directly transposing conventions from another platform.
- The rules for minimum hit target size, the use of the system font and typographic styles (San Francisco in wwdc2016_805, SF Compact and Dynamic Type in wwdc2015_805) and the exploration of physical hardware (bezel, Digital Crown, Taptic Engine in wwdc2015_802) show a consistent concern with anchoring the interface in the real limitations of the person's body and attention (vision, movement, available time), and not just in abstract visual rules.
- In three distinct sessions from different years (2014, 2015, 2016), Apple independently recommends testing design decisions with real people outside the controlled environment of the design software or the simulator, whether on the wrist in motion (wwdc2015_805), or in the context of app use (wwdc2014_223), or through structured internal critique with explicit criteria (wwdc2016_805), reinforcing that subjective evaluation "by eye" inside the design tool does not replace testing in a real context.
