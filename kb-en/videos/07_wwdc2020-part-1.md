## Evaluate and optimize voice interaction for your app (id: wwdc2020_10071, 10.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10071/.

Central thesis: Siri is a set of modalities (voice, touch, automation) built on intents, and voice only adds value when it simplifies multi-step interactions, not when it becomes a spoken form.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Jess, "conversational interaction designer" on the Siri team) starts from the question "what is sending a message?" to explain that an intent is born from breaking down an everyday task into the information needed (to whom, what to say). For tasks covered by System Intents, the dialogue flow already comes ready-made from Apple; for tasks outside that scope (e.g. ordering coffee), the developer uses Custom Intents and has to design the logic alone, thinking about: what steps the person needs to take, what information they need to provide, in what order to ask, what words to use.

Principles stated:
- Voice should not be used to fill out forms field by field; it should simplify multi-step tasks, especially frequent ones (example: asking to play "the most recent album by a given artist" instead of opening the app, searching and playing manually).
- Voice speeds up experienced users past steps they no longer need to see (example from the fictional app Soup Chef: someone who already knows what they want skips the menu).
- Voice is good for giving quick access to functions with low visibility in the UI (e.g. Settings) and for multitasking contexts (driving, with AirPods).
- Siri speaks less as the person becomes familiar with a function: the first time it gives more detail, then it shortens. The same applies to attribution of the information's source: if the shortcut is used a lot, the attribution disappears, but it is up to the developer to keep the dialogue consistent even so.
- Questions are conversational markers: use them when the person needs to provide information.
- Avoid jargon, because whoever uses the app may not be a specialist.
- Use good practices from spoken English, not written, to sound conversational.
- Keep the dialogue short, because it is hard to remember everything you hear.

Concrete techniques: two modes of communication for Siri. Silent mode (default on iOS 14 when the ring switch is muted): Siri does not speak, only the on-screen UI and printed text communicate. Voice mode: Siri speaks all the necessary information even with a screen available. System Intents already handle both modes automatically; Custom Intents require the developer to indicate which dialogue and UI to use in each mode, and the system chooses the right piece at the right moment. There are no pixel numbers, times or proportions cited in the text.

Examples cited: sending a message ("Play the latest album by DaBaby"), Soup Chef (fictional soup-ordering app), Settings as a power-user function, voice message confirmation scene ("Your message to Eden says, 'Want to get coffee later?' Ready to send it?").

Short quotes:
"Siri makes it easy for people to accomplish their everyday tasks quickly using Voice, Touch or Automation."
"Voice shouldn't just be about filling out forms."

## Design great App Clips (id: wwdc2020_10172, 21.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 20 of 20 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10172/.

Central thesis: App Clips are light, instant versions of apps, found in context (not in the App Store) and designed for a single purpose, with no persistent state on the device.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Grant Paul and Heena Ko) split the topic into three questions. What App Clips are (lightweight native experiences, with no installation, made for people who do not yet know the app). How people enter them (codes and physical tags, Maps, Smart App Banner in Safari, Messages, Siri Suggestions), never through search in the App Store. And what to put inside an App Clip. To turn an existing app into an App Clip, the method described is: decide the single purpose (a single task, such as renting a scooter or buying ice cream); define the absolute minimum flow to complete that task (considering Apple Pay and Sign in with Apple to speed it up); and, lastly, offer the full app, but only after the App Clip has already proven its value.

Principles stated:
- App Clips do not disappear on their own through accumulation: when dismissed, they need no management or manual deletion, because the system takes care of it.
- Each physical entry point (table, branch) should have a different tag, so the App Clip already knows the location and context without asking, avoiding error and saving a step.
- App Clips are not a "Trojan horse" for the app: they have to have their own value, even if the person never downloads the full app.
- Radical simplicity: no introductions, tutorials, login screens, global navigation, tabs or settings inside the App Clip.
- Promote the full app only after the main task has been completed, once the value has already been demonstrated.

Concrete building techniques: the App Clip Card is the equivalent of the App Store product page and has a title (usually the brand name, or the name of a specific function), a subtitle (explains what the App Clip does, since it may be the person's first contact with the brand), a header image (a real photo or brand graphic, never a screenshot or too much text) and an action button with three available options: Open, View and Play. Notifications: by default an App Clip can send notifications for up to eight hours after first use; for experiences that last more than a day (example cited: car rental), it is possible to request explicit authorization for one more week. Using Apple Pay for transactions is recommended. An App Clip allows up to three physical tags per App Clip Code (NFC in the center and a visual code around it, useful when you cannot get close, such as on a billboard).

Examples cited: fictional ice cream app (shows how an App Clip can represent a small business inside a larger delivery app, with the business's own brand); Etsy (Smart App Banner that leads straight to the product seen on the page); fictional coffee shop (minimal flow: browse the menu, order, pay with Apple Pay, then offer the app); fictional parking app (pay for a spot, expiration notification, remote extension); fictional accessories store (simple purchase plus Sign in with Apple after purchase); fictional restaurant with a code per table; fictional AR experience to identify mountain peaks; Files in iOS 13 had actions scattered across the screen, which takes focus away from the content (example used in another video in the group, but cited here as the target app for UI simplification).

Short quotes:
"App Clips are not a Trojan horse for your app."
"Great App Clips are simple."

## Design with iOS pickers, menus and actions (id: wwdc2020_10205, 14.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 15 of 15 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10205/.

Central thesis: the three new iOS 14 components (menus, date/time picker, color picker) solve the limitations of the old action sheets and popovers by staying closer to the touch point, being visually lighter and more aligned between iPad and Mac.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Cas Lemmens) contrasts the old behavior (action sheet on iPhone, popover on iPad) with the new one, explaining the problems that motivated the change: a darkened background in heavy transitions, large list items for short text, limited actions (no selection), the need to move the finger to the other side of the screen.

Principles stated:
- The menu appears near where the person tapped, reducing finger movement.
- Tapping outside the menu always cancels, so an explicit "cancel" item is not needed.
- Destructive actions never go in quick menus: they require confirmation in a location different from the original action, to generate enough friction and avoid accidental deletion (example: deleting a list in Reminders, canceling a draft in Mail).
- Do not hide primary actions behind a "more" button: only secondary actions should be collapsed.
- All the work on menus was based on how they already worked on Mac, which makes it easier to port apps between iPad and Mac.

Concrete techniques: four defined uses for menus. Disambiguation (a clear action triggers a more specific question, like the add button in Photos or Notes). Navigation (holding the "back" button in Safari shows the session's history). Selection (selected items get a checkmark, like the sort button in Podcasts). And secondary actions via a "more" button (example: Files on iOS 14, which became cleaner compared to iOS 13, where actions were scattered). The new date and time picker replaces the old wheels with a navigable calendar (swipe to change month, tap the month to jump directly) and a typeable field for the time; there is also compact mode, which shows a button with the date in an accent color over a light platform and opens the picker in a modal when tapped (example: adding a birthday in Contacts). The new color picker offers four ways to choose a color: grid, spectrum, RGB value, or picking a color from the screen via an eyedropper (with a draggable magnifier); the chosen color appears in the bottom left corner and can be saved to a palette shared across apps. All three components follow accessibility settings such as VoiceOver, Increase Contrast, Reduce Motion and Larger Text.

Examples cited: Music (the "Sort" button), Photos (the add button), Notes (adding an image), video editing in Photos (the "Done" button), Safari (the back button with a list of visited sites, tab management), Podcasts (sorting with checkmarks), Files (before and after the visual cleanup), Messages (composition menu), Reminders (list deletion with confirmation), Mail (canceling a draft with confirmation).

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote was marked as relevant enough for reproduction; the text is mostly descriptive and technical.

## Design for Game Center (id: wwdc2020_10145, 18.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 15 of 15 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10145/.

Central thesis: the new Game Center experience revolves around a consistent access point (the player's avatar) and a central dashboard that organizes profile, achievements, leaderboards and multiplayer in a visually unified way across platforms.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Joe and Michael, designers on the Game Center team) organize the presentation by dashboard section, each one covering a part: access point, dashboard and profile, achievements, leaderboards, multiplayer and App Store integration.

Principles stated:
- The access point should be a clear and recognizable way to access Game Center, preferably in the game's main menu, and only after splash screens and cinematics finish.
- Pausing the game when the dashboard appears (an explicit recommendation) avoids distraction, since the dashboard is a transparent layer over the game.
- Icons and shapes provided by Apple (for the access point, the profile link, achievements, leaderboards) should not be altered in shape, only styled in color.
- The term "Game Center" should never be localized; "profile", "achievements" and "leaderboards" have mandatory correct terms and cannot become "trophies", "rankings" etc.
- Order achievements in a way that reflects the game's progress, and be selective: it is not necessary to use all 100 allowed achievements in the first version, to leave room for future versions.
- Achievement and leaderboard images should be unique, with no embedded text, and differ from one another.

Concrete building techniques with exact numbers: on the iPhone 11 Pro, the access point in portrait games measures 62 by 335 points, with a recommended safe area of 114 points from the top or bottom covering the full width; in landscape, 62 by 280 points, with a safe area of 91 points. The dashboard art (optional, tvOS) should be TIFF or PNG, 923 by 150 pixels at 1x, 1846 by 300 pixels at 2x, at least 72 DPI, in sRGB. Achievement images should be JPEG, TIFF or PNG, 512 by 512 pixels at 1x, 1024 by 1024 pixels at 2x, 72 DPI, sRGB; the system automatically crops to a circle, so the main content should stay centered; title and description should stay under 30 characters to guarantee display on two lines without truncating, with the title in initial caps and the description in sentence case (never all uppercase). Up to 100 achievements are allowed per game. Leaderboard images follow the same specification as achievements (512x512 1x, 1024x1024 2x, 72 DPI, sRGB); on tvOS, leaderboards have a 16 by 9 ratio, with PNG at 659 by 371 pixels at 1x and 1318 by 742 pixels at 2x, supporting between one and three image layers for a parallax effect in focus; leaderboard titles should also stay under 30 characters (two lines before truncating).

Examples cited: The Coast (indie game used as a demonstration throughout the whole video, including the "Passage Lighthouse" achievement earned at level 5).

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted; the content is mostly technical specification and practical recommendation.

## What's new in watchOS design (id: wwdc2020_10171, 16.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10171/.

Central thesis: in watchOS 7, Apple eliminated gesture-based contextual menus (long press) in favor of buttons and navigation patterns that are visible, debatable and predictable, without losing functionality.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Jennifer Patton) explains that the team did a complete audit of all gesture-based contextual menus in the operating system and in the native apps, guided by three design goals defined in the process: interactions should be discoverable and predictable; relevant actions should always be visible in the app; and eliminate gesture-based contextual menus without removing functionality. From this, the team categorized the actions that needed a new place as "secondary actions" and defined, for each type of case, a substitute interface pattern.

Principles stated:
- Watch apps should be light: limit long interactions and be selective with functionality, aiming for the person to get the information at a glance.
- Functionality should be actionable at the right moment (example: filter in a list).
- An action considered critical should be discoverable, with a clear visual button.
- Always separate primary actions from secondary ones: never put a primary action inside a "more" menu.

Concrete building techniques: for long lists, use sort and filter menus nesting a picker in a list in SwiftUI (examples: Activity Sharing, Stocks with a toggle between viewing by percentage or points). Swipe actions use SwiftUI's onDelete modifier (example: remove a city in World Clock by swiping from right to left). For full screens without scrolling that need additional options, use a "More" button with the SF symbol "ellipsis" centered in a circular container (examples: Maps, with search and a toggle between public transit and standard map; Camera, with a more robust sheet of flash and Live Photo options). Technical detail of the More button in the Photos app, which uses a custom Watch Face glyph instead of the ellipsis because it has only one action: white circular background with 85% opacity and a 1 point black outer glow at 50% opacity, to guarantee legibility over light and dark backgrounds. Action buttons at the bottom of a detail screen are recommended when the action applies to the content above (example: send an email to the sender of an invite in Calendar, or delete an event of your own, with red text to indicate destructive and a confirmation alert when the information is not easily recoverable). A new component, the toolbar button, was created specifically for the compose button in Messages and Mail: it stays hidden under the navigation bar and only appears when the person scrolls the screen, available in SwiftUI, recommended only in views with scrolling and only for essential but not primary actions. For permanent destination changes, use hierarchical navigation (example: Mail opens directly into the "All Inboxes" view, but the back button leads to the top level to switch inboxes, remembering the choice on the next opening; the same pattern in the Home app to switch houses).

Examples cited: Sleep Cycle (limiting functionality), Wikiloc (filter in a list), Bear (clear visual button), Activity Sharing, Stocks, World Clock, Maps, Camera, Photos, Workout (use of More button in a carousel), Calendar, Reminders, Messages and Mail (toolbar button), Home (hierarchical navigation).

## Designed for iPad (id: wwdc2020_10206, 29.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 27 of 27 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10206/.

Central thesis: a good iPad app is not a middle ground between iPhone and Mac; it needs dedicated design decisions to take advantage of the large screen, support multiple simultaneous input types and stay always responsive.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Grant Paul and, in the second half, Vince Lane) structure the talk in four blocks: taking advantage of the large display, supporting the iPad's different inputs, the new iPadOS 14 UI components (sidebar and split view) and the use of toolbars. For each change made to Apple's own apps, they show the before (iPadOS 13 or iOS) and the after (iPadOS 14), explaining the reasoning behind the change.

Principles stated:
- Flatten navigation: avoid full screen transitions, preferring to update part of the screen at a time (example: Photos switched navigation from slide-over to sidebar).
- Show more content: a larger screen calls for more density, as long as the text stays legible (example: Files went from 12 visible icons to almost 300% more, reducing icon size without losing legibility).
- Add more context: keep people in context instead of blocking the screen with overlays (example: renaming a file inline in Files, instead of an isolated modal; the Calendars popover in Calendar moved to the same level as the events, with an instant result).
- Go deep into specific content when it makes sense: immersive screens (turning a page in Books, watching a video in Apple TV, sketching with the Pencil in Notes, editing a photo in Photos, the Now Playing screen in Music) make the device "disappear" as a piece of glass and become the content itself.
- Always start with Multi-Touch support before adding other inputs.
- Add keyboard shortcuts for all common actions: whoever doesn't use them isn't bothered, and whoever does appreciates it; shortcuts migrate automatically to Mac Catalyst.
- Combining multiple inputs at the same time is something unique to iPad (example: Command+tap to open a link in a new tab in Safari, Option+tap to download a file, Ctrl+tap for more options; Loom combines touch to select a frame and Pencil to draw).
- Constant responsiveness: the app should respond to touch even during animations (example: iPadOS 14 pull-down menus let you select an item by dragging your finger while the menu is still animating).

Concrete building techniques: sidebar is reserved for regular-width layouts; in compact-width (including iPhone) it should convert to a tab bar or standard table rows. Recommendation to keep the same top level items in both the sidebar and the tab bar. Content best suited to the sidebar tends to be long user-generated lists (albums in Photos, playlists in Music), organized below the primary navigation and nested under collapsible headers; overloading the sidebar with the whole app should be avoided. Add buttons sit at the end of each configurable section. The sidebar works both for flat navigation (items of similar weight, with a tab bar, like Photos or Music) and hierarchical navigation (like Mail or Notes), but it is not suited for navigating deep hierarchies, which should stay in the content area. The sidebar automatically becomes an overlay in portrait, in split view multitasking, and can be collapsed manually with a button at the top or brought back by swiping from the left edge. Mail's three column layout, previously exclusive to the larger iPad Pros, now reaches all iPads via the sidebar. Toolbars: by default, place buttons at the top in iPadOS (works well for toolbars of one, two or three buttons); in compact-width, use an alternative layout with the items at the bottom.

Examples cited: Photos (sidebar, immersive editing), iPad Home Screen (evolution from iOS 12 to iPadOS 14, with the addition of widgets), Files (icon density, inline renaming, sidebar with edit mode and drag-and-drop, spring-loaded folders), Calendar (Calendars popover), Books, Apple TV, Notes (Pencil), Music (full-screen Now Playing, sidebar), Safari (combination of keyboard and touch), Loom (animation app, combination of Pencil and Touch), iPadOS 14 dropdown menus (responsiveness), Home app (sidebar), Mail (three-column layout).

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted; the content is mostly description of patterns and before/after comparisons.

## SF Symbols 2 (id: wwdc2020_10207, 18.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 18 of 18 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10207/.

Central thesis: SF Symbols is a typographic icon system, not a graphic one, designed to behave like text (scale, align to the baseline, respond to Dynamic Type), now expanded with more symbols, multiple colors and native support on macOS.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Thalia and Paolo) show the usage flow in a design tool (Sketch, with the SF Symbols app to copy and paste the glyph) and in code (UIKit with UIImage systemName, and SwiftUI with Image systemName), highlighting the care to copy the symbol's name (shift-command-C) and not the glyph itself when working in Xcode.

Principles stated:
- Symbols are vertically centered on the cap height of the text next to them, but the relationship with the baseline is flexible: some symbols rise above it (e.g. infinity), others drop below it (e.g. folder), on purpose, to optically balance the shape with San Francisco.
- Symbols should not be locked into frames with a specific size; they have their own size and should be specified by scale and typographic configuration, not by fixed dimensions.
- When aligning symbols next to text, align by the baseline, never by the center.
- When renaming symbols to keep internal consistency (example: "bin.xmark" became "xmark.bin"), it is necessary to use the old name when targeting earlier versions of the OS and the new name when targeting the current version, at the risk of the symbol not appearing or the app crashing.
- Localized symbols (direction "mirrors" for right-to-left languages like Arabic and Hebrew) localize automatically, with no need to indicate the variants in the spec.

Concrete building techniques with exact numbers: all symbols come in three scales (small, medium, large), with medium as the default; small is approximately 20% smaller than medium, and large is approximately 30% larger than medium, with weight compensation to keep the stroke thickness equivalent across scales and with the text. In iOS 13, more than 1,500 symbols were launched; this year more than 750 new ones were added. The export template (version 2) has three rows (one per scale) and nine columns (one per weight, from ultralight to black), and in the new version the margins stopped being rectangles and became lines, allowing positive or negative margins (the margin can "shrink" the symbol, overlapping the outline). Colors: by default symbols use the app's tint color; multicolor symbols (new) allow predefined colors, accessible through the color preview mode in the SF Symbols app; in code, this is done by passing true (monochrome, default behavior) or false (multicolor) to setTemplate. When specifying layout, Apple recommends not defining frames, specifying only scale and point size (the same point size as the text next to it), center alignment in the symbol column and leading alignment on the labels, always aligning by the baseline.

Examples cited: Calendar (symbol with default tint color), Weather widget (multicolored symbols, more effective with multiple colors), Mail (macOS sidebar spec with symbol and text side by side), fictional music app used in the demo (symbols "play.fill" and "shuffle"), folder and badge-related symbols (vertical optical alignment).

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted; the content is mostly technical specification.

## Build for the iPadOS pointer (id: wwdc2020_10093, 22.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 18 of 18 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10093/.

Central thesis: to adopt the iPadOS pointer in an app, the recommended approach is top-down, starting with the highest-level APIs (which already guarantee behavior consistent with the system) before customizing your own interactions.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Mohammed, a UIKit engineer, and Joey, from the iOS System UI team) explain the strategy used to update iPadOS itself for the pointer and replicate that strategy in a code demonstration in a fictional app called Quilt Simulator. The process described: first, ensure that the app's "chrome" controls (top and bottom bars) have the appropriate pointer effects; then, use UIPointerInteraction for custom UI; finally, add polish (expanded padding, animations coordinated with the pointer).

Principles stated:
- A lot already works automatically with the pointer with no extra adoption, because system components (UIBarButtonItem, UISegmentedControl, UIMenuController, scroll views, UITextView) already have built-in support.
- When deciding where to add pointer support, follow the cues from the Human Interface Guidelines and from native apps.
- UIButtons placed in bars via the custom view API already receive interactions enabled by default, with the effect determined by the system according to the button's appearance, size and content.

Concrete building techniques: two types of UIPointerStyle. Content effect (the pointer turns into an app view and applies a visual treatment, like the highlight effect on bar buttons, which uses UIPointerEffect + UIPointerShape, for example a roundedRect). And shape customization, expressed via UIPointerShape and UIAxis mask, used for example in text views, where the verticalBeam shape is restricted to the vertical axis to simulate a horizontal rail over the text line. In the demo, the straight line guide button receives a pointerStyleProvider that creates an "outset" rectangle from the button's bounds, converted to the coordinate space of the proposed effect's preview, returning a style with the proposed effect but with an improved shape. The line color selection button uses the "lift" effect with a UITargetedPreview whose shadowPath matches the button's outline. For fully custom UI (the central "quilt" area), UIPointerInteraction is used directly, with an optional delegate that implements regionFor request (to return custom UIPointerRegions corresponding to guides) and styleForRegion (to return the style, for example a "crosshair" shape), including a guided mode that restricts the pointer's movement to the vertical axis (constrainedAxes = vertical) to make perfect horizontal stitches easier. ## Design for the iPadOS pointer (id: wwdc2020_10640, 41.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 35 of 35 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10640/.

Central thesis: the iPadOS pointer is not the traditional Mac pointer brought to the tablet; it is an "Adaptive Precision" system that dynamically adjusts its precision, shape and behavior to match the precision required by the interface, keeping the iPad's touch-first philosophy.

The design process Apple describes: four Apple designers (Brandon Walkin, Marcos Alonzo, CC Wan and Dylan Edwards) split the explanation into blocks: why add a pointer to the iPad (ergonomics, precision text editing), the reasoning behind Adaptive Precision, the internal workings (invisible pointer model, snapping, recentering, inertia, magnetism), the three pointer effects provided (Highlight, Lift, Hover), the pointer's appearance and material, how to design a custom pointer, and finally trackpad interactions and gestures. The team explains that it tested bringing the traditional Mac arrow pointer over, but that did not work well because the whole iPad was designed for generous touch targets, so a "high precision tool" (the arrow pointer) interacting with "low precision controls" (large buttons) generated wrong clicks.

Principles stated:
- Adaptive Precision: reduce the pointer's precision to match the precision required by the interface (example: playback buttons, where the app only cares about which of the three buttons was clicked, not the exact pixel).
- The pointer should highlight the element instead of obscuring it; that's why it moves on the z axis behind the icon, the label or the button when it snaps, letting the real color intended by the designer show through.
- When choosing a pointer effect, try Automatic Effect first, because the system decides based on rules (object type, location, size, shape) that can change in the future and remain consistent.
- Consistency within the same group of buttons is important (avoid different highlight background sizes between side-by-side buttons), but sometimes mixing effects is the right choice (example: segmented control uses Lift for the selected option instead of Highlight, because Highlight looks strange on rectangular objects).
- Never enable snapping without providing a custom pointer.
- Custom pointers should communicate intent as directly as possible, preferably be solid (not outlined or thin-stroked, which becomes hard to read because the pointer's color constantly changes), have a size visually balanced with the standard pointer, keep the anchor point centered whenever possible, apply the system's standard behaviors, and never be purely decorative.

Concrete building techniques with exact numbers: the standard pointer is a 19-point circle, sized to match a fingertip. One cited example of a custom pointer (an "X" mark for a function) is 24 points against the standard circle's 19 points, to balance visual weight. For toolbar buttons, Apple recommends a height of 37 points. Recommended padding around elements with a bezel: about 12 points; around elements without a bezel: about 24 points. The pointer has an acceleration curve identical to the Mac's (it gains extra speed the faster the finger moves on the trackpad) and uses inertia (movement continues for a bit after lifting the finger, like a scrolling "flick") and magnetism (when the finger lifts, the system calculates where the pointer would stop from inertia and scans in circles around that position, at a fixed radius, looking for the closest "snappable" target in the direction of movement). Three pointer effects: Highlight (for small controls with no background, such as bar buttons and tab bars: the pointer becomes the control's background, the elements scale and move with slight parallax, a light connected to the movement is added on top, and when clicked the three layers scale down toward the center); Lift (for medium elements with their own background, such as app icons or Control Center modules: the pointer disappears behind the element, which "lifts" with scale and shadow, and the color "bleeds" around it lighting up the background, an effect called radiosity; the intensity of the specular light varies with the object's size); Hover (for large objects that would behave badly morphing: the pointer keeps the standard shape and the object changes appearance via scale, shadow and/or tint, which are combinable). Trackpad gestures: one finger is reserved for pointing and clicking, three fingers are reserved by the system (multitasking), two fingers are available to the app (automatic scroll in scroll views); secondary click (two fingers on the trackpad, secondary mouse button, or Control+click) opens context menus instantly, with no need for a long touch.

Examples cited: playback buttons of a fictional music app (Adaptive Precision reducing precision to match button regions); text editing (I-beam that morphs into a vertical bar and engages with each line, avoiding selecting the wrong line); fictional calendar app (dragging to create an event with 15-minute rounding, adjusted with customized Adaptive Precision); Settings (pointer with inertia reaching the icon in a single movement); Books (using the pointer's hover position to control toolbar visibility); Reminders (Lift effect on finger-sized targets, creating a fun selector); Calendar (Hover effect on large events to indicate interactivity and disambiguate overlapping events); Numbers (dragging the divider between columns directly instead of selecting the header first); Maps (two-finger gestures for zoom and rotation anchored to the pointer's position; map scroll versus card scroll depending on where the pointer is); Pages (instant drag-and-drop and drag selection); Keynote (customized pointer with a pair of arrows indicating the drag direction of a selection handle); Books (bookmark icon used as an example of a poor choice of Lift effect inside a toolbar that uses Highlight).

"You'd be using a high precision tool to interact with low precision controls."
"Ideally people aren't even aware that this is happening."

## App accessibility for Switch Control (id: wwdc2020_10019, 14.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 13 of 13 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10019/.

Central thesis: Switch Control serves people with significant motor disabilities who navigate the device with one or more external switches or automatic scanning (autoscan), and an app that is already 100% accessible for VoiceOver usually already works well for Switch Control, but some cases require additional polish via API.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Sommer) first explains what Switch Control is and who uses it (people with limited use of their limbs, controlling switches mounted on wheelchairs by head taps, tongue clicks, or blowing into a straw; there is also the automatic scanning mode, "autoscanning", in which the cursor advances on its own until the person interacts). Then, she demonstrates the adaptation process using a fictional example app (an educational game called "Shape Shuffle", made for children to learn numbers, shapes and colors), identifying three accessibility problems in the app and fixing each one with a specific API.

Principles stated:
- Tap-timing is critical for people who use autoscanning, because a tap error generates several extra steps.
- Efficient grouping of navigation items is key, since poor grouping increases the wait time until reaching the desired item.
- Error tolerance is essential, because tremors or involuntary movements increase the rate of accidental taps; destructive actions (erasing all data, signing out of the account) should never be too easy to trigger.
- Avoid timeouts when Switch Control is active, since switch users generally take longer to enter information and correct errors.
- Avoid leaving private information (account numbers, medical data) exposed on screen for longer than necessary, since devices mounted on wheelchairs make it harder to keep the screen private.

Concrete building techniques (with API names): to fix poor grouping of items on a screen with a winding path (curved level of the game's phases), use accessibilityNavigationStyle set to "combined" to group items in a section, and explicitly control the order by defining the accessibilityElements array with the desired sequence. To reduce the number of interactions needed to "see" a card (the game required a tap to flip each card), implement accessibilityElementDidBecomeFocused (flips the card when it gains focus) and accessibilityElementDidLoseFocus (flips it back when it loses focus), allowing automatic flipping as the cursor passes, with no menu interaction. To reduce the need for double-tap and long-press gestures (which require navigating to the gestures submenu), use UIAccessibilityCustomAction: each action becomes an item, assigned to the accessibilityCustomActions array, appearing in Switch Control's top-level menu; an iOS 14 novelty allows assigning an image (e.g. SF Symbols) to these custom actions, and without an image the system uses the action name's first letter as the old default. To detect whether Switch Control is running, use isSwitchControlRunning or the associated notification. For static UI that actually responds to interaction (such as a UILabel that updates on tap), set accessibilityRespondsToUserInteraction to true, so Switch Control knows it should navigate to that item.

Examples cited: Ian McKay (real user cited navigating the forest and taking a photo with a mouth-operated switch); World Clock (autoscanning demonstration when adding Amsterdam); Shape Shuffle (fictional game used as a complete case study, with level 3 and the prompt "Two Blue Squares"); Reminders (swipe actions such as Delete and Flag presented as Custom Actions).

## Design great widgets (id: wwdc2020_10103, 16.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10103/.

Central thesis: a good widget is not a miniature of the app; it is a piece of personal, informational and contextual content that appears in the right place at the right time, and the design process has two clear phases: ideation (what to show) and creation (how to show).

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Mac and Christian, from the human interface design team) split the talk into two parts. Mac covers the ideation phase, with three guiding principles (personal, informational, contextual) applied to real examples of Apple's widgets, plus the impact of the new widget editing capability on the ideation process. Christian covers the creation phase: sizes and interactions, content and personality, and layout patterns.

Principles stated:
- Look for information that is personal (generates emotional connection), informational (avoids repeated actions inside the app) and contextual (uses signals from the moment to predict the person's need).
- When running out of events for the day, instead of showing a blank widget, expand the temporal scope (show what's coming tomorrow), think context beyond the present moment.
- The ability to edit and add multiple widgets eliminates the need to create complex layouts that try to combine several information entities in a single widget; instead, the person chooses how much they want to see by adding several configured widgets.
- When deciding between sizes, do not scale the small widget up to the large one; think about what makes sense for each size, and not every idea needs to support all three sizes.
- It is recommended to include at most four pieces of information in the small widget.
- The widget should work well in light and dark mode.
- Every widget needs a placeholder state (showing the base graphic elements and text blocks in place of the real information), so that the transition from placeholder to real data doesn't change layout or color.

Concrete building techniques with exact numbers: three supported sizes (small, medium, large); the small widget supports a single tap target (the "fill" style, which leads straight to the content shown); medium and large support multiple tap targets, with three tap styles: fill (single content), cell (an item that lives in its own shape, e.g. Files) and content (an item not contained within the widget). Recommended default layout margin of 16 points at the edges across all sizes; for layouts with graphic shapes like circles and platforms with an inset, use tighter margins of 11 points. Typography should use SF Pro or system variants (SF Mono, SF Pro Rounded); a custom font is allowed, but it must still feel "at home" next to other widgets. A logo should only appear if the app is an aggregator of content from different sources, always in the top right corner; avoid wordmarks, the app icon and the app name inside the widget (redundant with the system label below); avoid text that instructs or speaks directly to the person; avoid language like "last updated" or "last checked" for chronological information. Smart Stacks group several widgets in one place and switch automatically based on the person's behavior and context throughout the day.

Examples cited: Calendar (shows day of the week, date, next event, time and location; collapses less relevant information on full days; shows tomorrow when today is over; the country's holiday calendar; gift glyph for a contact's birthday), Photos widget (prioritizes the best photos, not the most recent; shows memories from this day in previous years), Weather widget (city, current temperature, high and low, forecast for the rest of the day; one-off events like strong wind, storms and minute-by-minute forecast of when rain will start or stop; allows switching city, with a practical example switching to Tokyo and then adding San Francisco in a second widget), Maps widget (shows where the car is parked when the person is not at home; contextualizes route and arrival time for scheduled events; shows ETA back home), Stocks (compact watchlist widget and a separate widget for a specific symbol at higher resolution), News (follows a specific topic), Notes (pin a favorite note or shared folder), Fitness/Screen Time (enlarged chart with more category and app detail at the large size), Files widget ("cell" tap style).

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted; the content is mostly descriptive and practical.

## Design for location privacy (id: wwdc2020_10162, 7.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 7 of 7 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10162/.

Central thesis: the introduction of approximate location in iOS 14 required the Apple Maps team to rethink an app entirely designed around precise location, and the resulting process generated three location privacy design principles applicable to any app.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Rachel, a designer on the Apple Maps team) explains that the team audited the app to identify every place that depended on precise data (arrival time and distance calculation in Favorites, search, place details), deciding, for each one, whether the feature could work with approximate data, whether it should be removed when the person shares approximate location, or whether it deserved a one-off request for precise data tied to a specific action (like asking for directions).

Principles stated, each with an explanation of why:
- Prioritize user control: give control over the data shared and respect preferences; do not require precise data to use the product; remove non-essential UI that depends on precise data when the person shares approximate location. Why: calculating arrival time and distance is not viable with approximate location, and removing them from the experience does not drastically hurt functionality.
- Build trust through transparency: use clear language to explain the use of data and its value, especially at important decision points (like the app's first launch); remind people what they are sharing; allow the experience to be flexible as needs change. Why: people may be sharing location data with several apps at the same time and need persistent reminders, but without distracting from the main functionality.
- Offer proportional value in exchange for the shared data: limit requests for precise data to specific features that truly need it, and position the request as close as possible to the moment of use. Why: connecting the request to a related action (like asking for directions) makes clear the use and the value of sharing precise data at that moment.

Concrete building techniques: to annotate approximate location on the map, the team drew a shaded, translucent circular area (instead of the pulsing blue dot used for precise location), which shares the visual language of the blue dot and adapts in style according to the interaction (the overlay fades when zooming in, to keep cartographic legibility). A status and control bar was added at the top of the map for people who share approximate location: on the first launch of Maps, this status appears larger and with a stronger visual treatment, recalling past decisions and giving direct access to the control; as the person interacts more with the app, the visual treatment reduces in prominence. For turn-by-turn directions (which require precise data), the app asks for "one-time access" to precise location, as close as possible to the moment of requesting the route; people who share approximate location still see the same direction buttons, but when tapping "Directions" can manually enter origin and destination, with a new option called "My Location" among the suggested places, which, when chosen, triggers the request for precise data.

Examples cited: Weather (uses location for local forecast), Wallet (uses location to associate transactions with places), Photos (uses location to tag photos and create Memories), Apple Maps (central and recurring example throughout the video: blue dot, approximate area overlay, Favorites, Search, place details, first-launch screen, status bar, Directions flow with "My Location").

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted; the content is mostly description of principles and flows.

## The details of UI typography (id: wwdc2020_10175, 30.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 22 of 22 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10175/.

Central thesis: quality UI typography depends on understanding the dynamic behavior of text (optical sizes, variable fonts, tracking, leading, text styles and Dynamic Type), and most of this behavior is already solved by the system APIs, so it is enough to use them correctly instead of reimplementing manually.

The design process Apple describes: two presenters split the video. Loïc Sander (type designer) covers the conceptual part of fonts: optical sizes, variable fonts, tracking and leading, including a historical demonstration with an 18th century typography book to show that optical sizes have existed since physical typography, they are not a digital invention. Jiang covers the practical APIs: text styles, emphasized variants, tight/loose leading, fonts with a design (rounded), support on macOS and AppKit, Catalyst, and Dynamic Type with custom fonts, including code demonstrations in UIKit and SwiftUI.

Principles stated:
- Optical sizes exist to balance legibility with aesthetic quality as scale changes; small letters need more space between them for the eye to distinguish them, and certain details (such as the position of the dot on the "i") are intentionally adjusted so as not to confuse similar-looking letters at small sizes.
- Each digital font traditionally contains a single design per glyph, so the type designer needs to choose an ideal size to design for; that is why Apple created optical variants (SF Text for sizes below 20 points, SF Display for 20 points and above) instead of relying on simple scaling.
- Variable fonts (a format introduced in 2016 as an evolution of OpenType) allow a single font file to describe how each point moves to produce related glyphs, controlled by design axes; this allowed Apple to merge optical sizes and weights into a single variable font, reducing the need for separate fonts.
- Tracking (spacing between letters) should be applied paired with optical sizes to avoid abrupt changes in text length when switching fonts; tracking is different from kerning (a micro correction between specific pairs of glyphs, made by the type designer and that normally should not be modified).
- For truncated strings, the best practice is to let the text tighten automatically (tight tracking table) instead of using the kerning API manually, because the tracking API disassembles ligatures correctly and applies tracking specific to each size.
- Leading (space between lines) should, in most cases, be left at the system's default behavior, except in exceptional cases (Arabic script, which has ascending and descending parts that can encroach on neighboring lines, gets more leading; watchOS uses tight leading to maximize information on the screen).
- Dynamic Type should be supported even with custom fonts, because it is critical for accessibility.

Concrete building techniques with exact numbers: SF Text covers sizes below 20 points and SF Display covers 20 points and above (that was the cutoff in the previous version); with SF Pro becoming variable this year, the transition between Text and Display now occurs between 17 and 28 points, requiring updated tracking tables for that range. Emphasized text styles apply a symbolic "bold" trait that maps to weights that can be medium, semibold, bold or heavy, depending on the text style. Tight leading reduces line height by 2 points and loose leading increases it by 2 points on iOS and macOS; on watchOS, the adjustment is reduced to 1 point (numeric example given: a paragraph with the body text style at 22 points of line height becomes 20 points with tight leading, or 24 points with loose leading). On Catalyst, apps with "Scale Interface to Match iPad" follow iOS sizes scaled at 77%; choosing "Optimize Interface for Mac" in Xcode activates a new set of text style sizes aligned with macOS. APIs cited: UIFontDescriptor with a symbolic bold trait for emphasized styles; UIFontMetrics (since iOS 11) to apply Dynamic Type behavior to arbitrary fonts via scaledFont, including to scale constant layout values (such as spacing between labels); in SwiftUI, the relativeTo parameter in the font(.custom) API specifies which text style the custom font should scale to (by default, body, new behavior in iOS 14); fontDescriptorwithDesign allows applying designs such as rounded to any system UI font, keeping size, weight and line height; the ScaledMetric property wrapper in SwiftUI allows scaling constant values (such as padding) proportionally to text size. New: tracking is now enabled for third-party fonts, as long as the font has a STAT table.

Examples cited: an 18th-century book (a physical type specimen used to show manual optical sizes, with letters of approximately 8 and 42 points in today's printing units), New York (the system's serif font, where optical size adaptation is most noticeable with variable fonts), Calendar (year and month header using a heavier weight than the default text style; a complete change of appearance between light and dark), Mail (large title navigation bar using the emphasized large title text style), Fitness app (tight leading to increase information density), Maps (loose leading to give large blocks of text some "breathing room"), Books and Reminders (use of New York and SF Pro Rounded combined with text styles), Podcasts (gradient background that already works well in light and dark), Notes (adopts the note's content for dark mode).

Short quotes: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted; the content is mostly technical and historical.

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

Platform adaptation, not cosmetic portability: in almost every video in this group, Apple states explicitly that bringing a Mac or iPhone component to another platform without rethinking its geometry and context of use is a recognized mistake. This appears in the explanation of why the iPad pointer is not the Mac arrow (ids wwdc2020_10640, wwdc2020_10093), in the critique of the Calendar popover in iPadOS 13 that only pointed at a button with no context (id wwdc2020_10206), and in the justification for the new iOS 14 menus that replace action sheets designed for small screens (id wwdc2020_10205).

Precision as an adjustable resource, not a fixed one: the Adaptive Precision concept for the iPad pointer (ids wwdc2020_10640, wwdc2020_10093) and the treatment of optical sizes and tracking in typography (id wwdc2020_10175) share the same logic: the interface should reduce or increase the precision it shows according to the precision the task actually needs, instead of always offering the maximum technical precision possible.

Destructive actions always require deliberate friction: the pattern of requiring confirmation in a place different from the original action appears in almost identical form in watchOS (id wwdc2020_10171, red button with a confirmation alert), in iOS 14 (id wwdc2020_10205, action sheets deliberately kept for deletion) and in the Switch Control recommendations (id wwdc2020_10019, error tolerance to avoid accidental data deletion).

Extensibility and "live intelligence" as a common thread: App Clips (id wwdc2020_10172), widgets (id wwdc2020_10103) and the introductory video on system intelligence (id wwdc2020_10086) describe the same underlying thesis: the app stopped being just an icon that opens and closes, and became a layer of functionality that manifests in context (a physical tag, a place, a time of day), always conditioned on proving value before asking for more commitment from the person (downloading the full app, sharing precise data).

Privacy and accessibility as design constraints, not extras: both the location privacy video (id wwdc2020_10162) and the Switch Control one (id wwdc2020_10019) treat these dimensions as part of the core design process (auditing the whole app, removing non-essential functionality, not as a layer added afterward), reinforcing the line from the intelligence video that "no intelligent system is worth the sacrifice of a fundamental human right" (id wwdc2020_10086).

Less is a content decision, not an aesthetic one: in App Clips (id wwdc2020_10172), widgets (id wwdc2020_10103) and watchOS (id wwdc2020_10171), the recommendation to "simplify" is always accompanied by a practical cutting criterion (a single task, at most four pieces of information, separating primary from secondary), not a vague visual preference.
