# wwdc2018

## Designing Fluid Interfaces (id: wwdc2018_803, 64.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 43 of 43 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/803/.

Central thesis: an interface feels fluid when it behaves like an extension of the mind and body of the person using it, responding, redirecting and moving the way we think and move, and not like a prescribed and fixed animation.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Chan, Nathan and Marcos, from the human interface team) say they built the iPhone X gestural interface by prototyping live, playing with the gesture until discovering the interface itself, instead of designing it statically. In the talk, the presenter describes that the question guiding the work is "what makes an interface feel fluid", and that the answer is not in hitting a frame rate, but in making the tool feel like an extension of the mind. The method cited is: first prototype the interaction together with the visual (never afterward), build interactive demos (described as being worth more than static designs), play with the prototype itself to discover how people handle it, and also use that prototype as an implementation target for the engineering team.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Response: the interface must respond instantly to any touch, because people are very sensitive to latency; any delay breaks the feeling of being an extension of the body. In the talk, the presenter describes that the iPhone was designed to respond faster to the finger.
- Constant redirection and interruption: the gesture must be able to be redirected at any moment (for example, going home and changing your mind to multitasking in the middle of the gesture), because thought and gesture must happen in parallel, not in sequence (think, decide, execute). This also helps with discovering new gestures along the path of an already known gesture.
- Fast detection of movement change: for the pause gesture that opens multitasking on the iPhone X, the team discovered that using a timer (waiting for the speed to drop for a while) was too slow; instead, they monitor the finger's acceleration, which has a peak when the person stops the gesture, allowing near-instant detection.
- Spatial consistency: elements must enter and exit the screen through symmetrical paths, because this mirrors our memory of object permanence in the real world. Example cited: iOS navigation, where an item slides in from the right when entering and returns to the right when exiting (same place in both states).
- Signaling the direction of the gesture (hinting): elements must grow toward the final state during the transition, because people are always predicting the next steps of an action. Example cited: the Control Center modules grow toward the finger as they are pressed before opening.
- Light interactions, but with amplified output: touches must be short and light, but the system must capture position, speed, force, etc. to generate an amplified result that still feels like an extension of the movement. Example cited: scrolling, where the finger stays on the screen for a short time but the content keeps moving from the transferred energy and momentum; and the swipe-to-go-home gesture.
- Rubberbanding (soft edges): when reaching the limit of the content, the interface must indicate this smoothly, never stopping abruptly, because an abrupt stop feels broken and indistinguishable from a freeze. This also applies to transitions between one element and another (for example, when moving from dragging the dock to dragging the app, the curve must be smooth, without an abrupt "handoff").
- Smooth motion frames: fluidity does not depend only on the frame rate, but on how much the content changes between adjacent frames; too much difference between frames creates a stroboscopic effect. Techniques cited: motion blur (more motion information per frame) and motion stretching (stretching the content in the direction of the movement, used on the iPhone X when the icon stretches when opening and closing an app).
- Working with behavior, not with prescribed animation: in the real world there are no fixed animation curves; objects react continuously to forces. Therefore, design must think of behavior as a continuous conversation between person and object, not an animation with a fixed beginning and end.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Layout and gesture architecture: the iPhone X gesture is described as a two-axis gestural space (horizontal between apps, vertical for home), allowing the two axes to be mixed (e.g.: going home and peeking at multitasking along the way).
- Touch (tap): the button must highlight immediately on touch, but only confirm the action when the finger lifts; there must be an extra margin around the touch area to avoid accidental cancellations; dragging the finger outside cancels the touch, and coming back inside reactivates the highlight.
- Swipe: the touch needs to move a minimum distance, called hysteresis, defined in the text as usually 10 points on iOS, before the gesture is recognized as a swipe; from there the content and the finger must move together, respecting the relative position (never use the center of the object as the drag point), and the system must track position and speed throughout the entire touch, not just the final position, in order to transfer the movement fluidly.
- One-to-one tracking: touch and content must move together; it is described as one of the central principles of iOS, so recognizable that the person immediately notices when the tracking stops being one-to-one (for example, when reaching the end of the content in a scroll). The same principle of direct connection between gesture and interface was also used in the design of UI manipulation on the Apple TV.
- Continuous feedback throughout the entire interaction, not just at the end: example cited, the flashlight button on the iPhone X changes size according to the pressure of the touch, confirming the action and teaching that pressing harder activates the flashlight; another example, the Apple TV's focus engine, which continuously represents the movement of the Siri remote on the screen, showing the selected item and the direction of the next selection.
- Multiple concurrent gestures: detect all possible gestures from the start of the action, and only cancel the others when the intention becomes clear, avoiding artificial delays; it is recommended to avoid recognizers that only respond at the end of the gesture (like UISwipeGestureRecognizer) and use ones that provide continuous information (touches, other gestureRecognizers), including position, speed, pressure and touch size. Example cited: in a contacts list, scrolling the list versus tapping to preview (3D touch) compete, and the system cancels the 3D touch if it detects scrolling.
- Inevitable delay on double tap: whenever there is a double tap, the single tap is delayed while the system waits to find out if it will be double; in the photos example, the single tap to open the app's menu is delayed, in the text, by about half a second.
- Elasticity (springs): elastic behavior is described as a ball attached to a spring, with the current value at one end and the target at the other; the underlying physical properties are mass, stiffness and damping, but the team simplified the design interface to two parameters: damping (from 100%, no overshoot, to 0%, indefinite oscillation) and response (the speed with which the value tries to reach the target); the corresponding technical terms are damping ratio and frequency response. Practical recommendation: start with 100% damping (no overshoot) so as not to distract from the task; use bounciness (less damping, like the 80% cited) when the gesture that originated the movement already has momentum, for example when swiping to dismiss Now Playing in the Music app (versus the single tap that opens Now Playing, without momentum, which uses 100% damping).
- Bounciness as a functional cue: on the iPhone X cover sheet, the flashlight and camera buttons respond with a bit of bounce to the single tap to teach, without text, that you need to press more firmly to activate (a more intentional gesture, to avoid accidental activation).
- Momentum projection: to decide the correct "endpoint" of a drag-and-drop gesture (the example used is FaceTime's floating PIP, which can go to any of the four corners of the screen), looking only at the closest position does not work well; the solution is to project a hypothetical position using the gesture's speed and a deceleration rate (the same familiar logic from scrolling, via UIScrollView.DecelerationRate), calculating an imaginary "endpoint" and sending the element there. The projection technique also works for scale and rotation, not just position.
- Teaching gestures: reusable visual cues (for example, content cut off at the bottom edge of a list indicating there is more to see, page indicators, "grabber handles" for sliding panels); raising interactive elements to a separate plane to indicate they are draggable (example cited: the on/off toggle button, whose "knob" is raised to another plane); reinforcing a dynamic behavior with an equivalent static animation (example cited: in Safari, tapping the X closes the tab by sliding it to the left, teaching that the same manual swipe gesture produces the same result); explicit text explanations, recommended only in moderation, and only for gestures used repeatedly in several places (never for intermittent gestures, which people forget).

Examples cited (apps, screens, components) and what each example teaches:
- iPhone X gesture (home, multitasking, switching apps): teaches continuous redirection and interruption, and the idea of a two-axis gestural space.
- Multitasking via pause on the iPhone X: teaches detection of movement change via acceleration instead of a timer.
- iOS navigation (pushing/going back a screen): teaches spatial consistency via symmetrical paths.
- Control Center: teaches "hinting" in the direction of the gesture, with the modules growing toward the finger before opening fully.
- Scrolling in general: teaches lightness of interaction with amplified output, and elastic behavior (rubberbanding) when exceeding the content limit.
- Swipe-to-go-home gesture: teaches lightness of interaction that still takes advantage of the muscle memory of a physical button (finger goes down and comes back up).
- Transition between dragging the dock and dragging the app on the iPhone X: teaches smooth handoff between two tracking behaviors, without an abrupt stop.
- Opening/closing apps on the iPhone X (motion stretching of the icon): teaches the technique of stretching the content in the direction of the movement to smooth the perception between frames.
- Photos app versus switching apps: teaches assigning more or less conceptual "mass" to a behavior according to the perceived weight of the content (lighter photos, heavier apps).
- Music app (Now Playing minibar): teaches when to use 100% damping (touch without momentum) versus 80% bounciness (swipe with momentum).
- Flashlight and camera buttons on the iPhone X cover sheet: teach bounciness as a functional cue that a firmer gesture is needed.
- FaceTime's PIP: teaches the momentum projection technique for choosing the correct endpoint among four possible corners.
- Calculator app (button tap): teaches the basic behavior of immediate highlight and confirmation only when the finger lifts, plus the extra touch margin.
- Contacts list (scroll versus 3D touch preview): teaches parallel detection of multiple gestures and late cancellation.
- Photos app (double tap to zoom): teaches the inevitable delay introduced by double-tap recognition.
- Safari (closing a tab via the X): teaches reinforcing behavior through static animation aligned with the corresponding gesture.
- On/off toggle: teaches the technique of raising an element to a separate plane to indicate that it is draggable.
- Apple TV (UI manipulation and focus engine): teaches that direct tracking and continuous feedback also apply to interfaces without touch on the screen.

Short literal quotes:
"It's really important that we build demos of this stuff."
"The interactive demo we think is really worth a million static designs."

## Intentional Design (id: wwdc2018_802, 40.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 28 of 28 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/802/.

Central thesis: truly good apps are born from a conscious and deliberate focus on people, sustained by five elements of intentional design: radical simplification, deep understanding, extreme focus, personal personality, and direct communication.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Doug LeMoine, interaction designer) analyzes existing App Store apps, collected over the course of a year, to extract a vocabulary of intention. The method, according to the talk, is to dig (dig) beyond people's surface needs until finding the real need, and then to ask, when reviewing UI decisions already taken by convention (such as the icon and the "Home" tab), why this thing needs to be this way, instead of applying automatic patterns just because they are familiar.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Radical simplification: remove UI, don't add it, to leave people focused on what they really need, considering the real context of use (for example, a tired person lost in another country). Being radical implies risk, because removing elements can be shocking if done poorly.
- Deep understanding: knowing the person and their real problem, not just the surface problem that appears first; the talk describes that personas, market segmentation, research and journey maps only work if they seem authentic and generate real empathy, because these artifacts often seem artificial and don't connect with anyone.
- Extreme focus: designing for one very specific person (an "extreme case") can free the design to be more coherent and simple, and paradoxically reach more people. The Rollaboard suitcase example shows that the inventor, Robert Plath, was a pilot and designed for pilots and flight attendants, an audience described in the text as 0.1% of 1% of the travelers market at the time (100 thousand "travel professionals" cited by the Census, against more than 100 million occasional travelers in the US), but the result became the market's de facto standard.
- Personality (personal touch): apps can create a personal connection by incorporating a specific and even risky voice; sharp copy only works if, in the right context, it is memorable, and it shouldn't be a generic embellishment.
- Direct communication: labels and metaphors must communicate exactly the scope of what the app offers, avoiding generic and indirect labels that hide the real usefulness.

Concrete interface-building techniques cited:
- Remove the UI until essentially only a single large button remains (example of the translation app) to eliminate decision and friction in a stressful context.
- Reduce the number of decisions the person needs to make at each stage of a flow (example of the workout app): the person chooses the possible movements a single time, and at each workout the app randomly draws the sequence and duration of each exercise, to combat inertia (defined in the text as "an inclination not to move").
- Tab labels (tab bar) must be direct and specific, never generic like "Home", because directness limits and clearly communicates the app's scope; the text uses the tab bar of the WWDC app itself as an example of quickly scanning the app's scope.
- Extension of familiar metaphors (physical cards) to create instantly understandable interactions, and the possibility of extending that metaphor even further to generate surprise and delight, as long as the extension increases real utility, not just the visual effect.
- Translation of a physical experience into digital using the same type of tactile feedback: turning the Digital Crown producing a slight haptic return at each step, to mimic the feeling of passing a rosary bead between the fingers.

Examples cited (apps, screens, components) and what each example teaches:
- iTranslate Converse: teaches radical simplification, with the screen reduced to a single large button, allowing two people to converse in real time without either person needing to already know the app.
- Vanido (singing app): teaches radical simplification of a complex domain (music education), removing sheet music and notes, with only one interactive element on screen during the lesson.
- Streaks Workout: teaches deep understanding of a real need (combat inertia and boredom, not just "staying healthy"), inspired, according to the developer's account to the presenter, by a workout method used in prisons with a deck of cards (each suit representing a movement and the card number representing repetitions), adapted to remove the need to memorize which suit corresponds to which movement.
- Rollaboard Suitcase (a suitcase with small wheels, not an app): teaches extreme focus, design decisions serving one very specific person (the inventor himself, an airline pilot), leading to the handle on top, wheels at the edges and a size compatible with an airplane's overhead compartment.
- Carrot Weather: teaches personality as an element of personal connection; the app's voice, according to the developer cited (Brian Mueller), is inspired by people close to him (wife, sister, mother), and the app allows adjusting the level of "attitude" of the personality.
- "Home" icon/tab in browsers and in the Finder versus app tab bars: teaches why a metaphor that works in one context (browser, Finder) can fail in another (app tab bar), for being generic and indirect.
- Tinycards: teaches direct and respectful extension of a physical metaphor (two-sided cards), making the interaction immediately understandable.
- Gorogoa (game): teaches how to extend a metaphor (cards) beyond what physical objects can do, creating a moment of surprise that reinforces, rather than breaks, the original metaphor.
- Rosarium (rosary app for Apple Watch): teaches translation of a physical and personal experience (rosary beads) into digital haptic feedback via the Digital Crown, combining simplicity, deep understanding and direct communication.

Short literal quotes:
"Directness creates predictability."
"It's a matter of turning off the automatic part of your brain, of slowing down, and challenging the obvious."

## The Qualities of Great Design (id: wwdc2018_801, 53.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 36 of 36 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/801/.

Central thesis: quality in design is not an isolated technical attribute, but the perceptible result of time, effort and carefulness (care), and it manifests in four aspirations recurring among designers: simplicity, being dazzling (stunning), timelessness and positive impact on people's lives.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Lauren Strehlow, design evangelist at Apple) interviewed 13 designers from outside and inside Apple (including the creative director of The Game Band, co-founders of the Layers Conference, the VP of Design at Khan Academy, and internal colleagues in typography, sound, motion, visual and interaction), gathering more than 15 hours of recording, without sending the questions in advance, and distilled the answers around central questions: what quality is, what it feels like, why it matters, and what techniques designers use day to day.

Principles stated and the why of each (cited by the interviewees and synthesized by the presenter):
- Quality is what is collectively agreed to be good, and it implies the absence of randomness: an interviewee (Mike) uses the example of a room's panels with irregular-width joints to show that small visible details communicate, or compromise, the sense of good finish (craftsmanship).
- Quality is the result of time, effort and carefulness (care): another interviewee (Gary) describes care as being careful enough to make something the best it can be and also caring about the experience of whoever will use it, "taking yourself out of yourself" to put yourself in someone else's place.
- Difficult work produces a more satisfying result: the analogy used is pedaling to the top of a mountain to then come down, where the effort of the climb is what makes the descent more rewarding.
- Quality evokes trust, and that trust comes from the product fulfilling its promise in every facet of the experience (not just the interface, but also the App Store description, response to reviews etc.); quality, according to one interviewee, "has to be earned" and should not be self-proclaimed with a seal or label.
- We want quality out of a survival instinct, according to one of the interviewees, because seeking things that are well built and safe is a basic survival strategy.

The four design aspirations cited, with the why of each:
- Simplicity: a simple app does not try to do more than it needs to, and does well what it does; the presenter argues that occasional-use apps (such as a recipe app or a pet care app) need to be instantly understandable, so that the person's mental energy goes to the real task in life, not to decoding the interface.
- Being dazzling (stunning): defined by an interviewee (Caroline) as "polish", elements aligned exactly as intended, including how the interaction feels (for example, swipe animations aligned to the gesture). In games, the speech describes that visual immersion should come along with teaching the rules through active discovery, not through imposed tutorials, because people remember better what they learn by experimenting on their own (example cited: the developer of the game Where Cards Fall).
- Timelessness: a sound designer (Hugo) describes the pursuit of "durability", citing the goal of creating a ringtone that still sounds good after 5 or 10 years, an idea the presenter extends to visual and interaction design as well.
- Positive impact on people's lives: cited by colleague Doug (the same presenter of video 802) as the element that separates valuable apps from generic apps.

Concrete design-work techniques cited by the interviewees:
- Drawing exaggerated caricatures of a shape (for example, the shoulder curve of a lowercase "m" in type design) to visually communicate what is bothersome about a detail, when words are not enough; a type designer (Loic) describes this as a path to finding a "joint gaze" between collaborators.
- Prototyping and testing in a real context with real people, instead of assuming an idea will work just because it was well discussed or coded; the example cited is colleague Doug testing a simple interface design with museum visitors to validate hypotheses before investing energy in the change.
- Being open to feedback and recognizing one's own blind spots that develop after spending a lot of time with one's own work, cited by interviewee May-Li as a reason to trust a collaborative team that gives honest criticism.
- Maintaining a posture of modesty when designing for broad audiences (cited by an interviewee named Cas, who works with platform standards): assuming that one's own experience and opinion are not the only correct ones, and actively asking, for example, whether an idea will work for someone in a wheelchair or for someone migrating from PC to Mac.
- Aligning communication expectations and ways of working at the start of a team collaboration, making explicit how each person prefers to communicate and what the shared goals are.

Examples cited: the examples in this video are mostly quotes from the interviewed designers (not screens of specific apps shown in a demo), except for the mention of the game in development Where Cards Fall (cited by name, without a screen demonstration) as an illustration of teaching rules through active discovery.

Short literal quotes:
"If something is quality, it implies that there is nothing random about it."
"Sensing the human behind the experience I think is really an earmark of something great."

## The Life of a Button (id: wwdc2018_804, 36.0 min)

Basis: transcript and 23 of 23 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/804/.

Central thesis: even the simplest interface control, a button, carries a complexity of interaction, visual and sound design decisions that deserve careful consideration in each of the three phases of an interaction: before, during and after the touch.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Julian, a designer and prototyper of interfaces for future hardware, and Hugo, a sound designer) build, as a live case study, a fictional "make toast" button for an app connected to a toaster, literally testing labels, shapes, animations and alternative sounds until deciding. The method described is explicitly iterative: "the only way to know whether an animation is appropriate or not is to try it out" (trying and testing on a real device repeatedly).

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Buttons are indirect controllers of action: tapping the button produces an effect somewhere else (different from direct manipulation, like pulling a lever), which requires designing the button and the action it triggers separately, but in a connected way.
- Feedback is how the app communicates what it is doing, and there are two categories: telling (explicit text or icons) and showing (letting the person experience the result over time, via visual, audio or haptic means).
- Feedback should be thought through across the three phases of the interaction: before touching (the so called "perceived affordance", that is, what the person thinks they can do with those pixels on the screen, based on previous experience with iOS and the current context), during the touch ("feedforward", which helps the person understand what is happening while the finger is still in contact), and after lifting the finger (classic feedback about the result).
- The touch area (hit area) can and should be larger than the button's visual area, especially for small controls.
- Touch confirmation animations should be very fast (or instant); growing slowly or fading in makes the control seem unresponsive; slower, continuous animations only make sense when they respond continuously to continuous pressure (like 3D touch).
- Interactions should allow changing your mind: dragging the finger outside the button's area cancels the tap, and moving back inside reactivates the highlight, the same principle that makes paging between screens fluid (the decision is only confirmed when the finger lifts).

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when spoken:
- Labeling: the text describes the process of choosing between "toast" (ambiguous between verb and noun), an icon (discarded for not being clear to someone using the app for the first time) and finally "make toast", chosen for clarity.
- Button shape: use standard iOS button shapes instead of inventing something new, because the person will open this fictional app rarely (once a day, at most) and will not have time to learn a new convention.
- Context of use: the designer tests the prototype designed in Keynote, placed on a physical iPhone, held in his own bedroom in the morning, to check whether the button's meaning is clear in the real context of use.
- Button sound (with Hugo): three sound options were created and compared (A, very minimalist, but incomplete; B, more realistic and physical, but a bit metallic and aggressive; C, chosen, with its own character and what the text calls "tonal confirmation", like a mark of correctness built into the sound). The final decision was option C, for having a friendly personality and being recognizable and unique, not associated with any other app.
- Two click structure in the sound: they discovered that button sounds are more satisfying when they have two distinct clicks, one on pressing and another on releasing the finger.
- Intermediate state sound (toaster heating): they considered a continuous sound, then a timer sound fading every half minute, and finally decided that this state did not need any sound at all, prioritizing only a clean, simple notification when the toast is ready, because that is the moment that really matters.
- Four building blocks of sound, described as used in any sound project: timbre (the color of the tone, determined by the material, shape and playing style of an instrument), frequency/pitch (a sound played at high frequency suggests a small object, and at low frequency suggests a large object), duration (sounds that repeat many times should be short and subtle; rare sounds can be longer and more elaborate), and volume/amplitude (taps and alarms cannot be missed, but a common UI sound should be subtle, at risk of becoming unpleasant).
- Separate cancel button: when testing a red "stop toasting" button in place of the original button, they decided that the red color was "too much" and opted for a separate button, visually distinct from the "make toast" button.

Examples cited: the fictional "toast" app (connected toaster) is the only detailed example in the video, used as an end to end case study; the text also mentions, in passing, the real sound of the iPhone X volume button (recorded and amplified) as a reference for how physical button sounds are byproducts of the material, not deliberately designed, unlike what is possible in software.

Short, literal quotes:
"Details are designed even if they seem obvious, even if they're as simple as a button, even if they're as simple as a click sound."
"Learn by trying things out."

## Designing Notifications (id: wwdc2018_806, 38.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 26 of 26 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/806/.

Central thesis: well-designed notifications are a powerful way to communicate timely, valuable information, but they require respect for people's attention at every step, from the permission request to the grouping and the content of each notification, on both iPhone and Apple Watch.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Jon Dascola and Heena Ko, designers on the human interface team) review the historical evolution of notifications on iOS (from the blue-box modal alert at the iPhone's launch, through the lock screen queue introduced in iOS 4, to rich notifications) to justify the new iOS 12 tools, and use real examples of App Store apps to illustrate good and bad practices.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- People's attention is valuable, and interrupting someone is a privilege that needs to be respected; that's why each notification must have a specific purpose and a clear message or task, not just be a pretext to open the app.
- Choose between asking for access to the lock screen (for urgent content or content that requires an immediate response) or delivering directly to the notification center, without the initial prompt (for content consumed passively, with no need for an immediate response); this second option, new in iOS 12, avoids the problem of having only one chance to ask for permission without context.
- When deciding to ask for lock screen permission, don't ask as soon as the person opens the app for the first time; give the person time to experience the app, explain why the notifications will be valuable, and present the request at the moment the reason is already clear (for example, right after completing an order in a delivery app).
- Notification grouping by default happens by app; more granular grouping (threads, via thread identifier) should be used only when there are significantly different types of content (such as separate conversations in a messaging app), and shouldn't be overused.
- Rich notifications should work as a self-contained information package, allowing a specific task to be completed without needing to open the app and lose the context of what was being done.
- On Apple Watch, notifications are described in the text as the primary form of people's interaction with apps, because the watch is light and always on the wrist; the system sends the notification to whichever device is most accessible at the moment (the watch, when the phone is out of range).
- Interactions on the watch should be quick; the goal is to enrich the notification, not recreate the full app experience inside it.

Concrete interface-building techniques cited:
- New "manage" button when you swipe a notification on the lock screen, which opens a quick-adjustment UI with the "deliver quietly" option (goes straight to the notification center, without appearing on the lock screen, with no sound or haptics) and a button to turn off all notifications from that app.
- Notifications delivered directly to the notification center (without the initial modal prompt) appear with the notification's normal content, but with extra buttons at the bottom of the "platter" to decide whether or not to allow future notifications.
- Use of the thread identifier (an existing API, already used in notification content extensions) to group related notifications under a free-form string identifier; notifications with the same identifier are grouped into the same thread.
- Notification settings should be designed with clear hierarchy and granularity by content category (example cited: ESPN separates notifications by sport, such as basketball versus baseball; New York Times lets you choose sections such as sports or politics, while keeping breaking news and highlights).
- On Apple Watch, elements such as the attached image, title, body and quick actions are automatically inherited from the iPhone notification; it's possible to customize further with sash color, background color, icons and inline video.
- Interactive notifications in watchOS 5 (feature cited as new) allow completing tasks directly in the notification using the Digital Crown and taps, without opening the app: rate and pay for a ride by turning the crown and tapping stars (example from the ride-hailing app DiDi), extend parking time by turning the crown on a stepper (example from the Pay By Phone app), extend a restaurant table wait time by up to 45 minutes directly in the notification (example from Yelp), and log taking medication with a "take all" button (example from a fictional medication reminder app).

Examples cited (apps, screens, components) and what each example teaches:
- Dark Sky: teaches a notification with a clear purpose and a real condition change ("the rain is starting"), not constant noise; also cited on Apple Watch, receiving a storm alert during a hike without the phone nearby.
- Door Dash: teaches a notification of a direct, expected outcome (food has arrived), with no need for more context.
- HQ (live game): teaches a timely notification justified by the nature of the product itself (a live game at a scheduled time).
- Reminders: teaches delivery flexibility by time or by location, according to the person's need.
- Headspace: teaches respect for timing, letting the person choose when to receive reminders, so as not to contradict the purpose of a meditation app with poorly timed notifications.
- CNN: teaches explicit configuration of alert frequency right after permission is granted.
- Duolingo: teaches consideration by pausing notifications when the person stops interacting, instead of insisting, as a long-term strategy to maintain trust.
- ESPN and The New York Times: teach the design of notification settings with granularity by content category.
- Photos, Calendar and Messages (with 3D Touch and tapback): teach rich notifications that let you like a photo, see availability and accept an invitation, or reply to a message with tapback, all without opening the app.
- Podcasts: teaches a single thread grouping all new episodes, instead of one thread per podcast, and a consolidated rich notification with individual play controls per episode.
- Castro (podcast app) on Apple Watch: teaches automatic inheritance of elements from the iPhone notification to the watch, with minimal effort.
- My Weather: teaches personalization by location.
- Wallet on Apple Watch: teaches timing personalization (a show ticket arrives exactly at the time of the event, with a full-screen QR code).
- Qantas Airlines: teaches personalization throughout a complete journey (sharing flight time, airport pickup reminder, option to report a delay).

Short literal quotes:
"Notifications are not just a reason to get people to launch into your app."
"Our attention is valuable."

## Presenting Design Work (id: wwdc2018_811, 15.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/811/.

Central thesis: a good design idea can die because of a poorly made presentation, so presenting design work requires as much care as the design work itself, following clear practices about objectives, feedback, one's own voice, respect for the audience and narrative.

The design process Apple describes: this video is not about interface construction, but about communicating design work; the presenter organizes the content around three elements of any presentation (who presents, the audience, and the central idea) and from these derive ten practices, illustrated with a continuous, humorous example of a fictional toaster app.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Clarify one's own objectives before putting together the presentation (approval, communication with engineering, proposing a new idea), and also understand the audience's objectives, because meeting their objectives is part of meeting one's own.
- Treat all feedback as useful data, never as personal failure; even when someone proposes an unworkable solution, the problem that person is trying to solve is usually valid and worth clarifying.
- Be willing to change position in the face of new information, because the audience knows things the presenter doesn't; never pretend to understand confusing feedback, but rather ask for clarification.
- Seek advice and practice the presentation with colleagues before the real presentation, because design work should not be done alone, and it is hard to keep control of many loose threads alone.
- Use one's own voice, be genuine, show passion for the reason to care about the subject, and explain the process (the journey) that led to the conclusions presented, avoiding both overconfidence and saying only what the audience wants to hear.
- Respect the audience: have a clear agenda, recap what has already been discussed in previous meetings, show how earlier feedback influenced the current work, and never assume the audience already knows what things mean or why certain decisions were made.
- Make the presentation relatable: speak in the first person about the experience of the person using the product, instead of talking about "users" as distant third parties, and use flow diagrams in moderation (useful for engineers, but they create distance when communicating concepts).
- Keep the presentation as a dialogue, not a monologue, asking questions and shaping the narrative to generate an "aha moment", because this keeps the audience's mind trying to predict the outcome, which increases engagement.
- Define each problem in a single sentence, and seek explicit agreement from the audience on these problem sentences before moving forward, revisiting them throughout the presentation; avoid justifying decisions with subjective reasons ("I chose this color because I like it"), because this does not persuade those who don't share the same preference.
- Structure the presentation as a story, with a beginning, middle and end, contrasting today's reality with a better future made possible by the proposed idea; the text attributes this structure to Nancy Duarte's research, described as a reference in presentations.
- Keep everything simple, removing what is not essential to the central message, citing the maxim "the main thing is to keep the main thing the main thing" and the phrase attributed to "if I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter".

Concrete techniques cited: no visual interface-building technique is discussed in this video; the content is entirely about the practice of communicating and presenting design work.

Examples cited: the only example throughout the video is the fictional connected toaster app (the same humorous premise used in video 804, "The Life of a Button"), used to illustrate, in narrative form, how to tell the story of a problem (cold and burnt toast) and a solution (notification and remote activation of the toaster) in the first person.

Short, literal quotes:
"There is no failure, only feedback."
"The main thing is to keep the main thing the main thing."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- In 2018, Apple treats interaction fluidity (id 803) and care for every micro-decision of an isolated control (id 804, the toaster button) as faces of the same discipline: both videos insist that small detail, whether a two-axis gesture or an audible click, is where perceived quality is really decided.
- There is a declared preference for prototyping and testing on a real device over static or purely theoretical design, repeated in four of the six videos in this group: 803 ("an interactive demo is worth more than a thousand static designs"), 804 ("the only way to know is to try it"), 801 (the example of colleague Doug testing a paper prototype with museum visitors) and 802 (the implicit method of analyzing real App Store apps instead of theorizing in the abstract).
- The group repeatedly argues that less decision is better decision: radical simplification (802), reducing choices in a flow (Streaks Workout, 802), a light touch with an amplified result (803), and the recommendation to keep the presentation of one's own work simple and focused on a single central message (811) all point to the same value of removing, not adding.
- Feedback, in the broad sense of "informing the person about what is happening", appears as a central concept in at least three videos with complementary meanings: interface feedback during a touch (803 and 804, including the specific term "feedforward"), sound and haptic feedback as part of the same communication system (804 and 806), and human feedback received during a design presentation (811), suggesting that Apple uses the same word and the same logic of responsiveness both for machines and for collaboration between people.
- Respect for people's attention appears as an explicit ethical principle both in notifications (806, "our attention is valuable, interrupting is a privilege") and in internal design presentations (811, "respect the audience or it will not respect you"), showing that the same care expected of an app is also expected of the design team's own internal work process.
- Extreme cases and very specific audiences are treated as a source of focus, not risk: the example of the Rollaboard suitcase and the audience of 0.1% of 1% of travelers (802) is cited explicitly as an argument that deeply serving a narrow need can produce a standard adopted by everyone afterward.
