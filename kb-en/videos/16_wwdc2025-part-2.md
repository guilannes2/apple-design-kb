# wwdc2025 (part 2)

## Better together: SwiftUI and RealityKit (id: wwdc2025_274, 30.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 21 of 21 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/274/.

Central thesis: in visionOS 26, SwiftUI and RealityKit start exchanging data in both directions and smoothly substituting for one another, so that traditional UI and interactive 3D content work as a single system.

The design process Apple describes: the session uses a narrative of two robots (Sparky, in SwiftUI/Model3D, and Bolts, in RealityKit) to illustrate the central question of when to use each framework. In the talk, the presenters describe that the decision depends on whether or not you need access to entities, components and systems: if the goal is to display a self-contained 3D asset, the answer is Model3D; if refined control is needed (particles, gestures, manipulation, custom systems), the answer is RealityView. The transition between the two is treated as part of the normal design flow, not as a rewrite: switching from Model3D to RealityView without breaking the layout is presented as an explicit technique.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Model3D is compared to a SwiftUI Image view, but for 3D content: it serves to display a self-contained asset, participating in SwiftUI's layout system.
- RealityView is the choice when the app needs detailed control (games, effects, custom behavior), because only RealityKit entities accept components such as ParticleEmitter.
- Rule against infinite loops: never modify, inside the `update` closure of a RealityView, an observable property that the view's body also reads, because that rewrites the value and triggers a chain reevaluation. It is safe to modify unobserved entities, modify inside the `make` closure (which does not create a dependency and only runs once), inside a custom system, or inside gesture closures.
- Splitting large views into small, self-contained views avoids loops and also helps performance, because a change in an unrelated entity no longer forces reevaluation of views that don't depend on it.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Model3DAsset loads animations from an asset and lets you choose which one to play (for example via a Picker); AnimationPlaybackController is now Observable, allowing the use of `@Bindable` and a Slider to show and control the animation's time.
- ConfigurationCatalog stores alternative representations of an entity (geometries, component values, material properties); in visionOS 26, you can initialize a Model3D with a ConfigurationCatalog and switch between variants (for example, changing the robot's "outfit").
- The `.realityViewLayoutBehavior` modifier has three options: `.fixedSize` (the RealityView rigidly adjusts to the content's initial bounds, mimicking Model3D), `.flexible` (default behavior, occupies all available space, origin at the center of the view) and `.centered` (shifts the origin so the content is centered in the view). None of the three options repositions or scales the entities themselves; they only move the RealityView's origin point. The size is evaluated only once, right after the `make` closure.
- Object Manipulation: in SwiftUI, the `.manipulable` modifier enables moving with one hand, rotating with one or two hands, scaling by pinching with both hands and passing the object from one hand to the other; parameters let you restrict which operations are allowed (for example, disabling scale) and adjust inertia (high inertia makes the object "feel heavy"). In RealityKit, the same behavior comes from `ManipulationComponent`, added via `configureEntity`, which also automatically injects `CollisionComponent`, `InputTargetComponent` and `HoverEffectComponent`.
- Manipulation events (start, update, release and handoff between hands) can be subscribed to in order to play custom sounds, first disabling the default audio with `audioConfiguration = .none`.
- Three new components bring RealityKit closer to SwiftUI: `ViewAttachmentComponent` (adds a SwiftUI view to any entity, from any point in the code, without needing to declare attachments ahead of time as before), `GestureComponent` (applies SwiftUI gestures directly to the entity, with values relative to the entity's own coordinate space) and `PresentationComponent` (presents popovers, sheets and other SwiftUI presentations directly from RealityKit, with a boolean binding controlling display/dismissal). The practical recommendation is to always pair `GestureComponent` with `InputTargetComponent` and `CollisionComponent`.
- Entities are now observable: reading an entity's `observable` property lets you track changes in position, scale, rotation, children and components (including custom ones), via `withObservationTracking` or via SwiftUI's native observation, which allows an entity to function as a view's "data model" (for example, a minimap that follows a robot's position).
- The Spatial framework gained the `CoordinateSpace3D` protocol, which converts values between any pair of compatible coordinate spaces (Entity and Scene from RealityKit, GeometryProxy3D from SwiftUI via `.coordinateSpace3D()`), passing through an intermediate shared space and accounting for points-to-meters conversion and axis direction.
- Implicit animation between the two frameworks: `content.animate()` inside a RealityView, or `Entity.animate(with:)` outside it, apply a SwiftUI animation to changes in supported RealityKit components (Transform, Audio, Model and Light, the latter with color properties).

Examples cited: Sparky (Model3D with Model3DAsset and animated dance; changing "outfit" via ConfigurationCatalog) teaches the animation API and Model3D variants; Bolts (RealityKit entity in the greenhouse, with a name plate via ViewAttachmentComponent, toggle via GestureComponent, outfit popover via PresentationComponent and minimap via observable entity) teaches bringing SwiftUI UI and interaction into RealityKit; the spark effect between the two robots, computed via CoordinateSpace3D, teaches distance conversion between a Model3D in SwiftUI and an entity in RealityKit; Bolts's "return to original position" behavior when released teaches combining Object Manipulation with implicit animation.

Quotes: "Think of it like SwiftUI's Image view but for 3D assets." "Don't modify your observed state within your update closure."

## Set the scene with SwiftUI in visionOS (id: wwdc2025_290, 24.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 19 of 19 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/290/.

Central thesis: visionOS 26 brings to windows, volumes and immersive spaces a set of lifecycle APIs inherited from macOS/UIKit (restoration, launch behavior, surface anchoring, clipping margins), so that the app's content feels more present in the person's physical space.

The design process described: the presenter uses a fictional app for staging Shakespeare plays (with the BOTanist robot) to walk through lifecycle decisions. The decision logic is made explicit as questions: is a window transient, dependent on a specific app state, or a one-time action (like login)? If so, disable restoration. Can a secondary window leave the person stuck in an unexpected state when relaunching the app? If so, use `.suppressed`. Does the app need more than one instance of this window? If not, use `Window` instead of `WindowGroup`.

Principles stated and why:
- Prefer restoration for most scenes, because people expect to be able to lock their windows in space and retrieve them afterward; the system does this automatically.
- Disable restoration (`restorationBehavior(.disabled)`) for welcome screens, UI dependent on a specific app state, or completed one-time actions, because restoring them out of context leaves the person in a state that makes no sense (for example, a tools window alone, without the immersive space it is supposed to control).
- Immersive spaces are never restored automatically.
- Prefer `defaultLaunchBehavior(.suppressed)` on secondary scenes, to avoid bringing them back when relaunching the app from the Home View, avoiding the same type of unexpected state.
- Single windows (`Window`, not `WindowGroup`) serve to prevent duplication of important interfaces (a game window, a video call) or for supplementary functionality that does not need more than one instance.
- Surface anchoring (surface snapping) and clipping margins should reflect a real change of context: when anchoring a volume on a table, for example, the example shows hiding the virtual "platform" under the robot because the real table already fulfills that role.
- Clipping margins (`preferredWindowClippingMargins`) serve only for visual effect, never for interactive content, and the margins granted may be smaller than the ones requested (which is why it is necessary to read `windowClippingMargins` from the environment before drawing).
- Presentations (popovers, sheets, alerts, menus etc.) get a "breakthrough" visual treatment to stay visible when occluded by 3D content; the default is a subtle blend, but it can be customized to `prominent` (breaks over the content) or `none`.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- `restorationBehavior(.disabled)` on `WindowGroup`; equivalent in UIKit via `destructionConditions` with `.systemDisconnection`.
- `defaultLaunchBehavior` to prioritize a specific window on first launch (conditioned on an app state) and `defaultLaunchBehavior(.suppressed)` to prevent a window from coming back on relaunch; the role of the default window needs to match the "preferred default scene session role" from Info.plist, otherwise the wrong type of scene (for example, a volume) is ignored.
- `SurfaceSnappingInfo`: `isSnapped` gives the general anchoring status; the ARKit classification of the anchored surface requires explicit permission, enabled with the Info.plist keys "Application Wants Detailed Surface Info" and "Privacy World-Sensing Usage-Description".
- `onVolumeViewpointChange` reacts to the change in the person's viewpoint (for example, to hide walls that block the view of the volume while walking around the table).
- `preferredWindowClippingMargins` (set the desired margin, converting meters to points with `PhysicalMetric`) and `windowClippingMargins` (read the margin actually granted by the environment) to render decorative content (such as a waterfall) outside the bounds of the scene.
- `presentationBreakthroughEffect` (for presentations) and `breakthroughEffect` (for other elements), with options `subtle`, `prominent` and `none`.
- `onWorldRecenter`: listens for the event fired when the person presses and holds the Digital Crown to recenter the experience, important for recalculating positions based on ARKit data.
- Progressive immersion style: the immersive content portal can be resized by turning the Digital Crown, and now accepts landscape aspect ratio or the new portrait, recommended for experiences with a high degree of motion (such as ported iPhone games), because keeping the periphery stable helps comfort.
- Mixed immersion style with `immersiveEnvironmentBehavior(.coexist)`: allows the immersive content to also blend with system environments (the example shows watching the play "on the Moon"), recommended when the app does not require the person to be aware of the real environment around them.
- `RemoteImmersiveSpace` (visionOS 26 + macOS Tahoe): uses `CompositorLayer` to render with Metal from a Mac app and display remotely on Vision Pro, useful for previewing scenes without transferring the project between platforms.
- `CompositorContent`: new builder type that gives `CompositorLayer` access to environment variables (such as `scenePhase`, `openWindow`) and to modifiers (such as `onImmersionChange`, `onWorldRecenter`), previously unavailable because `CompositorLayer` is not a View.
- Scene bridging for UIKit/AppKit apps: create a class that extends `UIHostingSceneDelegate`, declare the SwiftUI scenes in the `rootScene` property, and then request that scene with `UISceneSessionActivationRequest` and `activateSceneSession` (an equivalent API exists for AppKit on macOS).

Examples cited: BOTanist (the robot game that takes care of plants, a recurring reference for the design team) illustrates volumes and surface anchoring; the presenter's personal theater app (stage selection, robot staging Shakespeare) walks through launch, restoration, snapping and clipping margins in practice; Safari, cited as a real example of scene bridging for its Spatial Browsing feature, teaches that legacy UIKit apps can gain volumes and immersive spaces without a complete rewrite.

Quotes: "prefer the .suppressed defaultLaunchBehavior on secondary scenes" "Immersive spaces are not restored."

## Design hover interactions for visionOS (id: wwdc2025_303, 14.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 16 of 16 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/303/.

Central thesis: well-designed hover effects (custom animations, Look to Scroll and persistent controls) make visionOS apps feel more alive and responsive, as long as they respect privacy (the app never knows where the person is looking) and avoid unexpected movement.

The design process described: the session follows the order eye input fundamentals, custom effects, Look to Scroll, persistent controls. In the speech, the presenter insists that designing hover is more iterative than other parts of the interface: the guidance is to try several options, always test on a real device (because the effect reacts to where the person is actually looking, something a video does not reproduce) and build intuition over time. To decide whether a scroll view should get "Look to Scroll", the criterion is to ask: is this view primarily for reading/navigation (enable) or is it a list of UI controls (do not enable)? Is this primary content of the app? Is there consistency with similar lists within the same app?

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Rounded shapes (circles, pills, rounded rectangles) on interactive elements, because they draw the eye to the center of the shape, making it easier to aim.
- Give each interactive element at least 60 points of total space, even if the element looks visually smaller, to allow precise interaction; for fixed-scale 3D objects, 60 points correspond to an angular size of about 2.5 degrees, which is equivalent to approximately 4.4 cm on an object at a distance of one meter.
- Apply a highlight effect to every interactive element, including selectable 3D objects, with the shape of the highlight always matching the shape of the content.
- The system applies hover outside the app's process, to protect privacy; that is why the view only defines two states (default and hover) and the system animates between them, which means a custom effect can never trigger an action inside the app just by being looked at.
- Custom effects fall into three categories, each with a purpose: "Instant" (start the instant the person looks, for small, contextual, non-interactive information), "Delayed" (show more content only after a delay, preventing the person from accidentally activating something by glancing past it, the case of tooltips) and "Ramp" (start slowly as a visual cue and complete with a spring-like curve when the person shows clear intent, balancing immediate feedback with not being distracting).
- Provide anchoring elements: keep part of the view the same between the two states to give context; text should not shift (it interrupts reading); if the view is completely different between the two states, people get confused about what they are looking at.
- Every hover effect must start from a visible element; hidden elements should only appear when the person looks at something visible nearby (the example is the resize control appearing when looking at the corner of a window).
- Apply custom effects with caution: reserved for where they really help focus or delight; avoid them in high-use views, such as toolbar buttons or table cells, where even a small scale effect can be distracting because it shifts the very target of the gaze.
- Keep effects small and subtle, working well in small views; avoid applying them to large views (such as a whole photo), because that causes too much movement and can be hard to understand; for images, avoid effects that wash out the colors, the recommended solution is an instant highlight that fades away, revealing the real colors (applied to both photos and 3D objects, such as a plant).
- Avoid unexpected movement: the presenter himself reports falling into this mistake often. The example is the Safari tab close button: hiding it by default and only showing it when looking at the tab seems cleaner, but the eye instinctively jumps to the button as soon as it appears, making the person close the tab by accident while performing the tap gesture while still looking at the button. The solution was to make the button appear with partial opacity when looking at the tab, and only become fully visible when looking directly at the button.

Look to Scroll (scrolling with just the eyes): scrolling starts when the person looks near the edge of the scroll view (top/bottom in vertical views, sides in horizontal ones); it is not enabled by default, requiring opt-in per view. Practical recommendations: the scroll view should ideally occupy the full width or height of the window, to give generous scrolling space and clear edges; if the view is inset relative to the window, edges need to be clearly indicated; if the app uses the scroll position for parallax effects or custom animations, consider adjusting the design to scroll at normal speed, or simply not enabling Look to Scroll in those views.

Persistent controls: a UI that normally disappears on its own after a delay (such as a video's playback controls) should remain visible while the person is looking at it, and only disappear when they look back at the content. Standard video (inline, windowed or immersive) already gets this behavior for free; custom video controls need to enable it manually. The recommendation is to adopt this pattern for any auto-hide UI, not just video (the examples cited are FaceTime calls and the Mindfulness session controls).

Examples cited: tab bars (open to reveal tab names), back buttons (grow to show the previous page's title), sliders (show a "knob" inviting interaction), tooltips (appear under buttons describing their function) and the Safari navigation bar (expands to show open tabs) illustrate custom effects already used by the system; the environment icons in the Home View ("ramp" effect: scale slowly and then "open" to reveal more landscape) teach how to signal intent without opening prematurely as the gaze passes over the grid; a fictional photos app (download button that shows the file size when looked at, but requires a tap to actually download) illustrates the boundary between animation and action; the compass (shows location status) and an animated waterfall illustrate where to apply effects with focus; the Safari tab overview (tab close button) teaches the risk of unexpected movement; Music (scrolls to center the last playlist), Notes (the central note scrolls, the sidebar list does not) and Settings (does not use Look to Scroll, being mostly controls) teach the opt-in criteria; TV (list of related shows uses Look to Scroll for visual consistency with the main library) illustrates the app's internal consistency criterion.

Quotes: "It's critical to try these effects on a real device!" "Give each element at least 60 points of space."

## What's new in visionOS 26 (id: wwdc2025_317, 38.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 30 of 30 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/317/.

Central thesis: this is a panoramic "what's new" video (not a process tutorial): visionOS 26 deepens volumetrics in SwiftUI, further unites SwiftUI, RealityKit and ARKit into a single toolset, and expands games, shared experiences, immersive media, spatial web and enterprise features.

The design process described is minimal (the session is a list of features presented by three people), but the speech explicitly states the division of responsibilities between frameworks as an organizing principle: SwiftUI provides the foundation and the app's interface elements; RealityKit is "the best way to show 3D content"; ARKit delivers rich contextual information about the real environment; together, they form "a powerful toolbox" for building spatial experiences.

Principles stated and why:
- Use `depthAlignment.front` to align views in depth, improving legibility in 3D layouts composed with 2D stacks.
- Presentations (alerts, sheets, menus, popovers) must "break" (break through) to remain visible when 3D content overlaps them, because this momentary information is an essential part of the app experience.
- Interactions with virtual objects must imitate the real world: natural rotation with the hand, without requiring the developer to implement a complex set of gestures.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with numbers as spoken:
- Depth alignments and the `rotation3DLayout` modifier to rotate geometry within the layout system, communicating the rotation back to the view; several 2D SwiftUI modifiers gain a first-class 3D analog.
- "Dynamic bounds restrictions": a view modifier lets the app's content render discreetly outside the bounds of its own window/volume, increasing the sense of immersion without changing the app's declared size.
- Object Manipulation via a single-line SwiftUI modifier, or via `ManipulationComponent` in RealityKit; apps that use the Quick Look API to preview 3D models gain this support automatically.
- Unified Coordinate Conversion API: simplifies converting positions between SwiftUI, RealityKit and ARKit accessory anchors.
- Entities and their animations in RealityKit are now observable, contributing to SwiftUI updates like any other observable; SwiftUI gestures can be applied directly to entities, without going through RealityView as an intermediary; `ViewAttachmentComponent` lets you declare UI in-line alongside the rest of the RealityKit code, without needing to declare attachments in advance.
- Model3D gains support for playing, pausing, resuming and stopping animations, controlling playback time, loading and selecting variants of a USD file, and loading and selecting configurations of a .reality file.
- `realityViewSizingBehavior`: a new modifier that gives flexibility in how RealityView sizes itself relative to the 3D content inside it.
- Spatial Audio Experience API: before, every sound played via AudioToolbox or AVFoundation was spatialized from the app's first window; now each sound can be spatialized from its own window or volume, and can move from one scene to another.
- `EnvironmentBlendingComponent`: lets static real objects occlude virtual objects; this behavior is already applied automatically to pin widgets and Quick Look 3D models.
- `MeshInstancesComponent`: efficiently draws many unique variations of an asset; the example cited renders and animates 1,000 distinct objects derived from just 5 unique assets.
- Support for textures in the AV1 Image File Format, and a new `ImagePresentationComponent`.
- Foundation Models framework: direct access to the on-device large language model at the core of several Apple Intelligence features; "guided generation" generates structured output, and "tool calling" lets the model autonomously execute actions defined in the developer's code.
- Image Playground gains more control over expressions and attributes (such as hairstyle), image generation via ChatGPT, new styles (oil painting, vector) and an "Any" style to freely describe the idea.
- New SpeechAnalyzer framework for iOS, macOS and visionOS, with the SpeechTranscriber model, described as faster and more flexible than the previous generation, ideal for difficult transcription tasks such as media captioning, and running entirely on-device; already used in Audio and Call Transcription in Notes and in FaceTime Live Captions.
- Restoration and anchoring also come to widgets; the `levelOfDetail` API shows alternative widget views depending on the person's proximity; the `widgetTexture` API adjusts the widget's appearance between glass and paper.
- Hand tracking up to three times faster, without requiring additional code.

- A new memory limit allows bringing high-quality iPad games to Vision Pro via App Store Connect, taking advantage of the high-resolution screen and native game controller support with little or no additional code.
- Progressive immersion style gains a new portrait aspect ratio (in addition to the existing landscape), also available via Compositor Services for those using their own engine in Metal.
- Compositor Services gains privacy-preserving hover effects for immersive apps in Metal (configuring the tracking area's texture format, adding tracking areas to the drawable and configuring the hover effect) and "dynamic render quality" (controls the quality of rendered frames; increasing the quality expands the high-relevance area in the texture, which increases the total texture size and also the app's memory and energy use).
- macOS spatial rendering: Compositor Services and ARKit become available on the Mac, used with the new SwiftUI scene type `RemoteImmersiveSpace`.
- TabletopKit gains `CustomEquipmentState` (custom data fields on game pieces, automatically synced over the network) and `CustomActions` (custom actions defined by the developer, also synced over the network with no extra effort).
- Nearby Window Sharing: shared spatial experiences for people physically in the same room, working in existing SharePlay apps without additional code; ARKit gains support for shared world anchors.
- Spatial Persona leaves beta, with improvements to hair, skin tone, expressions and representation.
- `ImagePresentationComponent` uses on-device generative AI to display monoscopic or stereo spatial photos inside a 3D scene, optimized for real-time rendering from multiple points of view.
- System support for 180º, 360º and wide field-of-view media captured by popular third-party cameras (Canon, GoPro, Insta360), via Apple Projected Media Profile (APMP), a metadata that signals the correct projection for QuickTime and MPEG-4 files and enables high-motion detection for the viewer's comfort; visionOS automatically generates APMP metadata for selected third-party cameras.
- Reality Composer Pro allows capturing up to three times more content per recording session; the simulator now supports recording at 4K resolution.
- Safari gains the HTML `<model>` element to embed stereoscopic USDZ models directly in web pages, stylable with CSS, configurable with JavaScript and draggable into the real world via Quick Look; it also gains the developer preview "Web Backdrop", to offer custom immersive environments via HTML markup.
- Look to Scroll (scrolling with your eyes) comes to Safari, TV, Music, App Store, Notes, Mail and Quick Look, adoptable via APIs in SwiftUI and UIKit.
- Enterprise features: extended access to the main camera in the shared space (via approved entitlement), enabling camera experiences that run alongside other spatial apps, with access to a single camera or both main cameras simultaneously; ARKit's `CameraRegionProvider` delivers a stabilized feed from a region of interest, with contrast and vibrance parameters; the new "Protected Content" API blocks screen captures, recordings, AirPlay and SharePlay in a view marked as protected; "Window Follow Mode" (for apps with a licensed entitlement) makes windows automatically follow the person's position; "Return to Service" allows sharing devices between team members by wiping sensitive data between sessions, requiring configuration in Apple Business Manager; improvements to QuickStart allow importing saved Vision Pro configuration data via iCloud or from an iOS device, without needing to re-enroll hands and eyes; ARKit's `SharedCoordinateSpaceProvider` API allows co-locating people in the same physical space without depending on FaceTime/SharePlay, using the company's own infrastructure.

## Say hello to the new look of app icons (id: wwdc2025_220, 11.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/220/.

Central thesis: app icons were redesigned with a "liquid glass" material specific to icons, a grid unified across platforms and new appearance modes (light, dark, monochrome, tinted), designed to give each icon a better-built version full of identity.

The design process described: the presenter explains that the team drew inspiration from visionOS's layered icons and researched real properties of glass, combining that into the liquid glass material for icons. From there, the session describes the underlying grid system (previously designed per device, now unified into a rounded rectangle and circle) and explains how old icons are automatically adapted (masking or auto-scaling), but recommends redesigning to make better use of the new canvas.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Layers (layering): an icon has a background and one or more foreground layers; stacking shapes creates real dimensionality, instead of just suggesting depth with stencil tricks.
- Avoid realistic 3D objects and complex perspectives, because they compete with the material's own qualities; prefer a frontal view and a flatter appearance, letting the material add nuance to the artwork.
- Use dimensional shapes with a clear, non-decorative purpose (the example of the Preview magnifying glass is cited as a perspective that has a reason to exist, emphasizing the area of focus).
- Translucency in moderation ("less is more"): reducing excessive overlap allows the material's own reflective intersections and edges to shine, instead of relying on manually drawn transparency.
- Reduce static effects "built into" the artwork (drop shadows, beveled edges), because the dynamic material already applies per-layer shadow, translucency and specular highlights automatically; keeping those static effects in the source artwork competes with the material instead of complementing it.
- Avoid sharp edges and thin lines, preferring rounded corners, because that helps light travel continuously along the element's edges; use thicker strokes on elements that receive the material treatment, to preserve detail at small scale.
- In backgrounds, smooth gradients from light to dark harmonize better with the direction of the simulated light; use the standard "System Light" and "System Dark" gradients instead of pure white or black, because they guarantee contrast and serve as the ideal canvas for the material's effects; it is also recommended to use more colored backgrounds, to better distinguish between light and dark modes.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when spoken:
- 1024-pixel canvas for the rounded rectangle and circle shapes; watchOS uses a 1088-pixel canvas, which exceeds the standard rounded rectangle to make translation between platforms easier.
- The material combines edge highlights, "frostiness" and layered translucency.
- On iOS, the light effect reacts to gyroscope input: the light moves along the edge of the icon as if reflecting the environment.
- Available appearance modes: light, dark, a monochrome glass (in light or dark version), a dark tint (adds color to the foreground) and a light tint (infuses color directly into the glass); all modes work on iPhone, iPad and Mac, and even Apple Watch reflects the light mode version of the updated icon.
- On macOS, the canvas now works as a mask for the design: existing icons with a shape similar to the rounded rectangle are automatically masked or extended and receive the new material without manual work; icons with very distinctive shapes have their shadows removed and are auto-scaled to fit the canvas, but the recommendation is to redesign (the example cited is Photo Booth) to make better use of the space.
- Updated templates available for Figma, Sketch, Photoshop and Illustrator on the Apple Design Resources page.
- Instead of a simple fade, icons "materialize" as they appear and disappear by gradually modulating the curvature and "lensing" of the light.

Examples cited: Messages (background plus one foreground layer, translucency in the "bubble" and subtle shadow) teaches the simplest case of layering; Podcasts (from stencil style to stacked layers) teaches the stacking technique; Chess (from realistic 3D object to a frontal, flat view) teaches why to avoid complex perspectives; Preview (the magnifying glass) teaches the deliberate use of dimensionality; Photos (reduced overlap of the petals, updated colors to look like tinted glass) teaches "less is more" with translucency; Home (simplified, fewer layers, no static shadows and bevels) teaches removing "baked" effects to let the material do the work; Settings (rounder gears) teaches care with edges and thin lines; Contacts (the divider tabs are now part of the canvas mask) and Photo Booth (redesigned to make better use of the canvas) teach the limits of automatic adaptation on macOS.

Quotes: "Less is more." "It's about unlocking more opportunities for your creativity."

## Meet Liquid Glass (id: wwdc2025_219, 19.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 20 of 20 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/.

Central thesis: Liquid Glass is described as a new "digital metamaterial" that bends and shapes light dynamically (rather than recreating a physical material), unifying Apple's visual language across all platforms and making the interface more fluid, adaptable and expressive.

The design process described: the session is divided into three parts, each with a presenter: Chan covers the material's central dynamic properties, Shubham covers how it automatically adapts to different contexts and environments, and Bruno covers practical principles of when and how to use it. In the speech, the material is presented as built on learnings from Apple's earlier materials: the Aqua interface from Mac OS X, the real-time blurs from iOS 7, the fluidity of the iPhone X, the flexibility of the Dynamic Island and the immersive interface of visionOS.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Lensing: instead of scattering light like earlier materials, Liquid Glass bends, shapes and concentrates light in real time; this communicates presence, movement and form in an intuitive way (based on how we perceive transparent objects in the real world), creating separation between layers without hiding the content underneath.
- Elements "materialize" by gradually modulating how much light is bent, rather than simply appearing with a fade, which preserves the optical integrity of the transition.
- Visual and motion were designed as a single thing, from the foundation: the material reacts to interaction by flexing and energizing with light instantly, with a "gel-like" flexibility that communicates its transient and malleable nature, making the interface feel responsive, satisfying and alive.
- Elements can temporarily "rise" into the Liquid Glass during interaction (for example, a control), which keeps the resting state visually quiet and only brings it to life on touch.
- Continuous adaptation: each layer of the material reacts to what is behind it, shadows get stronger when text passes underneath (scrolling), the amount of tint and the dynamic range change to keep buttons legible while letting through as much of the content as possible, and the material can switch independently between light and dark appearance depending on the context.
- When growing in size, for example when a menu opens from a toolbar button, the material's characteristics change to simulate a thicker material: deeper, richer shadows, more pronounced lensing and refraction, and softer light dispersion, reinforcing the perception of depth and helping the legibility of the content inside the glass.
- On iPad and Mac, the same principles apply; in larger elements like sidebars, light from nearby colored content can subtly "leak" onto the surface, reinforcing the material's context and its elevation in the visual hierarchy, the effect is not only on the surface, the light also reflects, scatters and bleeds into the shadow, as it would physically. Sidebar and tab bar together come to be thought of as "a single navigation element" that scales fluidly as the app's canvas grows.
- Scroll edge effects work together with Liquid Glass to maintain the separation between the UI layer and the content layer, especially with content scrolling dynamically: the default style gradually dissolves the content passing underneath; when dark content passes underneath, triggering the glass to switch to its dark style, the effect applies a subtle darkening instead of a gradual fade; for accessory views pinned under a toolbar (like column headers), a "hard style" applies the effect uniformly, giving more visual separation.
- Usage rules: reserve Liquid Glass for the navigation layer that floats above the app's content (applying it, for example, to a content tableview would compete with other elements and confuse the hierarchy); always avoid stacking glass on glass, for elements placed on top of Liquid Glass, use fills, transparency and vibrancy, so that they look like a thin overlay that is part of the material, not a second layer of glass.
- Two variants, never mixed: Regular (the more versatile one, with all visual and adaptive effects, works at any size and over any content, and anything can be placed on top of it) and Clear (with no adaptive behaviors, permanently more transparent, letting the richness of the content behind show through; it needs a dimming layer to give legibility to symbols and labels, without it, legibility visibly worsens). Clear should only be used when three conditions are met together: the element is over media-rich content; the content layer is not harmed by introducing a dimming layer; and the content above is bold and bright.
- Legibility: small elements (like navbars and tabbars) constantly switch appearance (light/dark) depending on what is behind them, to ensure the material always looks good and is easily distinguishable; larger elements (menus, sidebars) also adapt to context, but do not make this light-to-dark switch, because on such a large surface area the transition would be distracting; symbols and glyphs on the glass follow the same switching behavior as the material, to maximize contrast, and this is applied automatically to all content placed on the Regular variant.
- Tinting: the new way of tinting elements generates a range of tones mapped to the brightness of the content behind it, inspired by how real colored glass changes hue, brightness and saturation depending on what is behind it, without straying too far from the intended color; this reinforces the material's physicality and also helps legibility and contrast. Tinting should be used selectively, only to give emphasis to elements and actions with a distinct functional purpose, tinting every element makes nothing stand out; to bring color to the app in general, the recommendation is to do that at the content layer, not in the material.
- Avoid unwanted intersections between content and Liquid Glass in resting states (like the app's first launch): reposition or scale the content to maintain separation, instead of letting the two layers overlap by accident.
- Accessibility features work as modifiers of the material, without sacrificing its central properties: "Reduced Transparency" makes the Liquid Glass more "frostier" (frosted) and obscures more of the content behind it; "Increased Contrast" makes elements predominantly black or white, highlighted with a contrast border; "Reduced Motion" decreases the intensity of some effects and disables any elastic property of the material. These adjustments are applied automatically to every Liquid Glass element whenever the person turns on the corresponding option in the system.

Concrete interface-building techniques: the layers mentioned include the highlights layer (highlights, which react to geometry and can respond to device motion), the shadows layer (which reacts to what is behind it), and the interaction glow (which originates under the touch point and spreads across the element and neighboring Liquid Glass elements). The video is conceptual and does not cite exact numeric values for points, pixels or milliseconds.

Examples cited: the historical lineage (Aqua, iOS 7 blurs, iPhone X, Dynamic Island, visionOS) situates where the material's learnings come from; the sidebar on iPad and Mac teaches the "leak" of light from nearby colored content; a menu opening from a toolbar button teaches the perceived change in thickness when growing in size; a window losing focus on Mac or iPad (the glass visually recedes) teaches how multiple layers adjust together to guide attention; a red "View Bag" button in a fictional food delivery app illustrates the correct use of tinting as emphasis for a primary action.

Quotes: "Liquid Glass is a new digital meta-material that dynamically bends and shapes light." "Always avoid glass on glass."

## Design interactive snippets (id: wwdc2025_281, 7.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 6 of 6 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/281/.

Central thesis: interactive snippets are compact views triggered by App Intents (in Spotlight, Siri and Shortcuts), which now support buttons and information updates, allowing quick, routine tasks to be carried out without opening the app.

The design process described: the session follows three parts, the appearance of content and layout, concise and useful interaction, and the two types of snippet (result and confirmation). The central question for choosing the snippet type is: does this intent need an action before it can show the result? If yes, it's a confirmation; if the snippet only shows a consequence or information with no pending decision, it's a result.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- Snippets always appear at the top of the screen, overlapping other content, and remain until they are confirmed, canceled or swiped away, which is why they work well for simple, routine tasks, without taking the person out of the context they're in.
- Text in snippets is larger than the system default, to draw attention to the most important information at the moment (glanceability); enough space must be given between elements so the layout doesn't feel overloaded.
- Consistent margins around the content keep the snippet organized, helping the person focus on what matters; the `ContainerRelativeShape` API ensures these margins adapt correctly across platforms and screen sizes.
- Since the text is larger, space is limited: avoid content that goes past 340 points in height, because that would require scrolling and introduce unexpected friction; keep the content concise with only the most important information, and link to the relevant screen in the app if the person might need more detail.
- Vibrant backgrounds based on the app's visual identity help the snippet stand out from being overlaid on other content, but sometimes this makes it harder to read; it's especially important to check contrast beyond the standard ratios when the snippet is viewed from a distance, increasing the contrast between content and background whenever necessary.
- Interactivity makes the snippet more "stateful" and actionable: buttons allow simple, relevant actions to be carried out directly while using the Intent, and the snippet can show updated data reflecting the app's most recent information.
- Clear visual feedback (the example uses scale and blur) when updating a piece of data confirms that the action succeeded, and this helps build trust in the App Intent for routine use.
- Even without interactivity, a snippet can animate the entrance of the app's most recent information.
- Result snippets show information that is already the outcome of a confirmation or that requires no further action, which is why the only button in the footer is "Done".
- Confirmation snippets are used when the intent needs an action before it can show the result; the button carries a clear action verb (for example, "Order"), chosen from pre-written options or customized, to make clear what the next step is.
- Dialog is what Siri speaks on behalf of the App Intent, essential for voice interaction (for example, someone using AirPods without looking at the screen). Even so, the challenge proposed is to make the snippet understandable on its own, even without the dialog visible or heard, this removes redundancy and makes the snippet more intuitive by itself.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when spoken:
- Type (font) larger than the system default to reinforce glanceability.
- Content height limit of 340 points, above which scrolling is necessary.
- `ContainerRelativeShape` API for responsive margins around the content.
- Result snippet: a single button, "Done".
- Confirmation snippet: button with an action verb (pre-written default or customized, like "Order").

Examples cited: a water intake tracking app ("add water" button, animated update with scale and blur) teaches lightweight interactivity and success feedback; an equalizer snippet (multiple buttons and presets, several pieces of content updated at the same time) teaches that a snippet can combine several related actions; an order status check snippet teaches the typical use case for a result snippet; a coffee order snippet (adjusting espresso shots, confirming with "Order", then seeing the result snippet with the order completed) teaches the full flow of confirmation followed by result.

Quotes: "Avoid including content past 340 points in height." "Challenge yourself to make your snippet understandable on its own."

## Principles of inclusive app design (id: wwdc2025_316, 21.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/316/.

Central thesis: designing with disability considered as a spectrum (not a binary condition) broadens who the app serves and is, in itself, a source of creativity and design innovation, not just a matter of compliance.

The design process described: the session is presented by two accessibility designers, Chris (who describes himself as legally blind due to a rare form of macular degeneration) and Lisa. They organize the reasoning into five categories of disability, Vision, Hearing, Motor, Speech and Cognitive, always treated as a spectrum (the practical recommendation is to remember to add the word "some": "some" vision, "some" hearing). The central concept is the "inclusion gap" (inclusion gap): the difference between what a body can do and what society expects of it, illustrated with the example of a two-story building with no elevator, where the disability arises from the lack of the elevator, not only from the person's body. From there, the presenters cite the motto of the disability community, "Nothing about us without us", as the reason to involve real people with disabilities in the process, instead of making design decisions by assumption.

The practical process proposed has four steps, to use while there's not yet direct collaboration available with people with disabilities:
1. Support multiple senses.
2. Provide customization.
3. Adopt the accessibility APIs.
4. Track the "inclusion debt" (inclusion debt).

Principles stated and the why of each:
- About one in seven people has some form of disability, and disability is described as a universal part of the human experience, most people will go through some form of it (temporary or permanent) at some point in life.
- The inclusion gap is a historical source of innovation: microphones were born because the human voice isn't loud enough for large audiences, but society expects people to speak to them; glasses were born because many people have blurry vision, but society expects them to read small text at a distance; and curb cuts were born to close the gap between wheelchair wheels and the sidewalk step, and today they also benefit cyclists, parents pushing strollers and delivery workers pushing cargo carts.
- Closing the inclusion gap, therefore, makes design better for everyone, not just for those with a specific disability.
- Supporting multiple senses (visual, auditory, tactile, voice, cognitive) in the same feature closes part of the gap, and has the side effect of making content accessible also to people without disabilities in specific situations (such as being in a quiet environment).
- Customization is necessary because supporting multiple senses doesn't cover each person's unique situation: it's necessary to allow the interface and the interaction of each sense to be personalized for each person's specific way of being.
- Adopting the accessibility APIs (VoiceOver, Switch Control, Voice Control, Larger Text) connects the app to assistive technologies that people already use day to day, the text compares this again to curb cuts: just as sidewalks are built with curb cuts for wheelchairs, apps should adopt these APIs so that people who use assistive technology can also use them.
- Tracking the inclusion debt acknowledges that the gap probably never closes for good: inclusion is a continuous journey, not a one-time task: what matters is being aware of the gap that still exists, planning how to close it, and making conscious design decisions with that in mind.

Concrete techniques and examples cited, with what each one teaches:
- Accessibility Reader (a built-in system feature) lets you read visually, listen to the content by tapping "play" without looking at the phone, or combine both with highlighting of spoken words while they are read aloud, the text explains that this combination especially helps people with cognitive disabilities, who do better reading while following along with what is spoken. It also supports customization of text size, color, "high legibility" characters in the San Francisco font, or a completely different font. And, by integrating the accessibility APIs, it also works with Switch Control, allowing you to navigate to the play button and select it without touching the screen.
- Crouton (a third-party recipe app) offers different ways to import a recipe: "From Image" and "From Camera" (good alternatives for those who cannot type), "From Text" (good for those who cannot see but can type), and a "Hands Free" mode that lets you go through the recipe steps without touching the device, useful for those who have difficulty using their hands or have hands dirty from cooking. The example teaches how to offer multiple input paths for the same task.
- Carrot Weather (a third-party weather app) lets you switch from a dense layout, with all details visible, to a simplified interface showing only what the person wants to see on screen, the example teaches information density customization, useful both for those who want full detail and for people with cognitive disabilities who need minimal information.
- Blackbox (a third-party puzzle game that uses iPhone sensors, such as the gyroscope) supports the VoiceOver API with descriptive audio labels such as "Clue. 360 dashes in a circle", combined with rich audio and haptic design, allowing blind or low vision players to solve sensor-based challenges even without seeing the screen, the example teaches that even game mechanics based on physical sensors can be designed accessibly with good sound design.

Quotes: "About one in seven people has a disability." "Nothing about us without us."

## Get to know the new design system (id: wwdc2025_356, 18.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 15 of 15 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/356/.

Central thesis: Apple's new design system, grounded in Liquid Glass, extends across visual language, information architecture and continuity between platforms, with concentricity and three types of geometric shape as a shared foundation.

The design process described: the presenter organizes the session into three areas, visual language (color, typography, concentric shapes), the structure that guides navigation and content engagement, and continuity between devices. A method principle is stated right at the start: approach the system holistically, designing with intent at every level, from the smallest control to the largest surface, always in relation to the whole, from the smallest detail to the largest surface.

Principles stated and the why of each:
- The system colors were adjusted subtly, but significantly, across the Light, Dark and Increased Contrast appearances, to work in harmony with Liquid Glass, improving hue differentiation without losing the "optimistic spirit" of Apple's colors.
- Typography was refined to be more bold and left-aligned at key moments, such as alerts and onboarding, to reinforce clarity and reading structure.
- Concentricity: aligning radii and margins around a shared center lets shapes fit comfortably into one another; views are mathematically centered when that makes optical sense, and subtly offset when it does not, to preserve visual balance.
- Three shape types build these concentric layouts: "fixed" (constant corner radius), "capsule" (radius equal to half the container's height) and "concentric" (radius calculated by subtracting the padding from the parent container's radius).
- The capsule geometry naturally supports concentricity, which is why it appears in sliders, switches, bars, buttons and the rounded corners of grouped table views; in dense desktop environments, capsule works better reserved for prominent actions, while Mini, Small and Medium controls on macOS keep using a rounded rectangle (good for compact, high-density layouts, such as inspector panels); Large controls and the new X-Large size use capsule, taking advantage of Liquid Glass to give emphasis to more spacious areas.
- It is important for the developer's own visual language and interface elements to harmonize with Liquid Glass ("play in the same musical key"), instead of conflicting with the system's rhythm and tone, using the three shape types helps maintain that alignment and makes it easier to work with Apple's APIs.
- Corners that are too "pinched" or too "flared" break the sense of balance; the usual solution is to make the shape concentric, letting the system calculate the inner radius automatically. Near device edges, the recommendation is to use capsule with extra margin on phones, and a concentric shape aligned to the window edge on iPad and Mac. A practical trick is to use a concentric shape with a fallback radius, so the component works both inside a container and on its own: the concentric value adapts when nested, and the fallback kicks in when the component is isolated.
- Liquid Glass defines a new functional layer that floats above the content, bringing structure and clarity without ever stealing focus. One of the ways this is expressed is by making the spatial relationships between surfaces clear: the Action Sheet, which used to always appear at the bottom of the screen no matter where the action came from, now originates from the very element that triggered it, serving as its visual origin; when creating custom controls, the recommendation is to apply the material directly to the control, not to its internal views.
- Liquid Glass reflects navigation focus through subtle material variations as navigation deepens or changes: a dimming layer signals modality when a sheet interrupts the main flow, making it feel like a clear, intentional space; when a task happens in parallel, Liquid Glass creates natural separation without breaking the flow; when dragging a sheet upward, the glass recedes subtly, becomes more opaque and grows smoothly in size, signaling a deeper level of engagement.
- The explicit recommendation is to clean up old bar customizations (extra backgrounds or borders added in the past just to give buttons visual weight), because in the new look these customizations become unnecessary; hierarchy should come from layout and grouping, not from decoration.
- Group bar items by function and frequency, with buttons for related actions staying together; avoid grouping a symbol with text (the example cited is "Select" next to the share icon), because that can be perceived as a single button; the primary action (such as "Done") should stay separate and tinted, a tinted blue checkmark on iOS/iPadOS, or prominent text on macOS, creating a clear, easy-to-find focal point.
- The tab bar is one of the app's most persistent parts, which is why organizing it well helps people orient themselves and navigate between sections fluidly; when content is not immediately visible, search becomes essential, which is why iOS now includes a dedicated Search tab at the bottom. Tab bars can also incorporate "accessory views" for persistent features (such as media playback controls), but actions specific to one screen (such as a checkout button) should stay with the content they support, because mixing elements from different parts of the UI blurs the hierarchy and makes it harder to distinguish what is persistent from what is contextual.
- Elements that use Liquid Glass need clear separation from the content to keep legibility, controls should sit on top of a system material, not directly on the content, otherwise contrast suffers. Scroll edge effects reinforce this boundary by replacing rigid dividers with a subtle blur, reducing visual clutter and keeping the UI legible; they are not decorative, they only clarify where the UI and the content meet, and should not be used where there are no floating UI elements.
- There are two scroll edge effect styles: "soft" (default, subtle transition, good for interactive elements with Liquid Glass, used mainly on iOS and iPadOS) and "hard" (used mainly on macOS, creates a stronger, more opaque boundary, ideal for interactive text, backgroundless controls, or pinned table headers that need extra clarity); the two styles should not be mixed or stacked with each other. Apply one scroll edge effect per view, in Split View layouts on iPad or macOS, each panel can have its own, keeping consistent height between them.
- The focus on content also reaches sidebars, which can now extend to the edge: they sit inset and are built with Liquid Glass, letting content flow behind them with the background extension effect (background extension), filling the full width while the visual remains centered, used for hero images, tinted backgrounds or any surface that should feel expansive; text and controls need to sit in a layer above to avoid unwanted visual distortion. Scroll views now extend by default underneath the sidebar, letting carousels slide naturally without interruption; the background extension effect can be applied per view individually, giving flexibility to combine areas that use it with separate scroll views that do not.
- Continuity: when someone switches devices or resizes a window, the person is not starting from zero, they are continuing the same task, the app should carry that moment forward without interruption. A layout, hierarchy or interaction decision should hold across every device where the design appears, which simplifies the design process: instead of starting over from zero for each platform, you only need to draw the app's anatomy once. On iPhone the layout is narrow and vertical (zoom); on Mac, everything unfolds into a wide, expansive canvas; iPad is the intermediate layer, the place where the design "learns to scale", connecting the iPhone's focused utility to the Mac's spacious depth.
- Content grouped intentionally should stay grouped even when the layout adapts between platforms; using the same symbols across devices preserves meaning and builds familiarity through repetition. Not every action has an obvious visual shortcut (a pencil might suggest "annotate", a checkmark might look like "confirm", making actions like Select or Edit easy to misread), when there is no clear visual shortcut, a text label is always the better choice. Bars (and now also menus) have come to rely more on symbols than on text; when several actions are closely related (for example, different ways to copy), the recommendation is not to repeat or vary the icon between them, use the symbol once to introduce the group and let the text do the rest. The Icons page of the Human Interface Guidelines was updated with a list of preferred glyphs for common actions.
- For continuity to hold across devices, components need to be structured to scale, treating variation between platforms as an expression of a shared structure, not as an exception to it. This involves defining a shared anatomy: parts of a component's layout should persist and be reused in familiar positions, visible in sidebars, table items and, now, also in menus: a popup menu on macOS and a context menu on iOS might not look alike visually, but both include a selection indicator, icon, label and accessory item. Even when the structure changes between platforms, behavior should fill that gap: components like tab bars, segmented controls and sidebars should always signal selection, navigation and state consistently, offering the same functional cues and feedback regardless of the form they take.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when they are stated: the "capsule" ratio (radius = half the container's height) and "concentric" (radius = parent container's radius minus the padding) are the only exact proportions stated; macOS control sizes cited by name are Mini, Small and Medium (rounded rectangle) and Large and X-Large, the new size (capsule). The video does not cite specific numeric point or pixel values for these measurements.

Examples cited: the Action Sheet (which now originates from the element that triggers it) teaches how Liquid Glass expresses the spatial relationship between origin and surface; Safari, cited as an app that already shows controls over system material (not directly over the content), teaches the separation rule for legibility; sidebars, table items and menus, together, teach the concept of shared anatomy between platforms; inspector panels on macOS teach the use of compact controls in a rounded rectangle for high density.

Quotes: "Think of it like playing in the same music key." "Hierarchy should be expressed through layout and grouping."

## Design widgets for visionOS (id: wwdc2025_255, 21.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 16 of 16 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/255/.

Central thesis: in visionOS, widgets become persistent three-dimensional objects in the person's physical space (wall, table, shelf), governed by four design principles: persistence, fixed size, personalization and proximity awareness.

The design process described: two presenters split the subject, Jonathan covers persistence and fixed size, Moritz covers personalization (materials and colors) and proximity awareness. The method recommendation repeated in the talk is to think of the widget as part of the room it will live in, not as something floating in isolation: when choosing the template size, the practical question is where the widget will live (a table beside the Mac, a wall as decoration), because size has a real physical presence in the person's space. Anyone who already has widgets in the iPad app has a free starting point: enabling "compatibility mode" automatically adapts the existing widget to visionOS's new spatial and visual treatment, with no extra work; building a native widget gives access to platform-specific sizes and a more in-depth visual style.

Principles stated and the why of each, organized under the four pillars cited:

Persistence: once placed, the widget stays exactly where the person positioned it, carrying across sessions, even when changing rooms or turning the device off and back on. When added through the Widgets app, it first appears "detached", floating near the library, until it is anchored to a horizontal or vertical surface, which locks its position persistently. On a horizontal surface (table, shelf), the widget tilts slightly toward the person (helping legibility) and casts a shadow that makes it look anchored in space; on a wall, it aligns level with the surface and casts a realistic shadow, like a picture frame. visionOS supports multiple instances of the same widget in the same space, which snap into a familiar grid when placed on a wall. Widgets always appear behind all virtual content, which reinforces their connection to real space, and they only anchor to physical surfaces, never to virtual environments.

Fixed size: template sizes map to real dimensions in the world, which means the size choice has a true physical presence in the person's space, so it's necessary to be intentional. Layout should follow print design or wayfinding principles, with clear hierarchy, strong typography and considered scale, for the content to remain legible across a range of distances.

Personalization: two style treatments are available, chosen by the developer. Paper is a more "grounded" style, print-like in appearance, in which the whole widget reacts to ambient light, to look solid and part of the environment. Glass is a lighter, layered style, which adds depth and visual separation between foreground and background: the foreground elements always stay in full color, unaffected by ambient light, keeping key content crisp all day, while the background can respond to light. The Glass style is made up of layers that work together: the frame (provided by the system), the background/backplate (defined by the developer), the "UI Duplicate Layer" (a darker, shadow-like version of the interface, positioned just behind the main content, adding subtle depth), the "UI Layer" (where text, glyphs and graphics live, elements that need to stay crisp and highly legible) and the "Coating Layer" (a soft reflective finish that helps the widget respond to ambient lighting). The Paper style is made up of a system frame, the content designed by the developer, and a subtle reflective coating that reacts to the surrounding light.

Still within personalization: color, wall-mounting style and frame width are also adjustable by the person. visionOS offers a set of system palettes designed to work across varied environments; the widget always starts without tint, showing the full color of the original design, until the person chooses a color through the configuration UI; the developer decides whether the widget's background participates in tinting or not (for example, to preserve a photo or illustration), but the widget's frame always receives the tint and cannot be excluded. Materials also change with the light of day: Paper darkens with the room, to stay visually integrated; Glass keeps the foreground elements bright and legible even in low light. Two wall-mounting styles are available: Elevated (default, the widget sits on the wall surface, like a frame, works well on vertical and horizontal surfaces, which is why it's always the style used on horizontal surfaces) and Recessed (creates the illusion of a cutout in the wall, with the content recessed in space, available only on vertical surfaces because the effect depends on an alignment that wouldn't work on a table; it's possible to disable Recessed or make it exclusive, but that removes the horizontal placement option).

Proximity awareness: gives the widget a powerful signal, how far away the person is, allowing information density to adapt in real time, for the widget to stay legible, relevant and at the right level of detail, whether seen from across the room or up close. visionOS defines two design thresholds: Default (seen up close) and Simplified (seen from afar); supporting both doesn't require a complete redesign, just deliberate layout adjustments, like reducing detail or changing type size, keeping the content glanceable and effective at any distance. The recommendation is to keep shared elements between the two thresholds whenever possible, for the layout to feel continuous, even though each element is rendered at a size appropriate and legible for that distance. Proximity can also influence interaction: it's possible to offer light, glanceable interactions (like a button to reveal more details of a live game); if the widget has no interaction at all, a tap simply opens the app as a shortcut.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when stated:
- Scale adjustable by the person, using the corner handle, from 75% to 125%, preserving the original layout.
- visionOS offers seven light color palettes and seven dark color palettes provided by the system.
- Five frame width options, independent of template size, ranging from thin to thick; the frame width is fixed and not customizable when the style is Recessed.
- The Music Poster widget uses the extra-large template size, giving it a familiar dimension, "like a printed art frame".

Examples cited: Music Poster (native widget, Paper style, extra-large size, with a light or dark theme derived from the album's own art, or an automatic option that adjusts the tone according to the time of day) teaches personalization of app-specific content; Weather (large format, "window-like", prioritizing clear reading of current conditions, text designed to be legible from a distance when wall-mounted, using the Recessed style to create the feeling of looking into another place) teaches how scale and mounting style reinforce the content metaphor; News (Glass style, with print-like editorial images in the background and headlines always crisp in the foreground) teaches the layering separation between background and key content; a to-do list in small template next to the Mac Virtual Display teaches size choice by context of use; Sports (shows only the essential details when seen from afar, revealing more live game information as the person gets closer) teaches proximity awareness in practice.

Quotes: "Always design with context in mind." "The rest is just spacing, taste, and knowing when to stop."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- Liquid Glass works as the unifying thread of the period: the same material (lensing, layers, concentricity) described in wwdc2025_219 reappears applied to icons (wwdc2025_220), to hover and scroll (wwdc2025_303) and to the whole architecture of the system (wwdc2025_356), suggesting that Apple treats visual change as a single system material spread across different surfaces, not as independent touch-ups per area.
- The same pattern of "reduce to let the material work" appears in different contexts: in wwdc2025_220, the recommendation is to remove static shadows and bevels from icon artwork because Liquid Glass already applies shadow and highlight dynamically; in wwdc2025_356, the recommendation is to remove custom backgrounds and borders from bars for the same reason. In both cases, simplifying what the developer draws is presented as a prerequisite for the system material to work well.
- Privacy is treated as a design constraint, not just an engineering one: in wwdc2025_303, the system applies hover outside the app's process precisely so the app never knows where the person is looking, which forces custom effects to be animation only, never an action trigger.
- SwiftUI, RealityKit and ARKit are described, in wwdc2025_274, wwdc2025_290 and wwdc2025_317, as converging toward the same system of observability, shared coordinates and animation, to the point that RealityKit entities can serve as a "data model" for SwiftUI views and vice versa, a sign that the boundary between traditional interface and spatial content is being treated as a continuity of a single system, not as two separate worlds.
- The same discipline against "unexpected states" appears in both technical and product rules: wwdc2025_274 formalizes the rule of never modifying, inside an observed closure, the very state the view reads (to avoid creating an infinite loop); wwdc2025_290 formalizes an equivalent concern at the product level, preventing restoration or relaunch from leaving the person stuck in a window with no context.
- The person's body and physical space enter as a design parameter on several fronts at the same time: wwdc2025_303 measures interaction precision in points and angular degrees according to the distance from the eye to the object; wwdc2025_255 adapts a widget's information density according to the person's physical distance from it; wwdc2025_316 treats the "inclusion gap" as the distance between what a body can do and what the design expects of it. In all three, the design ruler is not just the screen, it is the person's body in space.
- Testing on the real device is raised as non-negotiable in wwdc2025_303 ("it is impossible to experience these effects by watching a video"), which is consistent with how much the group's other videos about visionOS (wwdc2025_290, wwdc2025_255, wwdc2025_317) depend on physical reactions of the device (tilt, shadow, light, proximity) that only exist outside the screen.
- Continuity across platforms is formalized as an explicit goal in wwdc2025_356 ("design the anatomy once, apply it everywhere"), and the same pattern appears more concretely in wwdc2025_220 (unified icon grid across iOS, iPadOS, macOS and watchOS) and in wwdc2025_317 (Object Manipulation and other APIs working equivalently in SwiftUI and RealityKit).
