## Elevate your tab and sidebar experience in iPadOS (id: wwdc2024_10147, 15.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 11 of 11 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10147/.

Central thesis: in iPadOS 18, tab bar and sidebar stop being two separate navigation patterns and become the same data structure (Tab and UITab), allowing the app to switch between the two modes and offer real customization to the user.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter first justifies the change (reducing unused vertical and horizontal space, bringing the tab bar closer to other navigation controls), then shows the API that represents the app's hierarchy in a unified way (Tab/TabSection in SwiftUI, UITab/UITabGroup in UIKit), and finally addresses how this behaves on each platform (iPadOS, macOS, visionOS, tvOS). The implicit question guiding the design is "how to let the same app hierarchy description serve both a compact tab bar and a rich sidebar, without duplicating code".

Principles stated and why:
- Tabs must be consistent between iPhone and iPad, to allow the person to navigate easily regardless of the device.
- Avoid excess tabs, because "too many choices make it harder for people to locate information within your app".
- Tab bars prefer filled glyphs (filled) and sidebars prefer outline glyphs (outline); the system automatically selects the filled variant when displayed in the tab bar, so the developer must provide the symbol in outline.
- Customization (show/hide, reorder tabs) must be enabled because content-rich apps must be not just powerful, but personal.

Concrete interface-building techniques: the tab bar in iPadOS 18 moves to the top of the app, sharing space with the top bar. The sidebar, when hidden, animates back into the tab bar. The tab bar has three sections: fixed (important destinations, no customization, appear before the other items), customizable (items that can be reordered and added by drag and drop from the sidebar) and pinned/pinned (like the search button, always available at the trailing edge of the tab bar). In SwiftUI, the API uses `Tab` (with title, image and content view), `TabSection` to group, `tabViewStyle(.sidebarAdaptable)` to enable the sidebar, `TabViewCustomization` paired with `AppStorage` to persist customization, `customizationID` to take part in customization, `customizationBehavior` to lock important tabs (e.g. "Watch Now", "Library") and `defaultVisibility` to hide tabs by default. In UIKit, the equivalent API uses `UITab`, `UITabGroup`, `tabBarController.mode = .tabSidebar`, `allowsHiding`, `allowsReordering` and two `UITabBarControllerDelegate` methods to notify changes in visibility and order. Tabs also work as drop destinations (drag and drop), via the `dropDestination` modifier in SwiftUI or two `UITabBarControllerDelegate` methods in UIKit. No pixel or pt numbers are cited in the text.

By platform: on macOS Sequoia, the sidebar adopts the Mac's standard sidebar appearance, with drag and drop reordering. On visionOS 2, the tab bar appears in an ornament at the leading edge (leading) of the window for root tabs, and the system automatically selects the filled variant of the symbol. On tvOS 18, SwiftUI apps can use TabView and TabSection to adopt the new collapsible sidebar.

Examples cited: Clock app (four tabs: World Clock, Alarms, Stopwatch, Timers) illustrates the basic tab bar; Music app (Browse tab using the square.grid.2x2 symbol in outline, shown filled in the tab bar) illustrates automatic glyph variant selection; Podcasts is cited as an example of a sidebar section action (creating a new Station).

Short quotes: "Too many choices make it harder for people to locate information within your app." "Adopting the tab bar with your existing sidebar brings together the best of both worlds."

## Say hello to the next generation of CarPlay design system (id: wwdc2024_10112, 19.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10112/.

Central thesis: the next generation CarPlay design system was built to be deeply customizable by automakers, allowing each brand to create a unique, co-branded instrument cluster, while maintaining functional consistency across any hardware configuration.

The design process Apple describes: the session starts from context (larger, more numerous screens replacing the analog cluster) and shows, step by step, how an automaker designer builds a gauge: first the typography (SF variable font), then the width of the elements and the smoothness of the gauge's corners, then the arc and start/end points of the circular gauge, then color and gradients, then tick marks and numbers. The presenter emphasizes that the system's mechanics are simple, but combining the techniques makes almost everything possible, and that the final work is always a collaboration between Apple's design team and the automaker.

Principles stated and why: the design should not look "just Apple" nor "just a copy of the native system"; it should be a unique celebration of both brands, because the experience is "co-branded" by nature. The choice of variable fonts (weight, extended/condensed width and corner smoothness on a continuous scale) exists so as not to limit the designer to fixed options of "light, regular, bold".

Concrete interface-building techniques: typography via SF variable fonts (weight, width, corner smoothness on a continuous scale, not in fixed steps); circular gauges with arc, customizable start and end points (can become a full circle); solid color, multiple colors on the same gauge, and gradients, including expanding the stroke width to the center to "hero" the gradient; option of a traditional analog needle; tick marks customizable in number, length, style and position, with support for multiple sets of ticks. The modular system allows combining speedometer (always paired with a fuel or charge indicator), tachometer, power meter (for electric vehicles), hybrid gauges (showing combustion and electric engine at the same time, with the electric part highlighted by a thicker stroke), and allows swapping the transmission state indicator for a brand image or trim level. Supported functions: cruise control (marked with a dot on the gauge and target speed in text; adaptive cruise control draws a line between current and target speed when they diverge), speed limiter (simple line, different color from the cruise control dot), road signage and alternative speed units near the speedometer, low fuel/charge states (orange) and critical (red), with the threshold set by the automaker. The layout is modular: each gauge is a self-contained, resizable component; the "dynamic content" area is always reserved (it can be permanent, like a trip computer, or stacked behind non-critical gauge elements) and navigated by the steering wheel controls; it supports text and symbol notifications, interactive/dismissible, rich overlays with a vehicle image, and up to two simultaneous notifications. Custom wallpapers can be applied to the cluster and to all displays. There are no pt/px/ms numbers in the text: the customizations are described qualitatively.

Examples cited: no third-party app; the examples are internal gauge prototypes (minimalist linear in light mode vs. technical circular in dark mode) used to demonstrate how the same function (cruise control, speed limiter, tachometer) adapts to any chosen visual style.

Short quotes: "It's the best of iPhone and the best of your car together in a unified, consistent experience." "The result won't just look like Apple. And it also won't just look like a copy of the built-in system."

## Explore multiview video playback in visionOS (id: wwdc2024_10116, 14.0 min)

Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10116/.

Central thesis: multiview in AVKit/visionOS allows watching up to five video screens simultaneously in a way that is integrated with the system, but customizable by the app, and should be adopted with gradual progression and an intuitive content browser so as not to overwhelm the person.

The design process Apple describes: first the presenter demonstrates, in speech, the usage flow (watching in single screen, tapping the multiview button, opening the content browser, choosing a second video, switching the main focus, adding up to five videos, reordering by drag, removing, going back to a single screen). Then explains the key concepts behind it: each screen is an `AVPlayerViewController`; each one has an `AVExperienceController` that defines the set of possible experiences (embedded, expanded, multiview); an `AVMultiViewManager` coordinates the layout of the various controllers and keeps the content browser available (provided by the app via a `browser view controller`). Finally, it covers best practices for use.

Principles stated and why: support "graceful progression", meaning let the person decide how much they want to watch at once, because several screens at the same time can be tiring for someone who does not expect it; always start from the familiar single-screen experience and suggest multiview via the standard button. Multiview is intended for simultaneous content with primary emphasis on one piece of content, not for binging episodes of a series (in that case, AVQueuePlayer is recommended). The content browser should be intuitive: describe each item with a title and thumbnail, minimize decoration, keep thumbnails aspect-correct matching the content on tap, match accent colors with the experience, and clearly highlight which selections are already on screen, without reorganizing aggressively or crowding the limited space.

Concrete interface-building techniques: on entering multiview, the screens cast light effects ("light casting") on the surrounding environment; the original video receives main focus, with a slightly more prominent appearance and audio playback; tapping another video transfers the main focus and the audio. Some settings apply to the whole group (volume is the same regardless of focus) and others are per video (playback speed). The layout responds dynamically when dragging a video, indicating where it will fit within layout templates. Limit: up to five simultaneous video screens, with layout templates designed for comfort and visibility (no exact number of points or pixels is given). Transition events are delivered via the `AVExperienceController` delegate, allowing the app to prepare state, show/hide UI before the transition.

Examples cited: no third-party app named; the examples are generic use cases (watching multiple streamers, two simultaneous sports games, multiple camera angles of the same content) used to justify the feature's existence.

Short quotes: "Notice how the two screens cast light effects on their surroundings." "It should be intuitive, so people know what to expect and can get to watching what they want."

## Design advanced games for Apple platforms (id: wwdc2024_10085, 23.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 19 of 19 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10085/.

Central thesis: porting a high-end game to Mac, iPad and iPhone requires rethinking defaults, adaptive layout, full-screen use and legibility, and then adapting the input scheme to each method (keyboard, mouse, controller and, mainly, customized touch).

The design process Apple describes: Linda covers "designing for the device" across four fronts (getting into the game quickly, smart defaults, adaptive layout, full screen and legibility) and Dylan covers "designing for input", ending in a live case study of how to turn console controls into touch controls, removing, one by one, the limitations of a direct port.

Principles stated and why:
- Minimize the wait: download the first minutes of the game together with the app, load the rest in the background and hide the download progress, because players on Apple "expect their apps to work right out-of-the-box".
- Set defaults automatically from what the device already reports (model, resolution, paired accessories), so as not to ask the player to configure what the system already knows.
- Layout should react to different screen proportions by anchoring sections to specific edges of the screen, instead of simply scaling the whole interface, because scaling leads to controls that are less reachable and misaligned.
- Design for full screen respecting "safe areas" (avoid rounded corners, Home indicator, Dynamic Island), but using every available pixel for the game environment, since safe areas are UI positioning guides, not margins for the whole game.
- Legibility: text and controls need to be sized for the most compact screen size.
- On touch, the input is also the output: designing the control is designing part of the game itself, because the input and output surfaces are the same.
- Touch needs feedback (pressed state, sound, haptics), because touchscreens have no physical tactile feedback.

Concrete techniques with exact numbers: download around the first 15 minutes of the game together with the initial download. On iPhone and iPad, recommended type size starting at 17 points for body text and callouts; for less essential information, do not go below 11 points. On Mac, standard comfortable size starting at 13 points, with a minimum of 10 points still legible. Tap targets on iPhone and iPad: standard of 44 by 44 points; for less critical UI, can be reduced to as low as 28 points, knowing it becomes harder to hit. On Mac, click target of 28 by 28 points as standard, reducible to a minimum of 20 points for less important/infrequent controls. It is recommended to use scroll views instead of reducing type sizes when there is too much content on the screen.

Input techniques (Dylan): for movement in first/third-person games, create a virtual thumbstick that hides when not in use and whose touch area should be as wide as possible (unlike a physical thumbstick of fixed size), because the player does not physically feel where the hand is relative to the control. Incorporate the "sprint" function directly into the virtual thumbstick (instead of an additional button), taking advantage of the dynamic reach of the touch surface. For the camera, use direct touch for panning (more similar to the speed and precision of a mouse than a joystick), also with a wide area. In overhead/isometric perspective games, consider tap to move, adding inertia to camera panning to cover large distances, and using a two-finger pinch for zoom, patterns already familiar from iOS (citing Maps). Control placement: avoid edges (safe area), avoid the central region (where the character is usually located) and the area where movement/camera input occurs, leaving the thumb region for frequent/important actions and the top of the screen for less frequent actions (menus). When adapting physical control schemes (e.g. L2 to aim, R2 to attack), it may be necessary to switch sides to allow aiming and moving at the same time. Good practices for touch glyphs: use symbols that describe the action (not static console-controller glyphs), update the symbol when the control's behavior changes context, and remove the control entirely when the action is not available, so as not to accumulate noise on the screen. Feedback: every touch control needs a pressed state (darkening, highlighting, or a glow effect outside the control's boundaries, useful when the finger covers the button), plus subtle sound and haptics on touch and release.

Examples cited: no real third-party game is named in the app entry and exit text (it is the presenter's own prototype). Maps is cited as a reference for familiar gestures (pan with inertia, pinch to zoom).

Short quotes: "Players on Apple platforms expect their apps to work right out-of-the-box." "Because on touch screens, your input surface is also the output."

## Enhance your UI animations and transitions (id: wwdc2024_10145, 14.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10145/.

Central thesis: in iOS 18, SwiftUI, UIKit and AppKit share animation APIs more deeply, enabling the new zoom transition (continuous and interactive) and allowing SwiftUI.Animation-type animations to be used directly in UIKit/AppKit views, including inside continuous gestures.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter organizes the session into four layers, from the highest to the lowest: (1) new high-level transitions for navigation and presentations (zoom); (2) integration between SwiftUI and UIKit/AppKit animations; (3) how to build that bridge inside Representable types in mixed hierarchies; (4) how to animate UIViews/NSViews in response to continuous gestures while preserving velocity.

Principles stated and why:
- The zoom transition increases the sense of continuity by keeping the same UI elements visible throughout the transition (the tapped cell transforms into the arriving view).
- Fluid transitions require UIKit code to be "ready for a new transition to begin at any moment": never treat "being in transition" as a special case, just call push regardless of whether a transition is already underway, keep the minimum amount of temporary state and reset it in `viewDidAppear`/`viewDidDisappear` (or in the `will`/`didShowViewController` delegate methods), because these calls are guaranteed at the end of the transition.
- Canceling a push is conceptually different from canceling a pop: the system never cancels an interrupted push, it always converts it into a pop, so that the view controller always reaches the Appeared state and always receives the full cycle of callbacks.
- By incorporating SwiftUI (more functional than imperative), the app becomes better equipped to deal with a world in continuous change.

Concrete interface-building techniques: to adopt zoom in SwiftUI, use the `navigationTransitionStyle(.zoom)` modifier on the presented view and connect it to a source view with the same identifier and namespace; in UIKit, specify on the pushed view controller that it wants zoom and provide the source view via a closure that must capture a stable identifier (the model object, not the view directly), important when the source view can be reused (e.g. in a collection view); the same APIs work with `fullScreenCover` and `sheet`. The view controller's appearance states (Disappeared, Appearing, Appeared, Disappearing) and the corresponding callbacks (`viewWillAppear`, `isAppearing`, `didAppear`) are detailed: in a canceled pop, the view goes straight back to the Appearing state and then Appeared in the same run loop iteration; when a pop starts in the middle of a push, the push completes immediately and, in the same run loop iteration, the pop begins. To animate UIKit/AppKit with SwiftUI.Animation: it is possible to use any `SwiftUI.Animation`, including `CustomAnimation`, to animate UIKit/AppKit views; unlike the traditional UIKit API, which generates a `CAAnimation` added to the layer, the SwiftUI animation directly animates the layer's presentation values (still reflected in the presentation layer). In Representable types (e.g. `UIViewRepresentable`), the new `animate` method on `context` is used, which extracts the animation present in the `Transaction` and applies it via `UIView.animate`; if the Transaction is not animated, the closure and the completion run immediately inline. In continuous gestures (pan), SwiftUI animations preserve velocity by merging successive `.interactiveSpring` animations during the gesture and, at the end, generating a non-interactive spring that inherits the accumulated velocity, eliminating the need to manually calculate a unit initial velocity (as would be necessary in plain UIKit).

Examples cited: a fictional friendship bracelet planning app (bracelet designs), used by the presenter to illustrate zoom transition, the animation of adding a bead to the bracelet, and dragging a bead out of the box with a continuous velocity spring. No real third-party apps are cited.

Short quotes: "The push is always converted into a pop." "A single animation running across SwiftUI Views and UIViews runs perfectly in sync."

## Design great visionOS apps (id: wwdc2024_10086, 19.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10086/.

Central thesis: a great visionOS app is born from an intentional central idea built for the platform ("key moment"), embraces the immersion spectrum in varied forms, is ergonomically comfortable, and reveals a high standard of craft through extraordinary attention to interaction, sound and branding details.

The design process Apple describes: the session follows three blocks: (1) finding the app's "key moment", with three strategies (making new things possible; prototyping and iterating intensely; building something complementary to the existing app instead of porting everything); (2) embracing immersion via environmental full immersion, integration with the real physical space, and spatial sound; (3) designing for comfort (minimizing physical movement, keeping the interface cohesive in an "infinite canvas") and for "delightfulness" through craft (hover effects, subtle branding, animation, gestures that mirror real-world behavior, reduced complexity). The presenter interviews designers and developers of real apps (Loona, Super Fruit Ninja, Blackbox, Red Bull TV, DJay) to illustrate each principle in the voice of the people who built them.

Principles stated and why:
- Finding the "key moment": the app's moment optimized specifically for visionOS, because replicating an iOS app's UI in a window is "reasonable" but does not take advantage of the platform.
- Custom Environments are not relevant to every app; do not assume it is the only form of immersion.
- Integrating with the person's real physical space (sensors scan walls and surfaces) is a frequently forgotten form of immersion.
- Spatial audio is not just ambient music, it is an opportunity to guide, encourage and reward the person (parallel to how haptics work on iOS).
- Minimize physical movement because Vision Pro is used sitting or standing in unpredictable physical spaces, and the person cannot be made to walk.
- Keep the interface cohesive in the "infinite canvas": the app's content and UI must stay contained in one window (tab bars and toolbars can live outside it, but anchored to the view), to give clarity about what the app offers and make repositioning easier.
- Interactive elements need a hover effect, which gives feedback about where the person is looking.
- Keep interactive content close to the person, because objects within arm's reach are expected to be interactive.
- Reduce complexity by abstracting manual real-world tasks (e.g. syncing tempo in a DJ set) when automation does not harm the creative experience.

Concrete interface-building techniques: hover effects need a tap target of at least 60 points of space, because small targets demand excessive precision and create frustration. Material Glass is used in windows because, since there is no light/dark mode in visionOS, Glass is rendered by the system and intelligently adapts to the lighting of the person's environment. Elements with a hover effect must be distinct from read-only data (which does not need hover, at the risk of confusing the interface).

Examples cited: Mindfulness app (the flower with a gentle pulse that suggests breathing, a "key moment" possible only on visionOS); JigSpace (visualization of photorealistic 3D assets like a jet engine, deconstructible piece by piece, teaches "making new things possible"); Loona (a relaxing iOS app that, instead of porting the UI directly, prototyped intensely and arrived at a spatial puzzle game with standard tap, hold and release gestures); Lowe's Style Studio (instead of porting the feature-packed iOS app, they built a "complementary" and immersive kitchen design app, with cross-platform continuity); Sky Guide (full immersion, personal planetarium); Super Fruit Ninja (integration with the real physical space: juice splashes and drips down real walls, and the character Truffles interacts with real furniture; uses a geometric guide on the floor to indicate that walking is not needed); Blackbox (spatial sound designed to convey elasticity, "like an instrument"); PGA Tour (cohesive infinite canvas, main window + course volume, with no multiple loose windows); Red Bull TV (branding via high-quality images and a subtle flash of the brand color during loading, instead of a solid background, so as not to compromise comfort/usability); Carrot Weather (correct use of hover effect, applied only to elements with a detail view, not to read-only data); DJay by Algoriddim (direct interactivity with a turntable within arm's reach, a custom gesture of bringing the hand to the ear to preview the next track, tested extensively to avoid false positives, and abstraction of manual tempo syncing).

Short quotes: "That person isn't the problem" does not appear here (this is a quote from the user's memory, not from this video). Quotes from the video: "We just put ourselves in the mindset, we're going to build a lot of prototypes." "This required a lot of user testing."

## Create custom environments for your immersive apps in visionOS (id: wwdc2024_10087, 9.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10087/.

Central thesis: building a custom app environment for Vision Pro requires deciding the emotional function of the space first, and then keeping the geometry and textures as light and efficient as possible through a flow of blocking, decimation, texture baking and USD export to Reality Composer Pro.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter (RealityKit UX Designer) follows the steps: (1) define the function of the environment by asking about the intended emotional effect (welcoming, creative, calm, ideal work review conditions); (2) block out the scene in the DCC (Maya, Blender) with simple primitives and human figures to calibrate scale; (3) create only the necessary geometry, decimating what is far from the focus; (4) treat textures by combining procedural maps with real photography; (5) light with multiple setups (light and dark); (6) do texture baking, compacting the whole scene into a reduced number of UV maps and textures; (7) export a usdc file and import it into Reality Composer Pro, adjusting materials, maps and physical properties.

Principles stated and why: since the environments are built at real scale, using human figures helps calibrate size, because the perception of scale in the headset is different from the perception on a 2D screen, it is important to switch between the two tools when adjusting overall size and the size of individual elements. Placing assets in front of the viewing screen can create unwanted depth conflicts. It is not necessary to create geometry for areas that are not visible from the viewing area, because the experience is designed as a seated experience from an optimal spot. The geometry should be as complex as necessary to communicate the asset with sufficient detail, nothing more (which is why the tree asset, in the background of the scene, was decimated). Texture baking (capturing all the surface characteristics of 3D objects into a single image) drastically reduces the total number of textures in the scene and simplifies handling them.

Concrete interface-building techniques: use of Object Capture to scan a real asset (part of a tree) and then decimation in Blender. The concrete floor texture combines a procedural layer generated in Adobe Substance Designer (for high-frequency detail and to allow pattern repetition without losing definition) with a second layer of real photography, plus two additional layers of variation to break up the repetition. Lighting: the studio setting (for the Destination Video app) uses spotlights that uniformly light the art on the walls and the floor, plus a daytime HDRI coming in through the skylight; the environment has two modes, light and dark, switching to a nighttime HDRI and increasing the spotlight intensity in dark mode. In the case study scene, the UVs of the whole scene were repacked into six groups: Mid section, Ceiling, Floor, Front section, Rear section and Props, resulting in 6 UV maps and 6 textures. In the export flow, the only parameter changed is the name of the "Root Prim" in Blender, because that name is loaded into Reality Composer Pro. In Reality Composer Pro, the original materials are swapped for "unlit" (since the lighting was "baked"), the maps are connected manually to each material, and the "Apply Post Process" tone map is disabled to keep the look identical to the one developed in Blender; glass elements receive a physically-based material with adjustment of Roughness and Opacity.

Examples cited: the only example is the case study itself, the viewing environment for the Destination Video app (a large, open studio with a central focus on the screen), which teaches the complete flow of blocking, decimation, texturing, lighting in two modes and baking through to the final import into Reality Composer Pro.

Short quotes: "Geometry should be as complex as necessary to communicate the asset with enough detail." There is no second short quote that can be clearly isolated without paraphrasing; the text is predominantly instructional/technical and the quotable sentences tend to exceed 15 words.

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10098/.

Central thesis: in watchOS 11, Live Activities start to appear automatically in the Apple Watch Smart Stack, and the design must be highly "glanceable" (perceptible at a glance), showing only the minimum critical information, adapted to the small format and the watch's light interaction model.

The design process Apple describes: two presenters divide the topic into three parts: (1) improvements to the Smart Stack (suggested widgets that appear/disappear contextually, interactive widgets, and the arrival of Live Activities); (2) the different ways of interacting with a Live Activity on Apple Watch (automatic opening of the Smart Stack, the Smart Stack staying visible when lowering the wrist, compact view when using another app, expansion of the compact view); (3) visual guidelines for designing a good Live Activity.

Principles stated and why:
- Only show the most critical information at a time, because the smaller Apple Watch screen demands a more focused approach than the iPhone's Dynamic Island.
- Only show states that help understand progress, that require action, or that the person truly cares about (e.g. a change in a game's score).
- Use at most one control per Live Activity, because multiple controls are challenged by the small space and still need to communicate enough state.
- Live Activities must demand the right amount of attention according to how the person is interacting with the watch (wrist down vs. actively in use), the same way notifications already do.
- The best experience is the one that adopts the "Apple Watch size class" (instead of just inheriting assets from the Dynamic Island's compact state), because this allows more useful information and a more engaging design.

Concrete interface-building techniques: when a Live Activity starts on the iPhone, it automatically appears at the top of the Apple Watch Smart Stack; new in watchOS 11, the Smart Stack stays visible when the wrist is lowered, to keep seeing the time and the Live Activity together; tapping the Live Activity opens the app, or, with no app on the Watch, it expands into a system-provided full-screen view with a button to open the app on the iPhone. There is a compact view that appears at the bottom of the screen when the person is using another app and receives an update; pulling up that compact view expands it into the full widget. Without adoption of the Apple Watch size class, the system automatically generates the Live Activity by forwarding and recomposing assets from the Dynamic Island's Compact state (a lower-quality result, according to the text). It is recommended to use one of the existing design layouts (referenced in the previous year's session, "Design Widgets for the Smart Stack on Apple Watch") or, for something custom, to use standard margins and text styles to ensure glanceable legibility and visual consistency with the Smart Stack. There are no exact pt/px numbers cited in the text.

Examples cited: precipitation (a widget that appears 15 minutes before it rains and disappears when the weather clears) illustrates contextual suggested widgets; Shazam (appears when there is music playing nearby and disappears when the music stops); Home (interactive widget, allows tapping to turn on a nearby light); racing to the airport and a food delivery order are cited as examples of typical Live Activity use cases (progress and required action, respectively).

Short quotes: "A glance is all you need to get the right information, at the right time." There is no second quote that can be isolated without exceeding 15 words or losing meaning.

## Design App Intents for system experiences (id: wwdc2024_10176, 9.3 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10176/.

Central thesis: starting with iOS 18, the design guidance shifts from "only the most habitual tasks become app intents" to "anything the app does should be an app intent", as long as it is structured flexibly, readable as a sentence and with well typed parameters.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter first defines what an app intent is (a summary with app, verb and parameters) and where it appears (Spotlight, action button, squeeze, widgets, controls, Siri, Shortcuts app). Then it addresses "which functionality to expose", with negative rules (do not duplicate intents for the same task, do not tie intents to specific UI elements) and positive ones (expose live activities, playback and recording as background intents). Finally it addresses "how to structure" an app intent: parameter types, when to ask a follow-up question, when to use toggle as the default, and when to open the app as part of running the intent.

Principles stated and why:
- Avoid several app intents for the same task (e.g. one intent per reminder type); instead, structure it as a single flexible intent with the target as a parameter, because this avoids "brittle and unclear intents".
- Do not create app intents that exclusively trigger a specific UI element (tapping a cancel button, swiping a tray), because this hides the intent's real task and generates unexpected behavior; instead, represent the underlying task (e.g. saving a draft, deleting a draft).
- Parameters must keep the summary always readable as a sentence, regardless of which option is selected.
- Optional parameter is the preferred behavior: the intent does not ask follow-up questions if the parameter was not specified, and instead runs the action with the sensible default behavior (e.g. opening Notes in the folder overview if no folder was chosen); a parameter should be marked as "required" only when the intent has no use at all without that data (e.g. email search with no search text).
- An intent that toggles between exactly two states should support "toggle" as the default parameter, so as not to force the person to choose between "on" or "off" every time.
- Opening the app as part of the intent is no longer avoided by default and is now common behavior, to show the person that the intent actually changed something in the app.

Concrete interface-building techniques: parameter types include built-in parameters (for simple tasks, like adding a date to a reminder), static parameters (for a fixed set of options, like the app's tabs) and app entities as dynamic parameters (for options that change over time, like note folders). Follow-up questions (when the parameter is required) should be "concise and clear". There are two cases where the app should open as part of the intent: when the intent inherently works by opening to a specific view (it should conform to "open intent", e.g. the Clock intent that opens the stopwatch) and when the intent ends with a change in the app's UI or shows search results (e.g. creating a board in Freeform, which opens directly in the new board); this second case appears as an "open when run" toggle, on by default, which can be turned off when the intent is part of a larger shortcut that should not open every app in the flow.

Examples cited: the Finder intent in Shortcuts (basic summary structure with app, verb, parameters); a camera intent that opens in a specific mode (illustrates a static parameter while keeping the summary readable in any selected mode); a Notes intent that opens a selected folder (illustrates an optional parameter); the "search mail" intent (illustrates a required parameter); the "set flashlight" intent (illustrates toggle as the default parameter for a binary state); the Clock intent that opens the stopwatch (illustrates an inherent "open intent"); the "create freeform board" intent (illustrates opening the app at the end of execution).

Short quotes: "Anything your app does should be an app intent." "App intents should not exclusively trigger specific UI elements."

## Explore game input in visionOS (id: wwdc2024_10094, 16.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10094/.

Central thesis: the best game input in visionOS starts from system gestures, direct or indirect, because they are familiar and require no extra equipment; custom gestures and physical controls should complement, never replace, this starting point, and every input choice needs an accessible alternative.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter first distinguishes direct input (handling the virtual object directly, which can require large arm movements on the "infinite canvas") from indirect input (operating at a distance by looking and touching the fingers, allowing small movements amplified into large actions, more comfortable in long sessions). Then she goes through the technical options in order of "least cost, most familiarity": built-in system gestures, combinations of two or more system gestures, and finally fully custom gestures via ARKit hand tracking (only available in Full Space). She closes with physical devices (game controllers, keyboard, trackpad) and with accessibility.

Principles stated and why:
- Prefer system gestures because the player already knows how to use them (no need to learn anything new), they require no extra equipment, and they work in any space, from Shared Space to full immersion.
- If the game uses direct input, it is essential to include plenty of feedback (visual and audio) on every contact, because without physical haptics it is the only way to confirm that the touch "happened".
- Custom gestures should be easy to learn and remember, intuitively matched to the game's action, and used with restraint ("don't overdo custom gestures").
- Always build an input alternative for players with different abilities, because not every player can use both hands or arms.
- Automatically detecting the connection of a game controller and switching input to it is preferable to forcing the player to hunt for a switch in the settings menu.

Concrete interface-building techniques: available system gestures include look and tap two fingers of one hand (simple tap), double tap, pinch-and-hold, pinch-and-drag (both direct or indirect), and, with two hands, zoom and rotation. To make an entity "tappable" in RealityKit, it needs `InputTargetComponent` and `CollisionComponent`, with a gesture attached to the `RealityView` that contains the entities. To combine gestures, it is possible to detect two or more system gestures simultaneously or in sequence, combine gestures with keyboard modifiers, and use `SpatialEventGesture` for low-level access to events such as Tap Begins/Moves/Ends, useful when two hands manipulate two objects at the same time (the example given is selecting, moving, resizing and rotating a satellite simultaneously, using `simultaneously(with:)` for the app to listen to Drag, Magnify and Rotate at the same time). For custom gestures, full hand skeleton tracking via ARKit is required, available only in Full Space (not in Shared Space). A code example cited: check the distance between the tips of the two index fingers and the two thumbs, and recognize a circle gesture when that distance is less than 4 centimeters. Supported physical controllers include Xbox Series X and Sony PlayStation DualSense, as well as Bluetooth keyboards and trackpads, all using the same Game Controller Framework shared between iOS, iPadOS, macOS and visionOS, allowing the same code to be reused across platforms. To detect a connection, an observer is used for `GCControllerDidConnect` (and `GCControllerDidDisconnect` to go back to system gestures when the controller disappears); in visionOS 2, it is necessary to add the "game controller interaction modifier" to the `RealityView` to indicate that controller input addressed to it will be handled by the game; the `CoreHaptics` framework is cited for triggering controller vibration in sync with the action on screen.

Examples cited: Loona (spatial puzzle that uses indirect input: look to select a piece, move with the fingers, double tap to snap into place; gestures also rotate and move the puzzle, allowing play sitting or standing); Super Fruit Ninja (direct input when cutting flying fruit, with visual feedback, a lightning trail behind the hand and flying pulp, and swoosh and splatter sound); What the Golf (use of indirect pinch-and-hold to control the direction and force of a ball, freeing the gaze to aim while the hand controls the input); Blackbox (mixes system gestures with a custom gesture, taught during onboarding, to create a new bubble in the environment, used as the main way to enter and exit a level; also uses a two-finger tap, a system gesture, to pop bubbles); Synth Riders (teaches accessibility: has a "one-hand mode" option in the Comfort and Accessibility settings, for players who use only one arm); Wylde Flowers (good controller support: automatically detects and switches to the paired controller, and ensures that the whole game UI, not just gameplay, is accessible via the controller).

Short quotes: "Don't overdo custom gestures. Use them sparingly." "It's important to think about these types of accessibility use cases."

## What's new in SF Symbols 6 (id: wwdc2024_10188, 17.3 min)

Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10188/.

Central thesis: SF Symbols 6 adds three new universal animation presets (Wiggle, Rotate, Breathe) and improves Replace (with Magic Replace) and Variable Color, increasing symbol expressiveness as long as they are used with intention and without overdoing it.

The design process Apple describes: the presenter first presents each new preset in isolation with its direction/behavior logic, then demonstrates, in speech, how these presets would apply to a fictional plant-cataloging app (combining custom symbols with library symbols), then goes back to the SF Symbols app to show how to annotate custom symbols (define layers, preferred Wiggle direction, rotation anchor point) so they are ready for the new animations, and closes with the additions to the library.

Principles stated and why:
- Use animations with intention and purpose: "using too many or in the wrong context can make it feel overwhelming and distracting, drawing attention away from important content and actions."
- Choose between Breathe and Pulse by asking "what am I trying to communicate": Pulse changes only opacity (conveys ongoing activity), Breathe changes opacity and size (conveys a "living" quality, more presence).
- Magic Replace is the default behavior for Replace because it is a smart transition between symbols with related shapes (strokes can draw and erase, badges can appear/disappear/be replaced independently of the base symbol); when the symbols are not related enough, the system automatically falls back to a standard Replace, with direction still controllable by the developer.
- Symbols with Wiggle have a built-in preferred direction (the app chooses automatically), but it can be customized when the default angle does not match the symbol's visual orientation (the paper airplane example uses a custom angle of 315 degrees).

Concrete interface-building techniques with exact numbers: Wiggle can be horizontal (right/left or "forward/backward" according to reading direction), vertical (up/down), or rotational (clockwise/counterclockwise); when none of these directions matches the symbol's orientation, it is possible to set a custom angle, the example given uses 315 degrees for the paper airplane. Rotate can spin the whole symbol or, with the "By Layer" / "can Rotate" option, just a specific layer around an anchor point (the desk fan example rotates only the blades); the app has a "Snap to Points" button that automatically aligns the anchor point to the vertices of the layer's vector paths, and the anchor points need to be defined at all three weights, with the system interpolating the values between weights and scales. Variable Color now respects whether the symbol's design is "open loop" (opacity progresses from one point to another without the extremes meeting) or "closed loop" (the extremes meet), allowing continuous, smooth repetition for closed-loop symbols, given annotation. The SF Symbols 6 library has more than 800 new symbols, reaching more than 6,000 unique symbols, with expansion of the automotive category (batteries, convertibles, temperature indicators), figures representing various activities, new localized symbols (including scripts such as Greek, Cyrillic and Indic numeral systems), progress indicators, haptics, home and widgets symbols.

Examples cited: the fictional plant-cataloging app is the central example and teaches the practical application of each preset: Magic Replace to alert about weather events that affect plant health; Wiggle on a button's chevron to draw attention to the preferred action (adding a Monstera plant to the collection); Rotate on the arrows of a Live Activity to indicate a task in progress; Breathe on water droplets during a watering timer (recommendation cited in the text: a good way to water an orchid is to soak the roots for about 15 minutes), giving visual feedback that the activity is underway.

Short quotes: "Magic Replace is the new default behavior." "It's best to use animations intentionally and purposefully."

## Design interactive experiences for visionOS (id: wwdc2024_10096, 20.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10096/.

Central thesis: a successful interactive narrative experience in visionOS balances the setting that brings the story's world into the person's physical space, the interaction that gives the audience real agency without smothering the narrative, and a design thought out explicitly for the diversity of audiences, spaces and abilities.

The design process Apple describes: two presenters (design and creative) use the real case "Encounter Dinosaurs" (a free experience for Vision Pro) to walk through three blocks: (1) setting, choosing between window, volume, surrounding environment or a combination, deciding the placement of the content using resources such as scene understanding and head position, and always testing on the device because "everything changes once you're testing in device"; (2) interaction, introducing interactivity gradually (opening credits, onboarding graphics, a first small and simple character that invites contact), designing characters that react to proximity, eye contact and gestures, clearly signaling when interaction is allowed, and using spatial audio to deepen immersion and direct attention; (3) audience, designing around the real audience (not the idealized script), respecting that "not making a choice is also a choice", alternating tension and relief throughout the emotional journey, and ensuring accessible alternatives (captions, VoiceOver, audio description, Dynamic Type, and an alternative interaction system based on Dwell/VoiceOver that preserves the sense of discovery).

Principles stated and why:
- Test prototyping and ideas directly on the device as often as possible, because perception is only reliable when tested in the headset, never just imagined.
- The first moment of interaction should be small, simple and welcoming, because this is a new medium with no precedent for interaction, and early tests showed that many people found the experience passive because they did not realize they could interact.
- Interactions with characters should be designed based on how someone would interact with a comparable creature in the real world (e.g. treating a baby animal like a puppy).
- Rules for when and where interaction is allowed must be consistent throughout the whole experience, because this consistency sustains immersion; if the interaction pattern suddenly stopped working, immersion would break.
- Design around the audience, not around a fixed script: the more the team tried to be prescriptive, the more it fought against the audience's intention, and the experience became less intuitive; observing how people try to interact without prior instruction frees up more design freedom.
- Alternate moments of action and relief throughout the emotional journey, because constant interaction can also be physically tiring (not only narratively).
- Adopt as many of Vision Pro's accessibility features as possible (captions, VoiceOver, audio description, Dynamic Type) to make the experience more resonant and accessible to everyone.

Concrete interface-building techniques: use of a custom portal in RealityKit with a rectangular shape (evoking cinema and TV) to maintain a safe boundary between the audience and intimidating creatures, allowing a look into the world without full immersion; in large or outdoor spaces, the portal opens up to 4 meters wide along a plane; in smaller rooms with narrower walls, the portal wraps around the audience to maintain engagement without crossing a wall and breaking immersion; in very limited spaces (or environments like a bus or plane), the portal is positioned at a fixed distance from the person and passthrough is darkened to maintain immersion. Characters use multiple sources of spatial audio: omnidirectional emitters on the feet and tail, directional emitters at the mouth; the music is spatialized with surround mixing that draws focus before the portal opens and after it closes. The interaction rule was defined as follows: interaction only happens when the character is right at the portal's boundary or already inside the audience's space; before that point, the character can look at and track the hand, but interaction is limited. Close eye contact is the implicit signal that interaction is available; when the character's attention is diverted (by the sound of prey, for example), the audience naturally does not expect to be able to interact. The alternative interaction system (accessibility) works like a video game modal, responds to system gestures and to features such as VoiceOver or Dwell, and uses language that speaks broadly to the audience's intention (e.g. choosing "Be friendly" or "Be forceful" produces the same reaction from the creature that the equivalent physical movement would produce).

Examples cited: Encounter Dinosaurs is the only example detailed in the video (an Apple in-house production), and each element of it teaches a specific principle: the butterfly teaches how to introduce the first moment of interaction; Izzy (the baby Isisaurus) teaches how to design a "safe" character to generate trust and close contact; Raja (the adult, carnivorous Rajasaurus) teaches how to design an intimidating character with controlled curiosity, clear limits of approach and consistent signaling of the end of interaction.

Short quotes: "Everything changes once you're testing in device." "Not making a choice is also a choice."

## Create custom hover effects in visionOS (id: wwdc2024_10152, 16.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10152/.

Central thesis: visionOS 2's new Custom Hover Effect API allows composing rich, reusable hover effects from simple building blocks (content effects, effect groups, delayed effects and the CustomHoverEffect protocol), always respecting accessibility preferences such as reduce motion.

The design process Apple describes: the engineer builds, live and step by step, an "expanding button effect" for a profile button in the fictional app Destination Video, increasing complexity at each stage: first a simple scale effect; then a clip effect that expands to reveal hidden text; then notices a synchronization bug (the text only appears if the person looks exactly at the text's space) and fixes it by grouping the effects (effect groups, explicit or implicit); then adds delay (delayed effects) to prevent the button from expanding at a mere passing glance; and finally extracts the effects into reusable types (`CustomHoverEffect`) to be able to dynamically switch to a cross-fade effect when "reduce motion" is enabled.

Principles stated and why:
- Hover effects make the app responsive and give feedback about which element will be triggered when the fingers touch.
- Content effects (scale, clip, opacity) only change the view's appearance, never the layout of neighboring views.
- Effects applied to different views only activate together if they are in the same effect group, because the group is what controls which areas of the app activate an effect; without grouping, an opacity effect on text separate from the button would only activate by looking exactly at the text, not at the whole button.
- By default, hover effects are applied immediately, which is great for subtle effects that invite interaction; but almost every effect benefits from a small delay, because people are never perfectly still, and a delay prevents the effect from rapidly activating and deactivating (flickering) while someone is just looking around.
- Effects that reveal additional content should have longer delays, because they easily become distracting and should be reserved for moments when the person is actually focused on that element.
- CustomAnimation types are not supported in hover effects because they cannot be applied outside the app's process (the effects are applied by the system, outside the app's process, by privacy design, without requiring entitlements or extra extensions).
- Always consider accessibility and provide alternative effects: for people with motion sensitivity, the expansion effect can be uncomfortable, so an alternative (cross-fade) must exist when reduce motion is enabled.
- Keep static parts in a view (such as the profile button's icon) during transitions, because these anchor elements provide continuity.

Concrete interface-building techniques: content effects change opacity, transform geometry (e.g. scaleEffect) or apply clip shape, each transitioning between an inactive state and an active state when the person looks at the view. In the profile button example: first a 5% scale is applied in the active state and none in the inactive state; then a clip effect changes the `clipShape`, using the `geometry proxy` provided to the hover effect block to calculate the active size (the button's full width, revealing all the content) and the inactive size (width equal to height, forming a circle that only shows the icon), with an `anchor` parameter aligning the shape to the button's leading edge to keep the icon visible when inactive. To group effects, a `HoverEffectGroup` is used with a unique ID via `Namespace` (explicit grouping) or the `hoverEffectGroup` modifier applied to a view, which implicitly groups all the effects of that view and its subviews. Delay is applied by wrapping the effect in an `animation` modifier with a delayed animation (longer delay on activation, shorter delay on deactivation); the scaleEffect, since it gives immediate feedback, does not receive a delay, while the text's opacity effect receives the same animation as the clip effect to keep synchronization. The `CustomHoverEffect` protocol allows extracting the logic of a `hoverEffect` block into a reusable type (the text shows the creation of `FadeEffect`, with customizable active/inactive opacity values, and `ExpandEffect`); to dynamically switch between effects according to an accessibility setting (`@Environment` for `reduceMotion`), the different effects need to be wrapped in a `HoverEffect` type that type-erases their individual types; an "empty effect" is used when no transformation should occur. In the final app, when reduce motion is active, the button stops expanding and, instead, two backgrounds (a Capsule that occupies the button's full width and a circular background) cross-fade in sync with the text, each using `FadeEffect` with opposite opacity values.

Examples cited: sliders (show a "knob" to invite interaction), back buttons (grow to show the name of the previous page), tab bars (open to show labels), and Safari's navigation bar (expands to reveal tabs) are cited as system precedents that motivate the existence of the Custom Hover Effect API, but they are not broken down in detail; the example developed end to end is the profile-switching button in the fictional app Destination Video.

Short quotes: "These anchoring elements provide continuity during effect transitions." "The only way to know how effects feel is to test them out while wearing Apple Vision Pro."

## Create custom visual effects with SwiftUI (id: wwdc2024_10151, 26.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 20 of 20 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10151/.

Central thesis: custom visual effects in SwiftUI (scroll effects, mesh gradients, custom transitions, line/glyph text animation and Metal shaders) are born from iterative experimentation, and each technique must be tested over time to make sure it stays enjoyable long after the novelty wears off.

The design process Apple describes: two presenters build, live, five categories of effect, each starting from a simple, "boring" case and building up: (1) scroll effects, using `scrollTransition` and `visualEffect` to create a circular carousel and then a "looking through a window" effect (parallax); (2) mesh gradients, for rich color treatment; (3) custom transitions via the `Transition` protocol, building a "Twirl" effect incrementally (scale, opacity, blur, rotation, brightness); (4) text animation via `TextRenderer`, testing first line by line, then glyph by glyph, and finally deciding to emphasize only one specific sentence; (5) Metal shaders, building a "ripple effect" from the touch point, with a debug UI to adjust parameters experimentally.

Principles stated and why:
- When creating a new visual effect, "I'm often not sure what will work, until I start building", experimentation and "tinkering" are part of the process, they are not a deviation from it.
- Visual effects must remain enjoyable well after the novelty wears off; testing the experiments over time and in different contexts helps confirm whether the effect works or still needs improvement.
- A good transition must fit naturally into the app's broader context, without looking "tacked on" (tacked on); looking at the app holistically helps decide which transitions are appropriate.
- Debug UI (exposing parameters, drawing overlays with intermediate values) is a powerful iteration tool, because building good experiences requires a lot of trial and error, and immediate feedback speeds up iteration.
- `visualEffect` and `scrollTransition` are performant enough for use in scroll views because they calculate properties from the view's position and size efficiently.

Concrete interface-building techniques: `scrollTransition` exposes the content and a "phase", allowing rotation and offset to change according to the item's position in the scroll view (the `isIdentity` value is true when the view is fully on screen); used to create a circular carousel (rotation at the edges) and then, by changing the modifiers, a parallax effect changing the image's xOffset without changing the shape that clips it (clip); it can also be applied to a text caption below the image, making it disappear with offset to amplify the scroll's momentum. `visualEffect` exposes a content placeholder and a proxy with the view's geometric values (position, size), used for example to change the hue (hue) of items in a shopping list according to vertical position, or for offset, scale, fade and blur as the view approaches the top of the scroll view. Mesh Gradients: `MeshGradient` view defined by a grid of rows and columns (the example uses a 3 by 3 grid), with points defined as `SIMD2` float values from 0 to 1 on the X and Y axes, each point with an associated color; SwiftUI interpolates the colors between the grid points, with sharper transitions where the points are closer to each other; moving the coordinates of a point (e.g. the center point) shifts the corresponding color. Custom transitions via the `Transition` protocol: the `body` method receives `content` and `phase`, allowing scale to be conditioned (full scale when shown, half when not), opacity (fully visible/hidden), blur (to simulate the view coming in/out of focus), rotation (same continuous direction on exit, using a negative value) and brightness (extra brightness on entry, to "grab attention"). `TextRenderer` (new protocol in iOS 18) allows customizing how SwiftUI Text is drawn for an entire view tree via the `draw(layout:in:)` method, which receives a `Text.Layout` (giving access to Lines, Runs and Glyphs) and a `GraphicsContext`; to animate, three properties are added (elapsedTime, elementDuration, totalDuration) and the `Animatable` protocol is adopted, forwarding `animatableData` to `elapsedTime`; the first attempt animates line by line (opacity fade-in, blur radius reduction based on the line's typographic height, Y-axis translation via spring, starting from a position offset based on the line's descender), the second attempt iterates over `RunSlices` (the smallest layout unit, such as glyphs or embedded images) to animate glyph by glyph, and the final version uses the `TextAttribute` protocol (via the `customAttribute` modifier) to mark only one specific phrase ("Visual Effects") with an emphasis attribute, animating that phrase glyph by glyph and the rest of the text with a quick fade of 0.2 seconds. Shaders: instantiated via `ShaderLibrary`, applied with the `layerEffect` modifier, written in Metal Shading Language (not in Swift) because they run on the GPU for every pixel of the view; the `position` argument refers to the pixel's location and `layer` represents the view's content, samplable within the `maxSampleOffset` defined at instantiation; Metal types cited include `half4` (a four-component 16-bit floating-point vector, for RGBA) and `float2` (a two-component 32-bit vector, for 2D points/dimensions); shaders in SwiftUI can be used for Custom Fills and three types of effect (Color Effects, Distortion Effects, Layer Effects, with Layer Effects being the most powerful superset). The final example builds a "Ripple Effect" that distorts the view from the touch point, driven by a `ViewModifier` (`RippleModifier`) that exposes the shader's parameters, and a second `ViewModifier` (`RippleEffect`) that uses `keyframeAnimator` to animate `elapsedTime` from zero to the final duration whenever a trigger value changes; since shaders have no notion of time, this animation needs to be driven by SwiftUI.

Examples cited: no real third-party apps are cited in the video; all the examples are live builds by the two presenters (horizontal photo gallery, grocery list, Avatar View with online status, and the "Push Effect" turned into "Ripple Effect").

Short quotes: "I'm often not sure what will work, until I start building." "A good transition will fit naturally within its larger context, and not feel like it was tacked on."

## Add personality to your app through UX writing (id: wwdc2024_10140, 20.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 15 of 15 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10140/.

Central thesis: every app has a personality, expressed through voice (what does not change: the consistent expression of the brand and its values in words) and tone (how that voice adapts to each situation), and it is possible to deliberately find that voice through practical exercises.

The design process Apple describes: the two writers on the Human Interface team first define the concepts of personality, voice and tone with real Apple examples. Then they run two practical exercises with sticky notes: (1) imagine the app as a person, write free adjectives, group them by thematic similarity and distill the groups into the app voice's key attributes; (2) write a welcome screen sentence in that voice, starting from a generic sentence and refining it step by step until it sounds specific and aligned with the defined voice. Finally, a third exercise teaches how to modulate the tone: choose four qualities that describe the voice, place them on a spectrum, decide the balance needed for the situation, and write with that tone.

Principles stated and why:
- Voice is the expression of the brand and its values through words; these are the writing qualities that tend not to change. The four words that guide Apple's voice are clarity, simplicity, friendliness and help (helpfulness).
- Tone is how the voice adapts to the situation: qualities can be "turned up or down" (increased or decreased) depending on the context, without ever fully zeroing out any of them.
- There is a natural tension between simplicity and friendliness: adding an extra word to sound more friendly takes away a bit of simplicity, and vice versa; they balance each other out and help modulate the tone.
- Some qualities, such as "smartness" in the investment app example, must always be on, they are not something that gets increased or decreased by situation.
- Final best practices: do not lose usefulness in the name of voice and tone (look for the right moments for the personality to show); read the text out loud and ask whether it sounds like the best version of the app; avoid jargon (it excludes people who do not know the term); write inclusively.

Concrete interface-building techniques (writing process, not pixel measurements): the exercise of finding the voice uses free adjectives on sticky notes, grouped by theme until two or three categories emerge (investment app example: "smart" and "motivating"; children's savings app example: "kind", "fun" and "encouraging"); words that do not fit into any category (e.g. "polite", "quick-witted", "formal") do not define the voice, but can still serve as a one-off reference. The exercise of writing the welcome screen shows the evolution of a generic sentence ("Save your money") into a specific sentence aligned with the voice: for the investment app, swapping "Save" (passive verb) for "Customize" (more active) and adding audience specificity ("long-term") results in "Customize your long-term investment strategy"; for the children's app, adding "We'll help you" (reinforces kindness and encouragement) and swapping the ending for "save up to buy something you want" (more specific and playful) results in a sentence that is more "kind, fun and encouraging", and a later refinement for a tone of energy adds a more action-oriented verb, the word "really" (informal, suited to a children's audience) and an exclamation point. In the tone exercise with real Apple examples: for a celebratory fitness message (tripling the daily Move goal), friendliness is increased to the maximum (inclusion of "Three cheers!"), clarity is maintained (the reason for the reward is explicit: "You tripled your daily Move goal"), simplicity does not need to be maximal (extra words do not get in the way) and helpfulness is not a priority (the person has already earned the reward); for a "Cellular Data is Turned Off" alert (no connectivity, pending task), helpfulness and clarity are prioritized (repetition of the word "data" is deliberate to reinforce clarity), simplicity is secondary and friendliness is reduced (but never eliminated) because the priority is helping the person solve the problem; for an introduction of a new feature (Work Focus), friendliness and helpfulness come through via relatable examples, clarity comes from the feature's own name as the headline, and simplicity is secondary because the new feature needs a bit more explanation.

Examples cited: Duolingo is briefly cited as an example of an app with a personality that is "front and center" (color, the character Falstaff, illustration and the word "quest" sounding like a game, plus the exclamation point); the Apple Watch pairing screen on the iPhone ("Good morning" followed by a clear instruction and a "Start Pairing" button) teaches a clear, simple and friendly voice on a real setup screen; an achievement screen in Fitness+ ("Don't stop now!") and a high heart rate alert screen ("Your heart rate rose above 120 BPM...") are compared side by side to teach how the same Apple voice appears with a different tone (celebration vs. serious health information); the welcome screen of the Stocks app on the iPhone (headline "Welcome" followed by three bullet points, including "Market Data" with the description "View stock quotes, interactive charts, and other financial metrics") teaches the structure of a good welcome screen in the Apple voice.

Short quotes: "Every app has a personality." "Not everyone knows it, so it can make people feel left out."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- WWDC 2024 shows Apple treating visionOS as the year's most active design platform: seven of the fifteen videos in this group are dedicated exclusively to visionOS (ids wwdc2024_10116, 10086, 10087, 10094, 10096, 10152, and partially 10085/10151, which also cover multiplatform), covering everything from environment geometry to gestures, spatial sound and interactive storytelling. The recurring message is "always test on the device, never trust the screen alone" (ids 10087, 10086, 10096, 10152).
- There is an explicit pattern of unifying the same API/data structure for multiple platform contexts instead of duplicating code: Tab/UITab serves both tab bar and sidebar across four platforms (id 10147); SwiftUI.Animation now animates UIKit and AppKit directly (id 10145); the same Game Controller Framework works identically on iOS, iPadOS, macOS and visionOS (id 10094). Maintenance simplicity for the developer is treated as a design value, not just an engineering one.
- Deep customization and co-branding appear as a central theme both in third-party hardware (CarPlay, id 10112) and in content platforms (SF Symbols customizable by layer, id 10188; customizable hover effects, id 10152), suggesting a philosophy of "give the system of rules, let the partner/developer express their own brand within it".
- Several videos (ids 10085, 10096, 10098, 10188, 10151, 10152) repeat the same qualitative rule even in distinct technical contexts: use effects, animation and information with restraint and intention, because excess distracts from the content. It is a cross-cutting principle, not specific to any API.
- The group shows transparency about real iteration by third-party teams (Loona, Blackbox, Algoriddim/DJay, Red Bull, Super Fruit Ninja in id 10086 and 10094), with short interviews citing user testing as a formal part of the design process, not as optional final validation.
- Accessibility is treated as a mandatory part of design, not as an addendum: it appears explicitly in games (id 10094, one-handed mode), narrative experiences (id 10096, alternative interaction system via Dwell/VoiceOver) and visual effects (id 10152, cross-fade alternative for reduce motion).
- UX writing (id 10140) is the only video in the group dedicated entirely to text/voice, but it connects to the personality principles covered in id 10086 (an app's "key moment") and id 10188 (symbol expressiveness): Apple treats color, typography, animation, sound and words as sibling channels of the same app personality.
