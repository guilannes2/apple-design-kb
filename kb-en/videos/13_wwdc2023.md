## What's new in SF Symbols 5 (id: wwdc2023_10197, 18.9 min)

- Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10197/.
- Central thesis: SF Symbols 5 adds Animation as a new dimension of expressiveness to symbols, with configurable presets that work on any symbol, scale and rendering mode.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Thalia, Apple Design Team) places the new feature on an evolutionary timeline: monochrome symbols at the start, then Hierarchical/Palette/Multicolor in 2021, then Variable Color in 2022 and, now, Animation. She describes the decision to balance eye-catching animations with an interface that remains functional, always prioritizing the symbol's functional goal.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - The order of a symbol's layers determines how color is applied and also how it animates; that is why the layer structure was designed as part of the design, not just the color.
  - By default the animation happens "by layer" (one layer at a time, more precise choreography); it is possible to animate the whole symbol when uniform movement is wanted.
  - There is a notion of "space" in three planes (front, middle, back) to give a sense of depth without the planes being visible; symbols use these planes according to the animation's directionality.
  - Balance between eye-catching animation and functionality: animation should always prioritize the symbol's functional goal, not just be an effect.
- Concrete interface-building techniques:
  - Seven animation presets: Appear, Disappear, Bounce, Scale, Pulse, Variable Color and Replace.
  - Bounce: fast elastic movement to confirm interaction success, reinforce a concept or give a sense of energy.
  - Scale: increases or decreases size to indicate action acknowledgment or give focus; it is a "stateful" effect (stays in the scaled state until removed), unlike Bounce.
  - Pulse: changes opacity to communicate ongoing activity; it can be applied to the specific layer that best represents the idea (e.g., only the "screen" of a sharing symbol).
  - Variable Color in animation has two modes: Cumulative (highlights layers one after another while keeping the previous state) and Iterative (highlights one layer at a time, with a "reversing" option to reverse and restart the sequence).
  - Replace swaps one symbol for another to communicate a change of state/function; it supports Down/Up and Up/Up directionality, and can animate by layer (default) or as a whole symbol.
  - Drawing technique for animation: drawing with "whole shapes" and using "erase layers" (erase layers) gives more precise, fluid choreography in motion; symbols without erase layers "work well static" but lose depth and continuity when animated.
  - Expanded library: more than 700 new symbols, passing 5,000 unique symbols in total; new categories include Automotive (steering wheels, car seats), Gaming (consoles, joysticks) and electric vehicle plugs, plus new weather symbols (moonrise, moonset, rainbow).
- Examples cited (apps, screens, components) and what each example teaches:
  - Brightness slider with sun.min/sun.max: Bounce on reaching the maximum communicates that the limit was reached.
  - Hover over a folder in a list: Scale increases the symbol and keeps the state until focus changes, showing that Scale is used to indicate persistent focus.
  - Screen sharing symbol: Pulse only on the "screen" layer communicates activity in progress more precisely than pulsing the whole symbol.
  - Wi-Fi in the menu bar: Variable Color Iterative with reversing represents the continuous search for available networks.
  - Undo/redo/paste that turn into cut/copy when selecting text: Replace communicates which actions become available next.
  - Custom symbol of a toy bear (candy bear), drawn with draw/erase layers, demonstrates in practice how to prepare a custom symbol to animate with Bounce.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "SF Symbols has always aimed to make symbols more expressive." "With SF Symbols 5, symbols reach new dimensions of expressiveness."

## Principles of spatial design (id: wwdc2023_10072, 21.6 min)

- Basis: transcript and 22 of 22 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10072/.
- Central thesis: good spatial design balances familiarity with existing platforms (windows, points) with a human-centered focus, using depth, scale and immersion in a deliberate, ergonomic way.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Nathan Gitter and Amy DeDonato, Apple Design team) structure the session into four blocks, keep the app familiar, be human centered, give the app dimension, and be authentic to the platform, and illustrate each block with decisions made in their own apps (Safari, Music, Keynote, TV, Freeform, Photos, Mindfulness).
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - The glass material (glass) of windows gives contrast with the world and awareness of the surroundings, in addition to adapting to different lighting conditions.
  - Windows always turn to face the person when they move, to keep the interface readable and easy to use.
  - Points remain the unit of measurement; as the window scales with distance, points keep interfaces readable and usable at any distance.
  - Main content should stay within the field of view and centered, with landscape layouts, because the field of view is wide and it is more comfortable to look at the center.
  - Content should be positioned relative to the person's head, a little beyond arm's reach, to encourage interaction at a distance; avoid positioning it behind, too high or too low, except in immersive experiences.
  - Anchor content in the person's space, not in their view, so it does not feel "stuck" and disorienting; the person can recenter by holding the Digital Crown.
  - Depth creates hierarchy but should be subtle (easy to overdo); it needs to be reinforced with light and shadow, any object that "looks like it emits light" should illuminate what is nearby, and most objects should cast a shadow to feel integrated into the space.
  - Text should stay flat, not in 3D, because it becomes hard to read at an angle.
  - Scale is a tool for emphasis: small objects feel personal and light, large objects feel impressive; some content (e.g. shopping products) works better at real scale.
  - The immersion spectrum goes from a window in the Shared Space (next to other apps) to a Full Space (other apps hidden); it is recommended to start in the Shared Space to give the person control over the level of immersion.
  - Dimming (darkening the surroundings) is a simple way to create contrast with the content without taking the person out of their own space.
  - In immersive experiences, the person's focus needs to be guided (with movement, spatial audio, colored materials) to avoid overload, since they can look in any direction.
  - Transitions should be smooth and predictable to maintain continuity between states of the experience.
  - When blending whole scenes into the person's space, use soft edges to avoid abrupt transitions.
  - Subtle animation (water, clouds) brings a sense of "something alive" to a static scene.
  - "Do more with less": a small, subtle effect (like light reflected on the floor/ceiling) can convey the sense of a large space without rendering every real detail.
  - To maintain comfort, avoid large, fast movement: prefer fading out during movement and fading in when settling; if the person moves physically, the immersive experience should also fade.
  - Always provide a clear path in and out of the immersive experience, with short labels and recognizable symbols (expand/collapse arrows).
- The design process described for finding the app's idea: focus on a unique spatial "key moment", which can only be experienced spatially, instead of trying to spatialize everything at once.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches:
  - Music: toolbars/tab bars "push" outside the window, staying always accessible without taking up content space.
  - Safari: a navigation bar separate from the page content keeps the page in focus; the tab grid uses a wide layout aligned with the field of view, with tabs at the edges turned inward to make reading easier.
  - Keynote: multiple windows (large, distant slides, a smaller, closer presenter screen) show a valid use of more than one window; when rehearsing, the app enters Full Space, taking the person to a real scale theater.
  - TV app: a window moved over a chair stays visible during the drag to make positioning easier; when watching a movie, the video takes over the whole window and passthrough darkens (dimming); in the immersive cinema, playback controls stay small and close, but still take visual precedence over the large, distant movie.
  - Freeform: a wide creative space to see all the project content at once.
  - Mindfulness app: focus is guided to a single object through movement, spatial audio and colored materials; for deeper reflection, the flower expands and surrounds the person.
  - Photos: browsing the library stays familiar; when selecting a special photo, it grows to real scale and darkens the surroundings; panoramas take the person back to a place, with the "key moment" cited as an example of focus on a unique, memorable feature.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "The world around you is an infinite canvas for amazing new apps and games." "Do more with less."

## Design for spatial input (id: wwdc2023_10073, 20.1 min)

- Basis: transcript and 16 of 16 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10073/.
- Central thesis: the eyes are the primary mechanism for aiming at a distance and the hands (pinch gesture) the primary mechanism for selection; combined, they allow precise, comfortable interactions that other platforms cannot achieve.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Israel Pastrana Vicente and Eugene Krivoruchko, Apple Design team) organize the talk around design questions: how to make an app comfortable for the eyes, easy to aim at, responsive (feedback) and respectful of privacy; then they address the hands, covering standard gestures, custom gestures and direct touch.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - Keep the main content centered in the field of view, because looking toward the edges is more tiring.
  - Keep interactive elements at the same depth (same Z position) when switching between them, because frequently changing depth focus tires the eyes; a modal, for example, is placed at the same distance while the main view recedes.
  - Use rounded shapes (circles, pills, rounded rectangles) and avoid sharp edges or thick outlines, because the eyes naturally focus on the middle of round shapes, while sharp edges pull focus outward, reducing precision.
  - Center text and glyphs with generous padding to guide the eyes to the center of the element.
  - Respect the minimum eye target area of 60 points, combining the element's size with the spacing around it (the visual element can be smaller than 60 points as long as the spacing fills out the area).
  - Use dynamic window scaling (the app scales with distance) instead of fixed scaling, because fixed scaling shrinks the interface and the target areas as you move away, hurting eye precision.
  - System windows always stay oriented facing the viewer; custom windows should follow the same rule, because angled interfaces are hard to read and use.
  - Every interactive element should respond to gaze with a subtle "hover effect", so the person understands that their eyes are driving the interaction; the effect runs outside the app's process for privacy: the app only receives information about which element is in focus when there is an interaction gesture.
  - Prolonged "eye hover" is a sign of interest and allows more information to be revealed (tooltips, tab bars that expand to show labels, a search field that reveals "Speak to Search" when focusing the microphone).
  - Assistive feature Dwell Control: focusing on a button for a short time selects it without needing the hand gesture, showing how eye intent can replace touch.
  - Gestures should follow the language already used in the system so the person focuses on the experience, not on how to perform the interaction; custom gestures should only exist when the behavior cannot be expressed with the standard set.
  - A custom gesture needs to be easy to explain and perform, distinct from common everyday gestures (including movements used while talking), repeatable without fatigue, with a low false-activation rate, take into account people using assistive technologies, and avoid unintended meanings; it is always worth considering an alternative UI feature (fallback).
  - Using gaze direction as a signal of intent combined with the hand gesture creates more precise interactions than would be possible with the hand alone.
  - Direct touch (fingertip) is useful for close inspection, manipulating 3D objects or interactions that depend on real-world muscle memory, but it is tiring if the hands stay in the air too long; for that reason it should be reserved for cases where physical activity is central.
  - Since there is no real tactile response in direct touch, it is necessary to compensate with other types of feedback (visual, sound) for the interaction to feel reliable.
- Concrete techniques: hover effect with a subtle glow that works over any content (including photos); virtual keyboard with keys raised above the platform, hover state and glow that increases with finger proximity, quick state change on contact and spatial sound corresponding to the tap.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches:
  - Zooming into an image: the origin of the zoom is determined by where the eyes are focused at the start of the gesture, allowing the image to be navigated just by looking around and repeating the simple gesture.
  - Markup: the brush cursor follows the hand like a mouse, but when looking at the other side of the canvas and tapping, the cursor jumps to where the person is looking, speeding up coverage of a large canvas.
  - Safari brought close to the body allows direct scrolling with the finger.
  - Virtual keyboard: central example of how to compensate for the absence of touch with multiple layers of feedback.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "Great input experience is the one that you don't have to think about." "This feels really magical and 100 percent expected at the same time."

## Design spatial SharePlay experiences (id: wwdc2023_10075, 16.4 min)

- Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10075/.
- Central thesis: Spatial Personas in SharePlay enable real shared presence (eye contact, body language), and people will expect any app to fully support this feature.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Carnaven Chiu and Jay Moon, Apple Design team) describe decisions in sequence: first understand the type of experience to recreate (media, collaboration, game), then decide between window and Full Space, then choose the arrangement of people (seating template), then take care of how the interface adapts to the shared context, and finally decide when it is appropriate to leave the shared context.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - An app can share up to one window and one immersive space at a time via SharePlay.
  - Running in a window works like a communal device (TV, speaker, whiteboard, table): it allows multitasking and is clearly marked by the system as "shared" or "not shared"; it allows flows like dragging items from a private window to a public one.
  - Running in Full Space creates more immersion but prevents the simultaneous use of other apps; it is suited to games and focused media experiences, working like a "dedicated room".
  - Minimize entry friction: avoid mandatory accounts when possible, do not open extra unrelated windows, and use a "placeholder" window for those who do not yet meet the minimum requirements, revealing the activity as soon as the person is ready, exactly where the placeholder was.
  - The app's position, where people sit and where someone appears when joining are defined by one of three "Spatial Persona Templates": Side-by-side (when everyone needs to see the content well, shoulder to shoulder), Surround (content in the middle of a circle of people, like a table, each person seeing it from their own angle) and Conversational (the app stays in the background/environment, the conversation is the center; not everyone will have a good angle, so the app should not depend on everyone interacting with it).
  - The app should scale to work with 2 to 5 participants (up to four other Spatial Personas besides the person themselves).
  - The system takes care of the shared coordinate system (position, scale, orientation), but the app needs to synchronize its own animations and state changes in lockstep, otherwise gestures like pointing stop making sense for the group.
  - Spatial audio reinforces the shared context: everyone hears the same sound coming from the same direction (e.g. a lake), and a shared music window sounds like a speaker positioned in the space.
  - With shared context established, it is possible to use less UI, because people naturally perceive each other's actions (especially large physical movements) and can clarify doubts verbally.
  - Avoid complicated permission or turn-taking systems when they are not a natural part of the experience; be permissive with interactions and have a consistent, predictable way to resolve conflicts, letting the group establish its own norms.
  - Software can improve real-world physical limitations without breaking the shared context: each person can have their own volume or their own captions, while keeping the same content playing at the same time for everyone.
  - In collaboration, the main viewport (shared context) should be the same for everyone, but each person can have their own editing mode (e.g. tool color) and even open an additional personal window to focus on a part of the document.
  - Leaving the shared context for an individual Full Space is appropriate when the content has a single ideal viewing position (spatial captures, 180/360-degree panoramas); in these cases the Spatial Personas stay hidden, but each person's position remains synchronized.
  - Leaving the context is also valid for individual safety: the person can press the Digital Crown to return to passthrough at any moment, and the app should keep representing the shared context in a window, making it easier to return.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: TV app (Play button as the entry point to SharePlay); music player with two windows of the same app, allowing a track to be dragged from the private library to the public window; Head Set (shared spatial captures side by side in a window, but with the full effect only in Full Space, hiding the other people's Spatial Personas).
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "Shared context also allows us to communicate effortlessly." "Think of these moments as if each person has temporarily entered their own room."

## Design widgets for the Smart Stack on Apple Watch (id: wwdc2023_10309, 12.4 min)

- Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10309/.
- Central thesis: the Smart Stack on watchOS 10 is a dynamic and intelligently ordered way of showing widgets over the watch face, and each widget must follow standardized layouts, a color/icon identity and relevance signals to rise to the top of the stack.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Ed, Apple Design team) describes how the Smart Stack was designed to serve both those who use watch faces without complications (more personal expression) and those who use faces rich in complications (more utility), accessible with a turn of the Digital Crown.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - Visual consistency is key to a fluid reading: that's why the team created six standardized design layouts with text style classes that ensure appropriate sizes, weights and margins.
  - Widgets should show only what's necessary to ensure glanceability, because people usually spend at most around ten seconds with glanceable information on the watch.
  - It's acceptable to explore a graphically unique layout when the content doesn't fit the standards, but still using the text classes to maintain regularity and legibility.
  - Unlike complications (tinted by the color of the watch face), widgets in the Smart Stack don't need to follow the style of the face, giving more freedom to reinforce app recognition with their own background color.
  - Iconography (SF Symbol or vector icon in front of the widget) helps distinguish the type of content and complements the visual language of the widgets.
  - A "Session" is an active state with a clear beginning and end (music playing, countdown, timer, workout in progress); the system automatically generates a "Session Control" widget at the top of the stack when there's an active session, so the app's own widget should focus on complementary content (what leads to the session or what comes after) instead of duplicating the controls.
  - The Smart Stack orders widgets by relevance; designing with an eye to when the widget should rise to the top is part of content design, not just layout.
- Concrete interface-building techniques:
  - Six layouts: three lines of text (for mostly textual content), a variant with color coding by group/collection, a layout with an integrated progress bar (bar gauge) with supporting text, a layout with an integrated circular chart, a large text layout (numeric or single word), and a layout for charts over time.
  - Standard combo widget in every Smart Stack: a single layout that supports three circular complications chosen by the person (good candidates are sets that work together, such as three weather complications, or family contacts).
  - Five relevance moments to consider: an imminent date/time, a location reached, headphones detected, the person waking up or going to sleep, and the start/end of a workout.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches:
  - News uses the three-line layout for the headline and an excerpt of the article.
  - Calendar uses the color-coding layout to show the event and the color of the calendar it belongs to; it also rises in the stack when an event is in the next hour.
  - Audiobook uses the bar gauge layout for position in the book and chapter; it rises in the stack when headphones connect.
  - Activity uses the circular chart layout (Rings) complemented by text for calories, exercise minutes and stand hours; it rises in the stack at the end of a workout.
  - Calendar Month uses the large text layout.
  - Reminders uses location (specific GPS or inferred as Home/Work/School) to populate and prioritize the widget.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "People should only be spending around ten seconds max engaging with glanceable information on their Watch." "The more thoughtfully apps utilize these signals, the smarter the Stack becomes."

## Animate symbols in your app (id: wwdc2023_10258, 17.1 min)

- Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10258/.
- Central thesis: the new Symbols framework offers a unified API organized into four behaviors (discrete, indefinite, transition, content transition) to apply symbol animations in SwiftUI, UIKit and AppKit with little code.
- The design/engineering process Apple describes: the presenter (Anant, a UIKit engineer) starts from the list of seven "symbol effects" (the same names seen in the SF Symbols 5 session) and explains that, although there are many configuration options, they all fit into four behaviors, each corresponding to a Swift protocol that the effects implement; this classification determines which framework APIs work with each effect.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - Bounce has "discrete" behavior (a one-time animation), triggered by a change in a value in SwiftUI or applied directly in UIKit/AppKit, without needing to remove the effect afterward.
  - Scale has "indefinite" behavior: it changes the symbol's state and keeps it until explicitly removed (the isActive parameter in SwiftUI, or removeSymbolEffect in AppKit/UIKit).
  - Pulse and Variable Color support both indefinite and discrete behavior, depending on how they are applied (repeating or not).
  - Appear and Disappear are "indefinite" effects when they do not change the surrounding layout (the view stays in the hierarchy, only the rendering changes), and become "transition" effects when used with SwiftUI's transition modifier, actually changing the view hierarchy and, therefore, the surrounding layout.
  - Replace is a "content transition": it animates swapping one symbol image for another.
  - An effect called "Automatic" picks the most appropriate transition for that symbol.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: the symbolEffect modifier in SwiftUI (and the addSymbolEffect/removeSymbolEffect equivalents in AppKit/UIKit); a repeat count parameter to repeat a Bounce more than once; addSymbolEffect also available on UIBarButtonItem, useful for animating toolbars; UISlider already bounces automatically when the thumb reaches the ends of the track, controllable via isSymbolAnimationEnabled on UIControl/UIBarButtonItem; the symbolEffectsRemoved modifier keeps a view from inheriting effects applied to the parent view; animation-free transactions (SwiftUI) or the animated parameter (AppKit/UIKit) let you apply an initial state (for example, already scaled) without playing the animation; automatic crossfade between different variable values (e.g., Wi-Fi signal strength) happens on its own in SwiftUI, and via automatic symbol content transition in AppKit/UIKit.
- Examples cited: a pause/play button swapping via Replace inside a Button; a Wi-Fi symbol varying signal strength with automatic variable value crossfade; an image that appears/disappears inside a layout that changes (parallel universe of the layout) versus a layout that does not change.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "SF Symbols are an iconic part of Apple interfaces." "It's easy to do this in AppKit and UIKit too."

## Design dynamic Live Activities (id: wwdc2023_10194, 15.0 min)

- Basis: transcript and 19 of 19 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10194/.
- Central thesis: each context of a Live Activity (Lock Screen, StandBy, Dynamic Island) requires its own layout, compact and faithful to the app's visual identity, instead of simply replicating a notification's layout.
- The design process Apple describes: Mac Tyler covers Lock Screen and StandBy; Chan Karunamuni takes on the Dynamic Island. Both describe a progression of decisions: a unique layout per context, brand personality, space efficiency, transitions between moments, and alert and removal rules.
- Principles stated and the why of each (Lock Screen):
  - Live Activities share 14-point margins with notifications to keep a consistent visual rhythm in the list, even when using different layouts.
  - Don't replicate the notification layout: create a unique layout specific to the information displayed, prioritizing the size and scale of what is most useful at a glance.
  - Buttons should only control an essential part of the activity itself, because they take up space that could show information.
  - Use the app's own colors, iconography and typography so the Live Activity and the app appear to have the same personality; don't change the colors between light and dark mode if that breaks this visual association.
  - The background/foreground colors provided also automatically generate the dismiss button when dragging the Live Activity to remove it; it's worth checking whether this generated button looks correct.
  - Use space efficiently: reduce height by adjusting the size and position of elements, and change the height dynamically according to the amount of information available at each moment (e.g., waiting for a driver versus a ride already accepted).
  - Transitions: use a numeric content transition to count numbers up/down, and content replace to animate graphic elements and text entering/exiting; it is also possible to combine scale, opacity and position (e.g., map pins migrating smoothly); when animating positions in a list, animate only the row that is actually changing and use fade on the rest, to avoid overlap.
  - Alert (light up the screen and play the standard notification sound) only when there is an update that demands attention, visually emphasizing the information that caused the alert.
  - Remove the Live Activity from the lock screen shortly after it stops being relevant, so as not to force the person to dismiss it manually.
- Principles for StandBy: the layout is scaled 200% and must not draw anything under the sensor region; the background color is automatically extended by the system to fill the screen; elements drawn all the way to the edge get cut off when the background extends, so it is better to use a dividing line or a containing shape; in some cases it's worth removing the background and blending the layout with the device's bezel for a softer, more ambient look, also gaining more scale; use assets at a high enough resolution for the larger size; there is an automatic night mode (red tinting) that requires checking color contrast.
- Principles for the Dynamic Island (Chan): it was created as a single system layer that organically changes shape between alerts and activity indicators; each experience must be visually unique (its own identity) but still feel like "family" with the others; the inspiration is biological, with deliberate elasticity as a playful contrast with the fixed hardware. Concentricity is central: shapes must nest with uniform margins around the island's shape (trick described: blur the object and check whether the result is concentric with the outer edge); keep a concentric margin on the perimeter so as not to "poke" at the corners. Three sizes: Compact (the most common; the narrowest possible encapsulation, hugging the sensor region, filling as much content as possible, truncating or shortening units if necessary; can "tick" between multiple sessions); Expanded (on press; must capture the essence of the activity without showing too little or too much, keeping relative positioning similar to Compact for coherence, avoiding a height right on the border between a tall view and a short pill, and avoiding a "forehead" at the top that draws attention to the sensor region); Minimal (when switching between multiple sessions; must keep communicating information, not just a logo). Apps should not draw UI that points at the island or tries to interact with it, since it exists in a layer above the apps.
- Examples cited: Timer Live Activity (pause/cancel controls on the lock screen); TV (background/foreground colors shared with the app icon); ride/rideshare app (dynamic height as the wait evolves); sports game (play-by-play collapsing during the break); Nearby Friends (map pins migrating position); a cycling app entering the Dynamic Island when leaving the app, "as if the app would physically inhabit that space".
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "The Dynamic Island is designed to feel like a living organism." "It's really important to place objects and information in it in a way that stays in harmony with this shape."

## Design Shortcuts for Spotlight (id: wwdc2023_10193, 9.7 min)

- Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10193/.
- Central thesis: in iOS 17, App Shortcuts appear in Spotlight and should be designed around a few essential, habitual actions, directly actionable, predictable and personalized by the decisions already made inside the app.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Cameron, Human Interface team) splits the explanation into three steps: how to have a good shortcut idea, how to create its appearance and behavior, and how to make it discoverable in search.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - Focus on essential, habitual actions and content; it is not necessary to fill every available space, one or two shortcuts is already good (example: Clock offers Set Timer and Add Alarm, but discarded "Add City" for being less habitual and more complicated).
  - Prefer shortcuts that take action directly rather than mirroring the app's tab bar, because people already know how to use the primary navigation; the goal is to go beyond that and fulfill the app's main purpose (example: Phone calls favorites or recents directly).
  - Be predictable and personal: show what the person has already shown interest in, not new/unknown content (Podcasts suggests shows already listened to or with a new episode, but not trending content the person has never heard).
  - Reflect in the shortcuts the decisions the person has already made inside the app, helping build muscle memory about where things are (Notes: the new note button is to the right of the app icon, mirroring the position of the compose button inside the app; notes appear in reverse chronological order, except pinned ones, which come first because they were an intentional action by the person).
  - Each App Shortcut is made up of an "action" (verb, always an SF Symbol inside a circle) or an "entity" (noun, with an SF Symbol by default, but it usually makes more sense to provide a custom icon to be more visual and recognizable).
  - Titles can wrap to two lines, but should be short to avoid truncation and should never repeat the app name, which already appears in the interface.
  - Entity icons should mirror the shape of the corresponding concept inside the app (reminder lists are circles because they are circles in the app; Photos albums are squares because they are squares in the app) and carry relevant visual details (e.g., a small heart in the corner of the favorites album).
  - When searching directly for a shortcut, it is possible to show a subtitle with extra information (Photos shows the number of favorited photos inside the album).
  - Default color follows the system; if the app icon has a gradient or a strong color (like the yellow of Notes), it is possible to infuse that color into the Spotlight top hit and into the Shortcuts app screens; for apps with a dark complement color, it is possible to provide a secondary tint color for a two-tone effect.
  - Shortcut behavior: App Launch (open directly to the right screen, usually the right and simplest choice), Live Activity (ideal for simple actions with no need for continuous attention, like calling or starting a timer), or Snippet (a simple question or compact answer, which can optionally chain into a Live Activity or into opening the app; for more complex tasks, it is better to open the app).
  - Discovery: offer synonyms for the shortcut phrase (the natural phrase that also triggers the shortcut via Siri) to cover different ways of searching (example: someone searches "File Scanner" looking for the "Scan Document" shortcut); if the app is known by multiple names, offer synonyms for the app name (the Music Recognition built into iOS offers the synonym "Shazam").
- Examples cited and what they teach: Clock, Phone, Podcasts, Notes, Photos and the system's music recognition app, all used to illustrate each principle above.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "Prefer shortcuts that take action directly." "Design for people to build muscle memory."

## Make features discoverable with TipKit (id: wwdc2023_10229, 14.8 min)

- Basis: transcript and 11 of 11 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10229/.
- Central thesis: TipKit is a framework for teaching app features at the right moment, to the right audience, using short, instructional and actionable tips, avoiding in-app education that feels like spam or gets in the way of the person's task.
- The design process Apple describes: Ellie Gattozzi (Instructional Design Manager) shows the creation of a tip end to end, starting from a candidate feature (favoriting a Backyard in the fictional app Backyard Birds): identifying the feature, writing the title and message, configuring the TipsCenter, adjusting appearance and choosing between popover or in-line view. Charlie Parks (Software Engineering Manager) takes over the eligibility rules and the display/dismissal rules. Ellie returns to show the test APIs.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - A useful tip has a title with a direct action phrase and a message with benefit information or an easy-to-remember instruction, so the person understands why to use the feature and can repeat it on their own afterward.
  - TipKit should not be used for a promotional tone, error messages, notices that "something improved" with no action needed, or tips that are useful but too complicated to remember in the moment, none of these cases are educational.
  - In-app education should be focused on those who would benefit from it, avoiding getting in the way of those who are already trying to complete a task; that is why eligibility rules exist that restrict who and when sees the tip.
  - "parameter-based" rules are persistent and good for conditions based on a Swift value (example: the person being logged in); "event-based" rules require an action to occur a certain number of times before making the person eligible (example: visiting a Backyard's detail screen three times).
  - Rules can be refined with a date filter (example: three times in the last five days) and with "custom donations" using associated types, allowing filtering by a specific instance (example: a specific Backyard); attention is needed to the size of the data stored in these donations, because it affects query performance.
  - Display frequency in the TipsCenter avoids showing all tips at once: .daily (one every 24h), .hourly (one every 60 min), a custom duration, or .immediate (shows as soon as eligible, even if another tip just appeared); it is possible to ignore the global frequency on a specific tip with .ignoresDisplayFrequency.
  - A tip should exit the scene as soon as it stops being useful: invalidate(.userPerformedAction) when the person performs the taught action; .maxDisplayCount limits how many times the tip appears without action; manual .invalidate() covers any other app-specific criterion.
  - TipKit syncs tip status via iCloud across devices, so as not to repeat the same education on each of the person's devices.
  - Test APIs (.showAllTips, .showTips with specific IDs, .hideTips, .hideAllTips, .resetDatastore) exist to allow inspecting and testing tips without needing to satisfy the eligibility rules; the same options also exist as launch arguments for the project.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: two presentation types, popover view (points to a specific button or element without changing the current screen; it is the only mode used on tvOS) and in-line view (temporarily adjusts the app's surrounding UI, without blocking anything); in both, the tip should stay close to the relevant element; visual customization includes text color aligned with the app, an icon related to the feature, an action button that leads directly to the relevant settings, or a link to an additional resource such as an onboarding flow.
- Examples cited: the tip for favoriting a Backyard in the Backyard Birds app, used as the throughline of the entire session, including the star icon chosen to reinforce the association with "favoriting".
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "Useful tips have direct action phrases as titles." "Discovering a new feature can invoke a feeling of surprise and delight."

## Explore immersive sound design (id: wwdc2023_10271, 14.6 min)

- Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10271/.
- Central thesis: spatial audio on visionOS should reproduce how sound behaves in the real world, reinforcing presence, giving reliable interaction feedback and creating immersion without distracting.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Danielle Price, sound designer on the Design team) opens with an everyday example (locating a lost iPhone via Find My through the Apple Watch, guided by direction and volume) to argue that every human being already has some intuition for spatial audio. From there, she explains how the system recreates this behavior and goes through examples of interaction sound, UI sound and the composition of immersive environment soundtracks.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - The device adapts reverberation to the size and materials of the person's physical space, so the sound feels "really in the room"; spatial audio sources sound closer or farther depending on where they are positioned.
  - Since there is no real physical touch in eye and hand interactions, subtle sounds on each interaction give a sense of familiarity and confidence (example: the sound of each key on the virtual keyboard comes from that key's position).
  - When a sound plays frequently, it is worth subtly randomizing pitch and amplitude, to feel more natural and less "static" in fast sequences of repetition.
  - A good UI sound needs to be subtle and give only the necessary feedback; the team tested (and discarded) approaches that were too literal for Photos, such as the sound of a window opening or projector slides advancing, because they did not fit the app's visual aesthetic; they opted for subtler sounds, aligned with the rest of the system sounds and synced to the timing of the transitions.
  - In fully immersive environments, there are two types of sound: "spatial audio sources" (point elements like birds and crickets, generally in the foreground/midground, but that can be positioned anywhere) and "ambient background audio" (general surround ambience, in a continuous loop all around, that should not be perceptible as repetition).
  - Real field recordings can surprise you: in the case of Mount Hood, a large water drainage system masked the desired natural sounds; the design decision was to curate the best possible version of reality, not necessarily the most literal one, when the real sound is not pleasant.
  - Practical mixing: when a sound feels "too loud and too close", the solution is to reduce the volume by a few decibels and push the sound's position farther away; to vary repeated sounds (like frogs), alternate between multiple recordings and positions, in addition to randomizing playback timing.
  - Even in scenes that are not fully immersive and that take place in the person's real room (example: the onboarding "hello" animation), it is still worth using a spatial track combining foreground and ambient background to fill the space with sound.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: using highly sensitive directional microphones to capture specific sounds; microphones that record ambient immersive audio throughout the day and night, seeking locations with little noise pollution; mixing that combines point sources with surround ambience; randomizing pitch, amplitude, position and timing as a recurring technique to avoid perceptible repetition.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Find My playing in different spaces (demonstrates reverberation adapted to the environment); the virtual keyboard (positional sound per key); Photos (evolution of the selection/2D-3D transition sound); the Mount Hood environment (the complete process of recording, curation and mixing, including the drainage system problem); the onboarding opening animation ("hello").
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "We all have some expertise in spatial audio just from being human." "Feel free to recreate and curate the best of reality."

## Meet watchOS 10 (id: wwdc2023_10026, 9.9 min)

- Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10026/.
- Central thesis: watchOS 10 is described as the most significant change since the launch of Apple Watch, with an entirely new interface organized around widgets in the Smart Stack and consistent navigation centered on the Digital Crown.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Taylor, Apple Design Team) situates the change in the product's history (launch in 2014, evolution to larger screens and more capabilities) and describes the team's process as "rigorous and methodical", centered on clear design principles applied consistently throughout the system.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - Apple Watch apps should be focused and highly specialized in the utility they offer, because the device is used for short interactions.
  - Each screen should optimize for glanceability and short interaction time.
  - Controls and navigation should be clear and consistent across apps.
  - A grid system based on the curvature of the screen mathematically defines the size and position of controls, labels and content, bringing layout consistency across apps; there are three fundamental layouts, Dial, Infographic and List, all automatically adapted to the different Apple Watch sizes.
  - Background content (color, gradient or animation) should serve a utility role and help the person understand where they are or recognize the app, not just be a visual adornment (example: in Activity, the background of each Infographic view matches the color of the ring in focus).
  - Materials with translucency establish visual hierarchy and help understand the position within the system or the app, maximizing space for content; the system's semantic colors automatically adjust contrast over vibrant backgrounds.
  - The Digital Crown is used both to navigate between apps and within them; vertical pagination (the new standard) is preferred over horizontal pagination, considered harder to navigate on the watch.
  - It is recommended to limit the content of a page to one screen height, so that each page has a clear, distinct purpose and the result is more glanceable; when it is necessary to exceed that height, prefer placing the scroll view only after fixed-height pages.
  - Elements that exist on multiple pages of the same app (such as the Activity rings) should be animated via scale, position and information density between pages, instead of duplicating instances, this "object permanence" serves as a visual anchor during navigation.
  - Source Lists (new standard, via Split Views): apps that navigate between multiple entities of the same type (such as several locations in World Clock) open directly into the detail view, and the person switches between entities with a button in the upper left corner.
  - Traditional hierarchical navigation is still supported, but should be used sparingly, only when the content does not fit more concisely in a two-level design (Source List) or in distinct vertical pages (appropriate examples: Settings and Mail).
- Concrete interface-building techniques: grid derived from screen curvature available in the Apple Design Resources; new hierarchical navigation animation that makes clear which view the person is navigating to; use of the Digital Crown to advance the time displayed in World Clock (inspecting data) in addition to navigating lists and pages.

## Create animated symbols (id: wwdc2023_10257, 18.1 min)

- Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10257/.
- Central thesis: the SF Symbols app offers the tools to test the new animations, prepare custom symbols for motion, and, with the new "Symbol components" feature, combine your own artwork with the system's standardized behavior and artwork so the result looks native.
- The design process Apple describes: the presenter (Paul, from the SF Symbols app team) uses a personal example (a fictional app for practicing Rubik's cubes) to walk through, step by step, how to test animations in the app's inspector, prepare an old custom symbol for animation, group layers to control how much moves at once, and finally combine the symbol with system "components".
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - The animation inspector (a new tab in the app's sidebar) shows the label "Automatic" while the default settings are in use, and switches to "Modified" as soon as something is changed, making clear when you're outside the default behavior; it's possible to copy the effect's name in dot-syntax to use directly in code.
  - Variable Color already works automatically on a custom symbol that already has the right layers, with no extra work.
  - Pulse by default pulses the whole symbol, unless specific layers are marked to pulse individually in the annotation list, a decision that should reflect which part of the symbol actually represents the idea of ongoing activity.
  - Symbols exported from earlier versions of the app (v4 or compatible with Xcode 14) have no motion information and always animate as a whole symbol; in version 5 of the app, by default each layer moves independently when using "By Layer", which can be too much motion for the symbol.
  - The "layer groups" feature resolves the excess motion: layers inside a group keep their own annotations but move together; the decision of which layers to group should reflect how the parts actually relate in the real world (in the Rubik's cube example, all the cube's layers form a single group, because the faces of a cube do not move separately nor sit at different depths).
  - Consistency with the system is a central value of SF Symbols: custom symbols should try to reproduce reusable visual treatments that already exist in the library (circular enclosures, slashes, badges) to gain the same instant familiarity as native symbols.
  - "Symbol components" solve this work automatically: combining a custom symbol with a component (enclosure, badge, slash) already delivers correct annotations ready to go, the right color in multicolor, slash animation separate from the base symbol, the correct primary layer in hierarchical mode with correct erase in monochrome, and preservation of variable color settings already made; it's still possible to manually adjust a badge's position or a slash's length.
  - Components depend on "variable templates": three drawings are enough (Ultralight, Regular, Black at the Small scale) for the system to generate the remaining 24 variations by combining bezier paths that are compatible with each other; when encapsulating a symbol, the system automatically applies "weight compensation", increasing the symbol's weight and thickness as it shrinks inside the enclosure, so it stays visually consistent both with the larger version without encapsulation and with the circle around it.
  - Important restrictions: components only work with symbols based on variable templates; individual overrides of specific variants are ignored when combined with a component; and it's not possible to export an editable template back from a symbol created via components, since the system takes over the drawing from that point on.
  - Compatibility with older platform versions is part of the export process: a new "wireframe" style in the editable template helps separate the drawing phase from the annotation phase (marking what draws and what erases); a Compatibility selector when exporting delivers a simpler template (without motion optimization) for SF Symbols 3 (iOS 15/macOS Monterey), and a static template for SF Symbols 2 (iOS 14/macOS Big Sur), since variable templates only exist starting from version 3; customizable monochrome annotation is only available starting from version 4; when exporting to Xcode, it's enough to indicate the target Xcode version and the app chooses the right file format.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: more than 40 components available in the app; gallery with simultaneous preview at the three weights (Small + Ultralight/Regular/Black); "Override" checkbox to individually adjust a weight when there are overlaps in an enclosure or a need for different alignment.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: 3x3 Rubik's cube symbol (variable color ready, layer grouping, combination with slash, badge.plus, circle and circle.fill components); system video symbol as a reference for consistent circular treatment.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "Symbols are consistent... symbols are customizable... and of course, symbols are expressive." "Getting 27 symbols for the price of 3 is a pretty great deal."

## Design and build apps for watchOS 10 (id: wwdc2023_10138, 19.3 min)

- Basis: transcript and 15 of 15 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10138/.
- Central thesis: the watchOS 10 redesign rests on four goals (timely information, focused content, celebrating the shape of the screen with full-screen color/image, consistent navigation) and is made possible by direct evolution of the SwiftUI APIs (NavigationSplitView, TabView with vertical paging, NavigationStack, new materials and toolbars).
- The design process Apple describes: Jennifer Patton (designer) and Matthew Koonce (engineering manager for SwiftUI on watchOS) alternate the presentation between design decision and corresponding API: first the principles, then the three ways to structure navigation, then the grid layout system, and finally color and materials, always showing the pair "why the team decided this" and "how to do this in SwiftUI".
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - The first design question for Apple Watch is "what information or experience is most relevant that the app can offer the person at that moment" (example: Weather opens directly to the local weather for the day, and the Digital Crown advances to the rest of the day and the week).
  - This is described as the "Apple Watch Moment": narrowing the focus of the experience to what is relevant when the person raises their wrist; watch apps should be highly specialized for brief interactions.
  - A practical test of focus: "if you had ten seconds of someone's attention, what information would you show?" (example: News on the watch focuses only on the five main headlines, unlike the multi-tab, multi-level app on iPhone).
  - Interactions should be anchored on the Digital Crown as a way to reach and navigate through apps, scroll, page and make precise adjustments, but always with a touch backup.
  - When designing an app, it is worth thinking about which information would make the best Smart Stack widget, and designing the app's architecture around these relevant and timely experiences.
  - NavigationSplitView is the right choice when there is a source list with associated detail views (example: list of cities in Weather, or list of stocks and their details); the app should open directly into the detail view, using location, recency, frequency or another intent signal to decide which detail to show first; the detail view should be so unmistakable at first glance that it does not need a title.
  - TabView with the new "vertical page" style allows a single tab to expand and resize as needed, supporting localized text and large type sizes without breaking a design meant for a single screen; it automatically detects scrollable content and expands to accommodate it; it is possible to animate elements based on the TabView's selection value, including using matched geometry effect to migrate an element (such as the Activity rings) between the TabView and the toolbar.
  - NavigationStack remains the clearest way to take the person into and out of a view hierarchy (used in Workout, Calendar, Music); use a large title only on the first view, never on subviews that already have a back button.
  - The choice among the three navigation patterns should aim to fulfill the app's "Apple Watch Moment" in the smallest possible number of interactions.
  - The layout grid system is mathematically derived from the curvature of the active screen, defining three fundamental layouts, Dial-based (dense, good for weather and other glanceable data, allows up to four corner controls without obscuring the content), Infographic (ideal for charts and metrics with a text block) and List, all automatically adapted to the different Apple Watch sizes; the grids are available in Apple Design Resources.
  - One of the design goals of watchOS 10 is to celebrate the shape and fidelity of the screen with color and full-screen image, giving people "a sense of place" within the view hierarchy.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: scene padding to get the correct insets for a dial view; new toolbar placement "top bar trailing" (the clock shifts and centers when this button is used); Now Playing uses "top bar leading/trailing" placements and the bottom bar, with a control size modifier to make the pause button bigger and more prominent, without needing extra manual padding; four different full-screen background materials with blur (Ultra Thin, Thin, Regular, Thick); "container background" modifier for a tintable full-screen background gradient with an accent color (example: Activity uses the Move/Exercise/Stand colors to differentiate three tabs with similar layout); background color also communicates tone (calm blue in Sleep), time of day (solar gradients in World Clock) or state change (Timers: black while counting, vibrant orange when finished); vibrant fill materials for controls and list cells; vibrant text at four levels of prominence, Primary, Secondary, Tertiary and Quaternary; vibrant versions of all the system's semantic colors ensure legibility over full-screen backgrounds; sheets throughout the system now use a thin full-screen material, giving additional context about where the person is in the app; the navigation bar gained a "variable blur" that creates a smooth transition as the content scrolls underneath it.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Weather (progressive focus from the day to the week); News (five headlines versus the multi-tab app on iPhone); Heart Rate (finite data first, rest of the screen for animation); Activity (Smart Stack, vertical TabView, matched geometry effect for the rings); Noise app (complete final example, showing container background with a green gradient, unfilled bars/labels in vibrant secondary style, filled bars and other text in primary style).

## Design for spatial user interfaces (id: wwdc2023_10076, 22.7 min)

- Basis: transcript and 19 of 19 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10076/.
- Central thesis: Apple's spatial visual language maintains consistency and familiarity with existing platforms while evolving specific elements (three-dimensional icons, glass material, heavier typography, vibrancy, ornaments) to work in an immersive environment guided by eyes and hands.
- The design process Apple describes: Miquel Estany Rodriguez covers UI fundamentals (icons, materials, typography, vibrancy); Lorena Pazmino covers positioning ergonomics, target size and focus feedback, and then the two go together through the system components (window, tab bar, sidebar, ornaments, menus, popovers, sheets), treating each one as a translation of what already exists in iOS.
- Principles stated and the why of each:
  - App icons can have up to three square layers of 1024 by 1024 pixels (one background and up to two foreground, with transparent background); all layers are cut by a circular mask, and a "glass layer" is applied automatically, adding depth, specular highlights and shadows; graphics should stay centered (so they don't look off center when the icon expands) and should avoid large semitransparent areas, which blend with the cast shadow.
  - The glass material (glass) was created because apps can be opened under very different lighting conditions (bright room, small space, nighttime setting); it dynamically adapts contrast and color balance to the environment, giving the feeling of being part of the physical space; so solid colors should be avoided in windows, since many opaque windows together look heavy and constrictive.
  - System vibrant materials have different roles: darker to separate sections (like a sidebar) or increase contrast in input fields, lighter to draw attention to interactive elements like buttons; stacking light materials on top of each other hurts legibility and should be avoided.
  - The platform doesn't have a distinct light or dark appearance like iOS/macOS: instead, the glass and the UI adapt naturally to what's behind them, day or night.
  - Typography uses the same semantic names as other platforms (compatibility), but with slightly heavier weights to improve contrast over vibrant materials: body text uses "medium" weight (instead of "regular" on iOS) and titles use "bold" (instead of "semibold"); tracking was also slightly increased; there are two new font styles for wide editorial layouts (Extra Large Title 1 cited, used in Arcade); small or light custom fonts remain hard to read even in large windows, so it's recommended to increase the weight or use system typography, optimized for legibility.
  - Vibrancy lightens foreground content by pulling light and color from what's behind it, updating in real time (since the background can always be changing); there are three levels, primary (default text), secondary (descriptions, footnotes, subtitles) and a third level mentioned as tertiary; most of the time, white text and symbols are recommended, because color over the glass can become hard to see depending on the color of what's behind it; when color is necessary, apply it to a background layer or an entire button, rather than just the text/symbol; prefer system colors, already calibrated for legibility and dynamic adaptation of hue and contrast over the glass.
  - Positioning ergonomics: since the neck has more lateral range of motion (left/right) than vertical (up/down), it's more comfortable to turn the head sideways; that's why the UI should fit within the field of view, avoiding placing content too high or too low; larger screens should favor a wider proportion rather than a taller one (example: the Freeform canvas extends horizontally, not vertically); the most important information should stay centered.
  - Every interactive element needs a target area of at least 60 points around it, even if the visual element is smaller, a standard 44 point button needs at least 8 points of empty space around it to complete that area; a stack of standard system buttons should have at least 16 points of spacing between them; a "mini" 28 point button is still easy to select if it has 60 points of space around it (example: a disclosure control in a section header).
  - Every interactive system component shows a "hover effect" (subtle glow) when the person looks at it, giving confidence that the touch of the fingers will select the right element; inactive elements don't receive this feedback; lists and menus need at least 4 points of padding between items so the hover doesn't overlap between them; "lockups" (image with text below, for example) should define a single custom region so the hover covers the whole set as one element.
  - Nested elements should keep concentric corner radii with each other, formula: the radius of the inner element added to the padding between it and the container defines the correct outer radius; corners should be "continuous corners" to stay smooth; the entire system hierarchy, from the window down to the elements in the corners, follows this same logic of concentricity.
  - People interact by eyes, hands and voice (from a distance or touching directly), in addition to a connected keyboard, trackpad or game controller; all system components have already been built to respond to these inputs, so the recommendation is to use system components whenever possible, instead of recreating custom behavior.
- Concrete interface-building techniques (system components): the window uses glass material with a bar below to move the app through space; the tab bar is vertical, floating fixed to the left of the window (recommended not to exceed six items), it expands showing labels when the person looks for longer, and closes again when the gaze leaves it; sidebars sit inside the window next to the tab bar for sub-navigation; "ornaments" are a new feature, floating elements slightly in front of the window for persistent controls, ideal for toolbars and quick actions (they use borderless buttons by default); ornaments on the bottom edge of the window should overlap that edge by 20 points, to look integrated without blocking too much content; they can appear and disappear, but this is only recommended when focus is on a single piece of content (photo, movie); menus and popovers can expand outside the window and appear centered by default, instead of anchored to an edge like on iPad; the button that invokes a menu or popover should change to the "selected" state (black label on white background, reserved for that state, buttons with white background should be avoided outside of it) to give clear feedback of who opened what; sheets are presented as centered modals, keeping the same Z position as the parent window, which recedes and darkens; a secondary sheet can stack on top with an extra darkening layer; for views nested inside a modal, push navigation is preferred (the secondary view shows a back button, not a close button); the close button is always in the top left corner, by system convention.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Music (lockup with a light button over the glass, or a dark background behind it for more contrast); Arcade (Extra Large Title 1 in editorial layout); Photos (year/month/day navigation ornament, and the "Spatial Captures" example as a way to relive memories in a way only possible on this platform); Music - Now Playing (controls as a persistent ornament while navigating the app); Freeform (wide horizontal canvas).
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "It's important to be aware of your surroundings." "All our system components have been designed to support our wide array of inputs."

## Design considerations for vision and motion (id: wwdc2023_10078, 15.2 min)

- Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10078/.
- Central thesis: comfortable immersive experiences depend on providing correct and consistent visual depth cues, and on avoiding conflict between the visual motion information and the information the body's vestibular system is already registering.
- The design process Apple describes: Manda Paul (Apple Vision Science) covers the vision part (depth, content parameters, eye strain); Herman Damveld (research scientist) covers the motion part (movement of virtual objects, head-locked content, movement inside windows, oscillations). Both describe the reasoning starting from a bit of perceptual science before arriving at practical recommendations.
- Principles stated and the why of each (vision):
  - Visual comfort requires agreement between the depth the designer intended and the depth the brain actually perceives; when depth cues are absent, conflicting or misleading, the person may experience double vision or eye strain.
  - Depth cues include familiar size (ex.: recognizing the color of a lime helps the brain infer its size and distance), blur, relative size, added background, light and shadow, occlusion, texture density and subtle motion, very sparse scenes, without these cues, make it hard for the brain to converge the eyes correctly.
  - Conflicting cues hurt comfort: for example, if relative size suggests an object is closer but occlusion suggests the opposite, the brain receives contradictory information.
  - Repetitive patterns can trick each eye into locking onto a different element of the pattern, making the brain perceive the wrong depth when combining the two images; the fix is to use smaller portions of the pattern or break it up with another design element.
  - When manually rendering stereo content (such as stereo video), it is essential that the disparity between the two eyes be correct, because incorrect disparity can cause severe visual discomfort.
  - Content parameters should reduce visual effort according to the type of experience: content that requires prolonged eye fixation (such as reading) is more comfortable beyond arm's reach, and the person should be able to adjust that depth; content very close up should be reserved for momentary visual effort or for direct interaction (when the hand actually touches the item in 3D space); transparency or blur can redirect the eyes to a more comfortable depth when appropriate.
  - Contrast should also match the experience: high contrast for reading, low contrast (or transparency/blur) to redirect attention elsewhere; text size and window size/depth should allow comfortable reading without needing to turn the head or body.
  - Transitions from a dark scene to a light scene should be slower, giving the eye time to adapt to the brightness.
  - It is more comfortable to rotate the eyes downward or to the sides than upward or diagonally, which require more muscular effort and tire more; therefore, prolonged reading content should stay centered and slightly below the line of sight; if extreme eye rotation angles are necessary, the ideal is to design for brief interactions, or move the content to the center of the field of view to reduce effort; it is also important to plan for natural pause points in the experience, to allow the eyes to rest.
- Principles stated and the why of each (motion):
  - The vestibular system of the inner ear measures the movement of the body itself; when visual motion information is absent or conflicts with what the vestibular system is sensing, the person may experience dizziness or stomach discomfort; good content design allows the brain to perceive the world as stationary, which gives a sense of stability.
  - When one or more virtual objects cover a good part of the field of view and move, the brain can interpret that movement as if the person themselves were moving; the way to mitigate this is to make the objects semitransparent during the movement, so that passthrough remains visible.
  - Whenever possible, avoid "head-locked" content (locked to the head, always in the same position in the field of view), because it never gives a sense of stability; if necessary, use a small window, close to the center of the line of sight and farther away; always prefer "world-locked" views (locked to the world, not to the head); one alternative is "lazy-follow" animation, which moves the content slowly to its destination over time, instead of moving instantly.
  - For content moving inside a window (example: a virtual landscape moving), the brain can also interpret that movement as if the person themselves were moving; to optimize comfort: keep the content's horizon aligned with the real horizon; keep the "focus of expansion" (the point where the pixels appear to converge, and where people naturally look) with slow, predictable movement and within the field of view, avoiding fast turns or pure camera rotation (which take that point out of the field of view); as an alternative to fast turns, use instant direction changes during a quick fade-out.
  - Avoid having large objects pass close to and fast by the person; it is better to keep objects small and at a greater distance; using simple textures with low luminance contrast makes the movement less perceptible to the eyes.
  - Sustained oscillation of objects and animations should be avoided in general, and especially frequencies close to 0.2 Hz (one oscillation every five seconds); if the oscillation is truly unavoidable, keep the amplitude low and use transparency to reduce its perception; it is always good practice to offer an alternative without oscillation through the Reduce Motion accessibility setting.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: image of a citrus/lime used to illustrate familiar size and color as depth cues; repetitive pattern used to illustrate the problem of each eye locking onto a different element; blue polygons (blue polyhedrons) covering the field of view to illustrate movement of virtual objects; images of Mount Hood (the same location used in the immersive sound session) to discuss texture and distance in camera movement inside windows.
- Up to two short, literal quotes: "Well-designed content enables the brain to perceive the world as stationary." "Agreement between the depth intended by you and the depth perceived by the viewer is necessary for vision comfort."

## Design with SwiftUI (id: wwdc2023_10115, 18.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 15 of 15 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10115/.

Central thesis: Two designers from the Apple Maps team argue that SwiftUI is a design tool, not just a development framework, because designing directly on the device reveals hidden details, makes it possible to design how the interaction feels, test the design in real scenarios and present it with demos that explain themselves. All the designs for the new watchOS 10 Maps app were made in SwiftUI, directly on the device, which gave control over every detail, from spacing and text styles to animations and interactions.

The design process Apple describes:
1. Treat SwiftUI as a design tool: write declaratively in Xcode and see every change appear instantly in the visual canvas.
2. Get the details right by building on the device: start with a minimal prototype (a simple map on the watch), test it, fix what comes up (the zoom speed via the Digital Crown), add one layer at a time (points of interest, then interface controls, then the walking radius), bring that work back to the device, explore variations of a detail in separate prototypes and, finally, integrate those prototypes.
3. Design the interactions: prototype the feel of the interaction with gestures and also with real hardware sensors, adjusting animations, thresholds and haptic response until it feels right.
4. Test in realistic scenarios: take the design out into varied conditions, feed the components with real and diverse data, and create one-off, parameterized design tools to run through many variations.
5. Present with demos on the device: bring several demos together in a single SwiftUI app and take them to design reviews as often as possible, gathering feedback on the experience.
6. Ship what was designed: since the designer works with the same material as the engineer, the prototype can become an app submitted to the App Store.

Principles stated and why:
- Common things should be easy and complex things should be possible. Why: no time is lost recreating common interface elements and there is no limit when it is time to create something new. It is also what allows someone who does not code, like Will himself, to learn and design productively in a short time.
- SwiftUI balances two types of tool: it lowers the floor for simple tasks and raises the ceiling for creating new things. Why: tools that are only easy work for the simple and not the sophisticated; tools that are only powerful are hard to learn and bad at the basics.
- Declarative writing: you declare what you want to create (a Button, an Image). Why: this model relies on the intuition designers already have about how interfaces are put together.
- Modern interfaces are dynamic and require many decisions. Why: states like the image loading or the button pressed are what make the product feel complete; they stay hidden in static tools and show up right away in a dynamic tool.
- Building on the device gives a more complete understanding of what is being made. Why: the parts start to interact, the experience can be evaluated as a whole and it becomes clear what works and what still needs polishing.
- Interactions give the sense of how the design feels, not just how it looks. Why: this is why it helps that SwiftUI's animations are performant, easy to use and fully interruptible; and everything feels native because the work happens in a genuinely native environment.
- Design is also how the thing functions, and it is necessary to discover where it breaks. Why: it is easy to get carried away by ideal scenarios, with pretty photos and short labels; testing in the real world shows how the design holds up under everyday use.
- Demos on the device explain the design by themselves. Why: they build consensus, save meetings, reduce project risk and team confusion, and make the review spend its time evaluating instead of explaining.
- The tools of creative work have an enormous impact on the final experience. The reason given: using SwiftUI in the process would lead to a final product that is more thought through, complete and polished.

Concrete interface-building techniques, with exact numbers when stated:
- The talk gives no numeric interface values (spacing, font size, opacity, duration, threshold or spring constant). The only numbers stated are contextual: watchOS 10, compass prototype ready in a few hours, dozens of Apple frameworks.
- Typography: on a line of text, adjust font size, weight, content and color, with immediate update in the canvas; modifiers to change the text format with specific fonts.
- Style and layout via modifiers: drop shadows, borders, aspect ratio and padding.
- Ready-made system components and behaviors: color picker, push transition. System elements adapt on their own across iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch and Mac, and in most cases the code can be shared.
- Hardware and frameworks as design material: haptic feedback, audio, cameras, gestures, accelerometer; MapKit for dynamic maps, WeatherKit for live weather, RealityKit for augmented reality objects.
- States that need to be designed: how the image appears while loading and how the button appears when pressed.
- Digital Crown and zoom: test several sensitivity values in the prototype until the zoom speed feels natural.
- Progressive detail on scroll: the walking time gains more detail as the Crown is turned, solved with a counter (ticker) animation.
- Split-screen search scrolling: the goal is immediate response without excessive sensitivity. The platter follows slow movements of the Crown; when crossing a threshold, a new platter enters quickly with haptic response. Fast movements use a firmer spring animation so the interaction feels agile. This way the scrolling feels right both slow and fast.
- Compass: prototype with an enlarged compass using the watch's internal sensors to test transitions and make the cardinal direction precise.
- Outdoor legibility: observe how brightness and sunlight affect the reading of the cartography and the interface, which often leads to adjusting contrast levels across the whole app.
- Charts with real data: in the elevation chart, adjust the Y-axis scale so that flat routes appear as expected, and not like a mountain climb.
- Parameterized design tool: for the walking radius, which needs to render well over a very dynamic surface like the map, vary line width, opacity values and blend modes across different map environments.

Examples cited:
- Maps on watchOS 10 with a more map-centered experience: a simple map on the watch immediately revealed that the zoom via the Digital Crown was too fast. It teaches that testing on the device exposes problems that a static screen does not show.
- Walking radius around the current location, indicating what is nearby and how long it takes on foot: it called for more detail on scroll, several options were prototyped and the preferred one, the animated counter, was born in a separate prototype before being integrated. It teaches how much complexity hides in an apparently simple design, and that it only becomes obvious when you prototype it.
- Location button on the new home screen, which activates a compass showing the cardinal direction the person is facing: using the real sensors made the compass precise and refined a microinteraction.
- Search for a place, like a restaurant, in a split screen with map and results list: a scroll that would be hard to do in other tools. It teaches how to adjust the feel separately for slow and fast movements.
- Elevation chart for people who walk or cycle, with the route's elevation range and real-time progress: with San Francisco's hilly routes it worked as expected; with New York's flat routes it looked much more extreme than it was. It teaches testing with real and diverse data.
- Contrast adjustment because of brightness and sun: Maps is used outdoors in very varied conditions.
- Mini walking radius tool: it allows running through far more variations than would be efficient in a static tool, and tools like this answer specific questions or solve complex problems.
- Design reviews with several demos in a single app (walking radius, search results, cards with business information): everyone wanted to try it and give feedback, and the time went to evaluating, not explaining.
- The microwave analogy, easy but limited, versus the industrial espresso machine, powerful but hard for the basics: SwiftUI would sit in the balance between the two.

Quotes:
"common things should be easy and complex things should be possible" (Will)

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- Physical comfort and privacy appear as design prerequisites, not as an aesthetic touch-up: keeping elements at the same Z depth so the eyes don't tire, a hover effect that only reveals focus data to the app when there is an explicit gesture, and whole ergonomics rules about how far the eyes and neck can turn without effort (ids: wwdc2023_10072, wwdc2023_10073, wwdc2023_10078).
- When entering a new platform (visionOS), Apple treats familiarity with iOS as a deliberate design constraint: tab bar, sidebar, sheets, menus and popovers are reused almost one to one, and only depth, glass material and vibrancy are redesigned (ids: wwdc2023_10072, wwdc2023_10076, wwdc2023_10073).
- SwiftUI is presented explicitly as a design tool, and not only an implementation one: designers without a strong programming background prototype directly on the device to discover details (zoom speed, scroll behavior, the scale of a chart) that static design tools hide (ids: wwdc2023_10115, wwdc2023_10138).
- Symbol animation (SF Symbols 5) and the design of Dynamic Island/Live Activities share the same principle of "identity within a family": each element must be recognizable and distinct on its own, but still look like part of the same visual system (ids: wwdc2023_10197, wwdc2023_10258, wwdc2023_10257, wwdc2023_10194).
- Numeric ergonomics rules repeat in very different contexts within the same year: the 60 point minimum target area and the corner concentricity formula appear both in the general spatial UI session and in the specific eye input session, suggesting a single numeric system shared between the teams (ids: wwdc2023_10073, wwdc2023_10076).
- watchOS 10 and visionOS, released in the same year, use very similar design vocabulary despite being opposite platforms in scale: both talk about "glanceability" or quick legibility, layers of translucent material for hierarchy, and background color with a functional (not decorative) role to communicate state or identity (ids: wwdc2023_10026, wwdc2023_10138, wwdc2023_10309, wwdc2023_10072, wwdc2023_10076).
- Comfort decisions (vision and motion) and immersive sound decisions are validated with perceptual science and real field recording, not only with aesthetic intuition: the sound team reports having discarded the most "literal" recording of a location (the noise of a drainage system on Mount Hood) in favor of a more pleasant curation, and the vision/motion team explains each recommendation from how the human visual and vestibular system works (ids: wwdc2023_10078, wwdc2023_10271).
