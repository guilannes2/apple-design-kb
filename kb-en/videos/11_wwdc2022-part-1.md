## Design app experiences with charts (id: wwdc2022_110342, 15.7 min)

- Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/110342/.
- Presenter: Nicholas Felton, designer on the Human Interface team.
- Central thesis: charts focus attention on the most important information in an app; they should be used with intention, accompanied by a self-sufficient description, revealed in progressive complexity and, when there is more than one chart in the app, organized as a coherent design system.
- The design process Apple describes: in the talk, the presenter structures the session around three questions that guide the decision, when to use a chart, how to design it, and how multiple charts relate to each other (chart design system). To decide "when", he recommends first asking what the app experience needs, and only then evaluating whether the case fits one of the three scenarios useful for charts: history/forecast (change over time), part of a whole (progress or depletion), and comparison between items/categories. The whole process is tied to a fictional case study: a pancake food truck app, whose sales tab is redesigned throughout the session.
- Principles stated and why: (1) only the most important information should become a chart, because a chart is "a strong signal" that directs attention; (2) every chart description should be informative if read in isolation, because not everyone will look at the chart itself; (3) a new chart form should be central to the app, not supplementary, because the prominence given to it is what teaches people to interpret it; (4) differences between charts (color, orientation, style) should be designed on purpose, because they signal that something changed (subject, period, metric) and increase the chance that the person notices and reads the description.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: the chart description should become a complete sentence when the chart is not obvious (e.g.: "Sales in the past 30 days totaled 1,234 pancakes"), or interpret the data (e.g.: "Sales for the past 30 days are up 12%, totaling 1,234 pancakes") when the value is unknown to the reader. Data perspectives at three scales: macro (total, overall average), medium (subsets by time, weekday vs weekend, or by category), micro (individual points, such as the last transaction or the biggest sale). Size correlates with function: small, static charts (Watch complications, Stocks thumbnails, Health trend platters) skip grid lines, labels and interactivity; interactive charts the width of the view (but not full height) need axes and labels; the largest ones allow deep investigation. When linking a small version to an expanded version of the same chart, shape, values, context and state must be preserved, never showing something different from what has already been shown. Use of horizontal bars instead of vertical ones to visually differentiate a popularity chart from a time series chart, without needing to increase the platter's height.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Health (uses charts to understand the body), Fitness (motivational progress charts), Weather (charts to plan the day), Strava (detailed workout analysis), Duolingo (playful progress charts), all showing the variety of tone a chart can take; Activity onboarding, where the rings split to show move, exercise and stand, teaches how to introduce a new chart form with clarity.
- Short quotes: "A chart should be accompanied by text that describes the chart contents." "As the functionality of a chart increases, so will its size."

## Design App Shortcuts (id: wwdc2022_10169, 20.2 min)

- Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10169/.
- Presenter: Lynn, design producer for Siri and Shortcuts.
- Central thesis: with the App Intents framework and iOS 16, an app's App Shortcuts become automatically available in Siri, Spotlight and the Shortcuts app as soon as the app is installed, and designing a good shortcut requires choosing the right task, naming it well, taking care of the visual presentation and of how missing information is collected.
- The design process Apple describes: the first step is to select, among the app's features, the ones that are "self-contained" (completable without opening the app) and "straightforward" (quick to complete), the example given is "start sleep meditation" via Siri, against a long, multi-step form, which does not work for a shortcut. Then comes naming: the phrase needs to be short, include the app's name (official or a synonym submitted to the App Store) and have complete synonyms translated for each of the app's languages, covering natural speech variations without straying from the intent's central purpose. Finally, the session guides how to collect missing information at four increasing levels of friction: assume a value and proceed; Parameter Confirmation (assume and ask for confirmation); disambiguation (offer a short list); open-ended request (ask for an open value, such as a number, location or string); and, lastly, intent confirmation, reserved only for consequential actions.
- Principles stated and why: use at most one dynamic parameter per phrase and restrict it to a finite, predictable list, because in a voice-only context (AirPods) Siri reads the entire list and infinite values (like time) don't make sense there; use intent confirmation "appropriately but in moderation", because a shortcut's goal is to be as fast as possible to complete; never use ambiguous words like "Confirm" on intent confirmation buttons, and instead use a verb that restates the action, because the person needs to know exactly what will happen when they tap.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: maximum of 10 App Shortcuts per app, recommendation of 2 to 5 high-quality ones; disambiguation lists recommended for "5 values or less"; Custom Snippets use semi-translucent material (app elements placed over it, not an opaque background) and vibrant label colors to guarantee contrast, including in Dark Mode; Live Activity is indicated when there is a benefit to continuous access to information (e.g.: delivery progress, a counting timer), while Custom Snippet serves for a self-contained action or piece of information; watchOS 9 starts supporting Custom Snippets, which can require repositioning layout elements (the Coffee app repositions the address line to sit below the header on the watch); in Spotlight, the first App Shortcut in the array appears as a Siri Suggestion below the app, each shortcut gets an SF Symbols symbol on the right, and the display order depends on the order of the actions (only changes with an app update) and the order of the parameters (dynamic, can be updated at any time with the app open, following a meaningful heuristic such as recency or frequency).
- Examples cited: fictional app "Meditation" (self-contained/straightforward shortcuts, dynamic meditation type parameter); Voice Memo (synonyms "Start Voice Memo", "New Voice Memo" for "Record Voice Memo"); Panera (official name "Panera Bread" with synonym "Panera" incorporated into the phrase); Weather (app elements integrated into the snippet's translucent material); Coffee app (layout repositioning on watchOS).
- Short quotes: "Make it memorable." "The maximum you can create is 10."

## What's new in SF Symbols 4 (id: wwdc2022_10157, 17.8 min)

- Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10157/.
- Presenter: Thalia.
- Central thesis: SF Symbols 4 expands the library (more than 700 new symbols, surpassing 4,000 in total) and introduces Automatic Rendering and Variable Color, making the symbols more expressive and more integrated with San Francisco typography.
- The design process Apple describes: the decision of which rendering mode to use follows a logic of context, not a fixed rule. This year every symbol gets a "preferred rendering mode" (Automatic Rendering), but the talk insists on checking the context before trusting that, the example given is the AirPods Pro symbol, which prefers Hierarchical automatically, but at small size and low contrast is more legible when specified manually as Monochrome. To annotate custom symbols, the process described involves organizing the paths into layers according to the desired visual hierarchy (z-order) before applying color or hierarchy.
- Principles stated and why: Monochrome is "the most neutral", because it closely reflects SF Symbols' typographic nature; Hierarchical gives subtle emphasis by highlighting a symbol's most important shape, creating depth; Palette uses two or more contrasting colors to give more prominence and versatility without sacrificing aesthetics; Multicolor represents a symbol's intrinsic/native color and works best when the symbol is very prominent in the UI. Variable Color is meant to represent sequence/strength over time, not depth, so the layers that take part in the sequence are organized in order, and not every path of a symbol needs to take part.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: more than 700 new symbols, raising the library to more than 4,000 unique symbols; 5 new categories in the SF Symbols app (Camera & Photos, Accessibility, Privacy & Security, Home, Fitness). Variable Color works by percentage: 0% turns everything off, any value above 0% lights up the first layer, and the symbol is fully lit near 100%. Unified annotation unifies into a single layer structure what used to require two (one for Hierarchical/Palette, another for Multicolor); the concepts "Draw" and "Erase" control whether a layer draws or erases, creating a hole in the layers behind it, useful, for example, for the "+" of a badge to erase part of the badge.
- Examples cited: camera filters symbol (automatically prefers Hierarchical, to convey the translucency of physical lenses/filters); SharePlay symbol (Hierarchical, person in the foreground and waves in the background, reinforcing that the feature is about sharing and connecting); iPhone signal strength symbol (two smaller waves in one layer, two larger ones in another, the iPhone does not take part in the Variable Color sequence); volume symbol (speaker does not take part; three waves take part representing low, medium and high volume); room capacity symbol (empty, few people, half capacity, full capacity); step-by-step construction of a custom cupcake symbol (frosting with three paths in one layer, base, badge and "plus" in their own layers) annotated in Multicolor, Hierarchical/Palette and Monochrome with the same layer structure, and a kitchen timer with each path in its own layer to take part in Variable Color.
- Short quotes: "Symbols are used frequently and consistently, becoming second nature to us." "Variable Color is opacity based and it's available in all rendering modes."

## Apple Design Awards (id: wwdc2022_103, 18.6 min)

- Basis: transcript and 41 of 41 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/103/.
- This video is not a technical session with Apple's UI-building process, principles or techniques: it is a collage of short testimonials from developers and designers who are finalists/winners of the Apple Design Award, about what design is to them, where inspiration comes from, how they deal with failure, and the reaction to winning the award. There is no single presenter nor narrative structured by topics.
- Central thesis: for the interviewees, design is a form of personal expression that also needs to serve a purpose, and failing repeatedly is an inevitable part of the creative process ("Failing is part of any creation process").
- The design process Apple describes: it is fragmented and personal, not a formal method taught by Apple. Statements mention reducing an idea to a simplified version ("we often have to kind of reduce it down to a simplified version of it"), months of iteration, cutting a main feature that caused a 20% loss of users of an app not identified by name, and a habit of generating many ideas and discarding most of them ("if I came up with 50 ideas in a day and 49 were absolutely useless and one was really good, that's a very good day").
- Principles stated and why: there are no formal interface design principles stated by Apple in this piece; the statements are guests' personal opinions about creativity, inclusion and failure, not UI guidelines.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: not applicable. No pt/px/ms number, no layout, typography, color, motion or accessibility technique is described in this video.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Focus Noodles (app concept tied to a physical metaphor, "don't pick up the phone when you're focused and don't open the lid off the pot when cooking noodles"); Rebel Girls (character as "feminist aunt", brand voice coming from a character); Behind the Frame (escape room inspired by the color palette of Studio Ghibli and of Renaissance paintings); Townscaper (concept reduced to a single idea: "you just build beautiful houses"); Marvel Future Revolution (open-world mobile RPG with Marvel superheroes).
- Short quotes: "Design can be the difference between having a great experience or having a bad one." "Failing is part of any creation process."

## Make a great SharePlay experience (id: wwdc2022_10139, 18.6 min)

- Basis: transcript and 19 of 19 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10139/.
- Presenters: Ryan Williams (engineer on the SharePlay team) and Priya Shah.
- Central thesis: SharePlay creates closeness between people who are physically apart by bringing the group into a shared activity inside the app; for this to work, the app needs to foster a real sense of presence and take care of every stage of the activity: start, during and end.
- The design process Apple describes: the recommended exercise is the "portal exercise", imagining SharePlay as a portal that transports people, through their phones, into the same physical space, and asking what they would do with the app if they were really together. The example given is a physical turntable: anyone in the room can walk up and touch it, skip the song, change the track; from this comes the requirement to offer shared playback controls without restricting who can use them, and to remind people who else is there listening. When designing the activity itself, the recommended question is "what do people like to do together in person?" (cooking, yoga, watching a game, reacting to a show's cliffhanger), and then deciding whether the experience is single coordinated view (everyone sees the same thing, like Apple TV+, with AVPlaybackCoordinator and coordinateWithSession) or coordinated multiview (not everyone sees the same thing, as in the game Heads Up!, using GroupSessionMessenger.send for subsets of participants).
- Principles stated and why: never limit who can interact with the shared controls, because this reproduces the feeling of being physically present and able to act; communicate why the UI changed when the change was caused by another person (e.g., video paused by another participant), because people are used to associating a change on screen with their own touch; give easy access to controls even in immersive full-screen experiences, letting the status bar reappear with a simple, discoverable gesture.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: registerGroupActivity(_:) to appear in the Share Sheet; GroupActivitySharingController for a dedicated SharePlay button in the UI, with metadata (title, optional subtitle, image and a web URL for those without the app); as of this year, SharePlay can start directly from the app, without an active FaceTime call, including starting from Messages; lobby support to wait for latecomers before starting (example Heads Up!, "Let's Play!" button); Picture in Picture support for coordinated media experiences, to maintain presence when the person navigates out of the app during the activity.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Apple TV+ (single-view coordination, pause/play/seek synced for the whole group); Heads Up! (multiview, since the person guessing cannot see the same screen as the person giving clues); Flow, by Moleskine (shows the initials of whoever drew each stroke, reinforcing presence when there is no continuous audio/video, especially relevant with SharePlay via Messages); the Apple TV app (hides the status bar in fullscreen video, but a tap reveals it again).
- Short quotes: "One call can change everything." "Think of SharePlay like a portal transporting people through their phones and into the same space."

## Design for Collaboration with Messages (id: wwdc2022_10015, 11.5 min)

- Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10015/.
- Presenter: Jaewoo, designer on the Apple Design team.
- Central thesis: putting Messages at the center of collaboration, in place of email (asynchronous by nature), makes communication more responsive and integrates invitation, conversation/FaceTime and edit notification directly into the app's flow.
- The design process Apple describes: the session demonstrates a complete end-to-end flow starting from a Pages document with recipes. Tapping share opens the new system share popover, which offers "start a new collaboration" or "send a copy"; frequent conversations (such as a group called "Cupcake Designers") appear as a suggestion to eliminate the need to type emails; the invitation goes straight into the Messages message field, and the send button itself already starts the collaboration; from there, the group photo appears linked to the document, and the collaboration popover gives quick access to Messages, audio or video (FaceTime) without leaving the context.

## Design for Arabic (id: wwdc2022_10034, 20.2 min)

- Basis: transcript and 16 of 16 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10034/.
- Presenter: Mohamed Samir, designer on the Apple Design team.
- Central thesis: designing (or optimizing) an app for Arabic requires correctly handling right-to-left directionality throughout the layout and the mental navigation flow, as well as adjusting typography, iconography and specific numeral systems; using Apple's native frameworks, such as SwiftUI, already automatically solves a good part of this.
- The design process Apple describes: the recommended way to think about directionality is to turn the layout into wireframes and decide, element by element, what switches sides (titles, buttons, navigation bar, paragraphs always aligned to the right, carousels) and what stays the same (content such as images, videos and backgrounds, according to the description of the Weather example image, the sun always rises in the east, regardless of language, and should not be mirrored). After changing the layout, it's necessary to remember that the entire mental navigation flow also reverses: in the App Store example, the person starts at the Today tab on the right, goes through the story card, and ends at the product page on the left, "as if navigating an Arabic book from right to left". For charts with a time component, the recommendation is to check the target country before deciding the direction, because the behavior is not universal.
- Principles stated and why: increase the Arabic font size by 10% when next to Latin text in uppercase, because Arabic has no case distinction and Latin uppercase creates more optical volume, making the Arabic look smaller by comparison; use tracking 0 (or the system font) when the Arabic font is not optimized for spacing, because the connected nature of Arabic can break letter connections or create improper spacing; apply opacity to the whole word or phrase, not letter by letter, to avoid visible seams between connected letters when there is transparency.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: about 660 million people use the Arabic script today, the third most written language in the world after Latin and Chinese, present in more than 22 countries. SF Arabic has all weights from Ultralight to Black, with its own Optical Sizes, Text sizes prioritize legibility with higher stroke contrast and wider spacing, Display sizes simplify the geometry; the shape change depending on the point is automatic through the system. SF Arabic Rounded was introduced this year, with the same range of weights. Correct ligatures between connected letters (Kashida) are added automatically by the system font, with varied lengths for organic spacing. Iconography: the Today tab symbol was adjusted to align the lines to the right (more relevant to Arabic reading), while the magnifying glass kept its original direction because it reflects the angle of right-hand use, behavior of most users regardless of location; more than 300 Arabic and RTL symbols available in the SF Symbols app, including an Arabic signature symbol designed exclusively. Numerals: the "Western Arabic" form (the most globally familiar) is used in Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia; the "Eastern Arabic" form is used in Levant and Gulf countries; Egypt and Saudi Arabia use both; Arabic mathematical calculation also goes from right to left, adding units first, then tens, then hundreds; the choice between the two forms happens automatically according to the user's country, or can be switched manually.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Pages (navigation bar and icons flowing from right to left) shows basic UI directionality; App Store (Today tab → story card → product page flow) shows the reversal of the mental navigation flow; Weather (day carousel, temperature scale with the lowest value on the right, reversed pagination dots, but sun and images not mirrored) shows what changes and what does not change; Calendar (date progress from right to left, red lines under Islamic lunar calendar dates) shows cultural relevance beyond direction; battery usage graph in Settings (days of the week preferably right to left in Arabic) shows the specific case of charts with a time component; Clock, Health and Weather apps (use of different SF Arabic weights in title, body and numerals) show the practical application of the weights; Reminders (SF Arabic Rounded) shows the use of the new variant; Typograph watch face (numerals in both forms, Western and Eastern) shows the practical adaptation of numerals.
- Short quotes: "Arabic is a language that is written from right to left."

## Explore navigation design for iOS (id: wwdc2022_10001, 25.6 min)

- Basis: transcript and 18 of 18 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10001/.
- Presenter: Sarah McClanahan, designer on the Evangelism team.
- Central thesis: good navigation goes unnoticed because the person can focus on the content; tab bars must reflect the app's real information hierarchy, and each transition between screens (hierarchical push or modal) must correspond to the type of task at play.
- The design process Apple describes: the session uses a fictional cycling route app as a case study, starting from a crowded tab bar (map view with filters, editable itinerary and route collections, all on the first tab). The central question recommended for reorganizing is "why do people use your app?", starting from the premise that great apps do a few things very well, instead of trying to solve everything on one screen. From this comes a restructuring around "places" as the top level of the hierarchy (city → route list → route detail, with overview, distance, elevation, ascent/descent callouts, food stops), repeating the exercise for other key features, such as itineraries.
- Principles stated and why: use tabs to reflect the information hierarchy, with clear, descriptive labels, because the tab bar should translate an organization that is already clear, not create it; balance functionality across tabs, because the first tab tends to accumulate too much weight by default; avoid "Home" tabs that duplicate functionality from other tabs, because that creates redundancy and confusion about where things belong, plus the risk of "tab-jumping" when tapping an element; never hide or remove the tab bar, because persistent access to multiple top-level hierarchies, preserving context in each one, is one of the biggest advantages of tab navigation; use push to navigate hierarchy and reserve modal for a self-contained task, because each interaction carries a different mental expectation about continuity and focus.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: push transition slides the next screen in from the right to the left and should be used whenever there is a disclosure indicator/chevron; the tab bar must stay anchored during the push, and the navigation bar must show a clear title, with the "back" button reflecting the previous screen's title; in RTL languages (Arabic, Hebrew) the push direction reverses to follow the reading flow; use push when the flow requires frequent switching between screens (Messages: each conversation is a push, not a modal, to allow fluid transitions between chats). Modal always appears from the bottom of the screen, covering the tab bar, and serves three cases: a simple task (creating a reminder), a multi-step task (adding a transit card to Wallet) and full-screen content with minimal navigation (starting a video workout in Fitness). Modal anatomy: a guiding title in the navigation bar, the preferred action on the right in a bolder font (usually inactive until there is valid input), a "Cancel" action on the left (with an alert/action sheet if there is unsaved data); the "X" symbol should only be used when the modal has minimal interaction and no text input; avoid stacking modal on modal, though subviews inside a modal can use push (e.g.: removing a person from an itinerary, uploading a photo).
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: music apps with "Listen Now/Radio" or "Library/Albums/For You" tabs show how concise labels already tell the app's story without showing content; the fictional cycling app illustrates the tab bar reorganization from start to finish; Slopes, winner of the Apple Design Award in the Interaction category, with "Record a ski day", "browse resorts", "compare stats with friends" tabs, shows clear, specific labels in practice; Messages shows why push is better than modal in a flow with frequent switching; Fitness (video workout) and Wallet (transit card) illustrate, respectively, a fullscreen content modal and a multi-step modal; App Store (an article from the Today tab) and a fictional filter illustrate, respectively, a good and a bad use of the "X" symbol in a modal.
- Short quotes: "When apps have great navigation, it's often unnoticed." "Never force someone to change tabs automatically."

## Writing for interfaces (id: wwdc2022_10037, 23.5 min)

- Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10037/.
- Presenters: Kaely Coon and Jennifer Bush, writers (UX Writers/Content Designers) from Apple's Human Interface Design teams.
- Central thesis: writing for interfaces is part of the design itself, not something filled in afterward, and it must follow four pillars summarized in the acronym "PACE", Purpose, Anticipation, Context, Empathy, to create a natural dialogue between the app and the person using it.
- The design process Apple describes: for each screen, first define its Purpose (what the person most needs to know at that moment), using information hierarchy (what gets bigger/at the top, what gets smaller), deciding what to leave out, and ensuring that each step of a flow has its own clear purpose. Then comes Anticipation, treating the app as a conversation: first define the app's "voice" (what it would and would not say) and only then vary the "tone" according to the situation, always asking "what comes next" to anticipate the person's next question or action. Next, Context, which starts outside the app (where the person is, what else they are doing) and guides how to write alerts (confirming an action, a choice, or warning of an error) and empty states. Finally, Empathy: writing for everyone, with simple language, responsive to localization needs (text length, reading direction) and accessibility needs (VoiceOver).
- Principles stated and why: in alert buttons, never use "Yes"/"No" and always name the specific action (e.g.: "Cancel Subscription"/"Keep Subscription"), because this removes ambiguity about what is going to happen; use interjections like "oops" or excess "please"/"sorry" with moderation, because they can sound patronizing or insincere; avoid idioms and humor in the text, because they may not translate well or may exclude people; always describe VoiceOver elements (including symbols, graphics and images) with thoughtful text, because for some people these words are the entire app experience; use "person" instead of gender-specific references in descriptions, to keep everyone included.
- Concrete interface-building techniques (writing): the title "iPhone Needs to Cool Down" with long text was simplified to the title "Temperature" with one sentence and an "Emergency" button (before/after example of purpose); the subscription cancellation alert was rewritten from "Confirm Cancellation" (ambiguous) to "Cancel Platinum Subscription?" with buttons "Cancel Subscription"/"Keep Subscription" and body "You'll continue to have access until June 21, 2022" (date from the example itself); a generic error alert ("Oops! you can't do that", code "1234567", "Sorry, bad input. Please try again") rewritten into a title that clearly names the billing problem, with buttons "Add Payment Method" and "Not Now"; localization changes the text length and the vertical space needed (example of the iPhone language-change alert, in English, Thai, Dutch and Hebrew, the last read right to left); the abbreviation of weekday names varies by language: American English uses 1 letter, Catalan uses 2 letters (there is no 1-letter variation), Arabic does not abbreviate weekday names.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Apple Music with no words (shows how text is essential even when it seems minor); the Messages introduction screen "Share Your Name and Photo with Friends" (information hierarchy); Wind Down Shortcuts (stating the purpose openly); the Apple Cash for family setup flow (purpose per step, including a dedicated privacy screen); Clock app alarm ("Change Next Alarm Only" anticipating the most common need); Apple Watch fall detection (calm tone at a stressful moment); Activity app Move streak (celebratory tone, caution with exclamation marks); Breathe app and Maps commute notification (answering "what comes next"); Apple Watch outdoor walk reminder (one large button, context of little available attention); Camera Panorama (instruction positioned where the gaze already is); Weather location permission alert and "Remove iPhone?" alert (good examples of contextual alerts and of a destructive action); fictional restaurant app vs Apple Podcasts (bad empty state vs good); Memoji Stickers in VoiceOver (description of intent, not just physical shape).
- Short quotes: "You can tell people the purpose of a screen; it's not a secret." "Read your writing out loud."

## Design an effective chart (id: wwdc2022_110340, 25.0 min)

- Basis: transcript and 20 of 20 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/110340/.
- Presenters: Halden and Lilian.
- Central thesis: an effective chart is focused (it communicates only the few key pieces of information defined as the goal), approachable (easy to interpret quickly) and accessible (it works with VoiceOver and audio graphs), built in five steps: marks, axes, descriptions, interaction and color.
- The design process Apple describes: it continues the pancake food truck case study (now selling in San Francisco and Cupertino), focusing this time on how to design a specific chart of sales from the last 30 days, no longer on when to use charts. The first step is identifying the chart's goal ("how were pancake sales in the last 30 days?") and, among the many possible directions, choosing only the right priorities, in this case pattern, range (range of variation) and values (specific values). From there, the process follows the five steps in order: choosing the right marks by testing with real data (not just imagined "clean" data); designing the axes considering a fixed or dynamic range and the density of grid lines; writing descriptions that contextualize and summarize the take-away; adding interaction for on-demand exploration; and finally, using color as reinforcement, never as the only means of communicating critical information.
- Principles stated and why: "it's important to test your designs with real data early," because real data is rarely as "clean" as imagined data, and a mark that looks great with fictitious data can fail with real data (the example shows dots, lines and bars tested against irregular data, with the line failing when there are days without sales interspersed); fixing the axis's lower bound at 0 when using bar marks, because this keeps the bar height proportional to the value (a bar twice as tall represents double the sales); color must be reinforcement, never the only means of conveying critical information, because of color blindness and the system setting "Differentiate Without Color"; balancing the visual weight between category colors, so as not to imply an undue hierarchy between them.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: the vertical axis of the battery chart in Settings uses a fixed range from 0 to 100%; the steps axis in Health uses a dynamic range; in the sales chart, the vertical axis's lower bound stayed fixed at 0 and the upper bound dynamic; recommended density of about 4 horizontal grid lines, adjusted as the range changes, with intuitive values such as multiples of 20; for the 30-day axis, 5 grid lines reading time in steps of 7 days; title rewritten from "Sales in the Past 30 Days" to "Total Sales: 1,234 Pancakes", adding a concrete value and a summary; example of a take-away description cited from Weather: "Light Rain Forecasted, Light rain is expected to start in 9 minutes and last for 36 minutes"; the sales chart's audio graph describes via VoiceOver "The x-axis is time. The y-axis is sales. There is one data series."; touch targets stretched to the chart's full height (including the blank space above the bars), not limited to the mark's exact size; accessibility labels succinct, spelled out (e.g., "June" instead of "Jun"), with the date before the value (e.g., "June 1, 36 pancakes"); example of an accessibility label per bar section in Maps: "From 3.6 miles to 4.4 miles: Climb 100 feet, descend 5 feet".
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: the battery chart in Settings (fixed range) and the steps chart in Health (dynamic range) show the two types of range; the Health trend platter (no grid lines or labels) shows when to simplify; Weather (description with take-away) shows how to summarize the essential; Fitness (activity rings using color to distinguish categories, and trend charts giving recent activity more visual weight) and Health (removing color to highlight the minimum/maximum heart rate) show distinct uses of color; Maps (elevation on cycling routes, many vertical bars) shows how to summarize an entire section in a single accessibility label instead of navigating bar by bar.
- Short quotes: "An effective chart focuses on a few key pieces of information." "It's important to test your designs with real data early."

## Adopt Variable Color in SF Symbols (id: wwdc2022_10158, 17.2 min)

- Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10158/.
- Presenter: Paul, from the SF Symbols app team.
- Central thesis: Variable Color controls a symbol's appearance through a percentage value, allowing it to represent values that change over time (signal strength, progress, time passing), and "unified annotation" makes annotating custom symbols faster by sharing a single layer structure across all rendering modes.
- The design process Apple describes: the demonstration begins in the SF Symbols app, in a new preview area of the rendering inspector that shows the symbol simultaneously in all rendering modes, including the "Automatic" option (each symbol chooses its preferred one). Starting from a category dedicated to "Variable" symbols, the process of annotating a custom symbol (example of a puzzle cube, puzzle cube) follows: first separate the shapes into layers for Hierarchical (which also feeds Palette), then assign colors in Multicolor reusing the same layer structure, and finally separate the pieces that should "fill" over time into their own layers, ordered from the one that fills first (at the bottom of the list) to the one that fills last (at the top), turning on Variable Color on them.
- Principles stated and why: Variable Color works in all rendering modes (Monochrome, Hierarchical, Palette, Multicolor) and does not impose a limit on how many layers can take part in the sequence, because it is controlled by percentage, so "you don't have to worry" about the number of layers; the thresholds between layers are spaced equally between 0% and 100% and rounded to the nearest percentage point, turning on the next layer one whole point above, so that rounding errors do not make symbols behave unexpectedly or require manual control of decimal places.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: system symbols exist in 9 weights, each in 3 scales (27 variants), each in 4 rendering modes with and without Variable Color, totaling 216 possible configurations, resolved by drawing only 3 variants via variable templates, with the system generating the other 24 automatically. Example of thresholds in the microphone symbol with 4 points: 0% turns all of them off (the only "visually empty" point), any value above 0% turns on the first point, from 26% it turns on the second, from 51% it turns on the third, from 76% to 100% it turns on all four. Example of rounding with 3 layers: the first threshold, 33.3333%, rounds to 33%, so the second layer turns on at 34%; the second threshold, 66.6667%, rounds to 67%, so the last layer turns on at 68%. Two new layer features in unified annotation: "Erase" (the layer erases/creates a hole in the layers behind it, useful in badges with "+" or "-") and "Hidden" (excludes the layer from a specific rendering mode when it cannot be used in all of them). Export requires template 4.0 to control custom Monochrome and use Variable Color; symbols annotated in previous years are automatically updated to unified annotation, without losing the Hierarchical and Multicolor annotations; the 3.0 and 2.0 formats remain available for older platforms.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: speaker/volume symbol (live demonstration of the Variable Color dimming/brightening slider in all rendering modes); microphone symbol (exact thresholds of 4 points); Wi-Fi and battery symbols (only become visually empty at exactly 0%); brightness and volume symbols (become visually full before 100%); custom puzzle cube symbol, including the version inside a circle (.circle.fill), shows in practice the unified annotation flow, the use of Erase to recover contrast in Monochrome, and the final application as a visual timer for the presenter's daughter, changing only the symbol's name without changing the code.
- Short quotes: "Variable Color is a new feature of SF Symbols that allows you to affect the appearance of a symbol using a percentage value." "That's the power of unified annotation."

## Meet the expanded San Francisco font family (id: wwdc2022_110381, 18.5 min)

- Basis: transcript and 13 of 13 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/110381/.
- Presenter: Vincenzo, type designer at the Apple Design Team.
- Central thesis: this year the San Francisco family gains a new width axis (Width), Condensed, Compressed and Expanded, added to Regular, and expands its language coverage with SF Arabic Rounded, bringing more typographic control and more consistency across multiple scripts.
- The design process Apple describes: the new width axis was born from specific needs identified in real apps, Photos needed more stylistic variation in Memories titles, News needed expressive, space-efficient styles for editorial headlines, and Maps needed more typographic contrast and legibility, which Weight and Optical Size alone did not solve. From this, the team designed a Width axis, keeping the vertical proportions between styles constant (so fonts could be swapped or combined without scale discrepancy) and changing only the horizontal proportions of the glyphs (and, as a consequence, the line length).
- Principles stated and why: styles closer to the center of the weight and width "stylistic map" have a more neutral voice, and styles at the edges (more extreme proportions) have a more expressive voice, because they are useful when you want to draw attention to an area of the UI or reinforce a typographic hierarchy; "with great typographic power comes great responsibility", fonts are design tools made to make interfaces beautiful, but above all legible and accessible, so choosing a non-regular Width requires extra attention to legibility in long texts.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: the SF family includes SF Pro and SF Pro Rounded (the main fonts for iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS), SF Compact and SF Compact Rounded (optimized for narrow columns and small sizes, the default on watchOS) and SF Mono (monospaced, used in Xcode and Swift Playgrounds); most families cover weights from Ultralight to Black and have Optical Sizes that automatically adjust details according to point size. The new axis has four styles: Regular (the most versatile and legible), Condensed (narrow but still comfortable, fits more text without imposing itself), Compressed (the most compact available in SF, flat-sided shapes, ideal for Display, but can become too tight in long texts) and Expanded (a loose, open look, good for Display, small labels and secondary content). In the News example, a long headline goes from 5 lines in Regular to 4 lines in Condensed and 3 lines in Compressed. All width styles cover the Latin (from English to Vietnamese), Greek and Cyrillic scripts.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Photos (Memories titles combining Compressed and Expanded, or a single Width with contrasting weights like Black and Light, or a single weight with contrasting Widths) shows the three ways to create typographic contrast; News (Condensed and Compressed reducing the number of lines in a headline, Expanded working well in captions and bylines) shows a practical space gain paired with personality; Maps (Expanded with loose tracking to represent mountain ranges, other Widths differentiating labels for roads, countries and continents) shows an elaborate typographic system, although the general recommendation is that two or three styles are already enough for most cases; macOS screensaver (multilingual cursive "hello" greeting), iPhone (Arabic, Arabic-Indic and Devanagari numerals) and Typograph watch face (5 sets of numerals, including new Chinese numerals) illustrate Apple's commitment to typographic consistency across languages; Reminders and Fitness illustrate the practical use of the new SF Arabic Rounded.
- Short quotes: "With great typographic power, comes great responsibility."

## Qualities of great AR experiences (id: wwdc2022_10131, 13.3 min)

- Basis: transcript and 11 of 11 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10131/.
- Presenter: Alli Dryer, Apple Design team (augmented reality).
- Central thesis: augmented reality feels like magic when it is useful, delightful and connected to the physical world; the decision to use AR must rest on specific criteria, and the design needs to address the spatial, motion and ergonomic particularities that set it apart from 2D interfaces.
- The design process Apple describes: before designing anything, the session proposes four criteria for deciding whether AR makes sense for a feature, deliver a real/true representation of scale; meaningfully involve the physical space; visualize something in 3D to make understanding and evaluation easier; and simplify actions by attaching digital capabilities to physical things. Only after confirming that one of these criteria applies do the specific design recommendations come in: orient the person toward a suitable environment, make use of "screen space" for text and controls, design for constant motion, take care of ergonomics, deal with limited field of view, use depth cues, and limit the duration of the experience.
- Principles stated and why: place text and interactive elements in "screen space" (the 2D layer over the 3D scene captured by the camera), instead of in the 3D world, because this preserves legibility; give real-time feedback with visuals and sound while the person moves, even if the action happens outside the field of view, because it helps maintain the connection with the experience; emphasize legibility "at arm's length" and simplify interactions to the minimum effort, because holding the arm outstretched for too long is tiring; limit the experience to at most one or two minutes, both for ergonomic reasons and because AR uses a lot of battery and generates heat on the device.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: the pinch gesture to scale an object in AR Quick Look includes haptic feedback when exceeding 100% scale; when an object is outside the field of view to the right or left, use sound, haptics and simple indicators or text in screen space, or offer a map/aerial view (RoomPlan example, with a small 3D model at the bottom showing a preview and scanning progress); recommended depth cues include size, perspective that decreases toward the horizon, realistic shadows and lighting, adequate detail in textures, and overlap/occlusion of objects (example: a virtual airplane appears behind wooden blocks on a table because its lower part is hidden); experiences should last "no more than a minute or two", and longer experiences should be divided into chapters with pause points.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Monster Park (3D dinosaurs at real scale) shows true-to-size representation; Color Snap (tapping the wall to preview paint color, walking around to see it from various angles) shows meaningful involvement of the physical space; Warby Parker (trying on virtual glasses) and IKEA Place (virtual furniture with realistic shadows via ARKit lighting) show 3D visualization for confident purchase decisions; Measure App (a person's height shown automatically when centering the camera, with minimal UI and information that disappears after being seen) shows simplification of action via the physical environment; Mission to Mars (a coaching sequence asking for a safe place, a textured surface, a well-lit environment, and high-contrast text/buttons in screen space) shows how to orient the environment and use screen space; DoodleLens (animation of an iPhone swaying in front of a drawing, large buttons at the bottom of the screen) shows motion instruction and ergonomics in practice; For All Mankind: Time Capsule (an experience in chapters, with pauses) shows how to handle a longer duration.
- Short quotes: "Augmented Reality feels like magic when it's useful, delightful, and relates to the physical world." "AR can be surprisingly immersive, and yet at the same time, it's always grounded in what's really happening."

## What's new in iPad app design (id: wwdc2022_10009, 18.1 min)

- Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10009/.
- Presenters: Bryant Jow and Grant Paul, Apple Design Team.
- Central thesis: with larger screens, support for external display, display zoom and Stage Manager in iPadOS 16, iPad apps should embrace large screens and resizable windows, and Apple introduces a set of new patterns (reorganized toolbars, document menu, edit menus, find and replace, "browser-style" navigation, search, tables and selection) to make apps more productive.
- The design process Apple describes: the session divides the news into two blocks of use, document editing (Bryant) and content navigation/search (Grant), and, for each component, guides a practical decision: for toolbars, ask which are the app's most common workflows that deserve a one-tap direct-access spot in the center, and arrange the important items in the trailing section so they survive window resizing; for the document menu, decide whether an action affects the document as a whole (goes in the document menu), takes content outside the app (goes in Share) or directly affects the content inside the document (should support toolbar customization and appear in edit menus); for navigation, assess whether the app's hierarchy is complex enough (with frequent jumps between levels) to justify the "browser-style" pattern, or shallow enough to dispense with it.
- Principles stated and why: never remove the standard editing actions (Cut, Copy, Paste), because they are fundamental to many workflows; group custom actions near related system actions, to keep predictability; use pop-up buttons in lists only when there is a well-defined set of options (and do not force the pattern when the options really require more control, preferring in that case a "Custom" option that reveals extra controls); treat the new SwiftUI tables as an extension of list views (showing more information about a list of content), not as a data spreadsheet, because iPad screens are relatively small.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: the new toolbar layout aligns titles to the left, with the leading section reserved for navigation (back, sidebar) and the new title/document menu, the center section for elevated, commonly used actions (grouped and collapsible into a "+" button when space is lacking), and the trailing section for items that must always stay visible, plus an optional overflow button; when resizing the window, center items collapse first into overflow, and in very small windows only leading and trailing remain. Customizable toolbars let you add, remove and reorder items in the center section (navigation items outside it are not customizable). Edit menus appear horizontal and scrollable with touch, and as a more complete vertical list with pointer. Find and replace was incorporated into the system keyboard, with a compact interface above the app when a physical keyboard is connected. "Browser-style" navigation uses back/forward buttons (example Files). Search in the top right corner of the navigation bar serves to filter the content of the screen itself, with suggestions of recent searches, recommendations and filters; search across the whole app should be in a dedicated Search tab. Band selection no longer automatically enters edit mode; Command and Shift select/deselect without entering edit mode; a secondary click (or long press on touch) opens a context menu to act on the selection; context menus also work on empty areas to create something new. Submenus open horizontally when there is space on iPad (on iPhone they open vertically). SwiftUI tables show multiple sortable columns by tapping the header, but go back to single-column lists at compact width, moving the details from secondary columns into a second line of text in each row of the list.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: Pages (new toolbar, document menu) and Keynote (document menu combining standard and app-specific actions, edit menus in both touch and pointer to select layers) show the new document-editing patterns; Notes (edit menu grouping checklist and text formatting, band selection, drag-and-drop to move notes to a folder) shows selection and organization; Files (browser-style navigation between folders, context menu on empty area to create a new folder) shows the complex-navigation pattern; Calendar (paste a copied event, submenu to quickly move an event between calendars) and Reminders (pop-up button to change priority without leaving the list, date and priority submenus) show the new menu controls; Photos is cited as an example of a shallow hierarchy that does not need browser-style navigation, because all destinations are already one tap away in the sidebar.
- Short quotes: "Your app design should always embrace larger screens and different window sizes."

- This is the same testimonial video described in wwdc2022_103 ("Apple Design Awards"), published as the ASL (American Sign Language) version of the session. The transcribed script/caption is identical, word for word, to that of wwdc2022_103: the same collage of statements from Apple Design Award finalists/winners about what design is, inspiration, failure and the reaction to the award, with no single presenter and no technical topic structure.
- Central thesis: identical to that of wwdc2022_103, design as a form of personal expression in service of a purpose, and failure as an inevitable part of the creative process.
- The design process Apple describes: same content as wwdc2022_103 (see that card for the full detail of the statements about narrowing down an idea, iteration, feature cutting and generating many disposable ideas).
- Principles stated and why: not applicable as UI guidelines, for the same reason described in wwdc2022_103, they are personal testimonials, not a technical session.
- Concrete interface-building techniques: not applicable, same reason as wwdc2022_103.
- Examples cited and what each one teaches: the same as wwdc2022_103, Focus Noodles, Rebel Girls, Behind the Frame, Townscaper, Marvel Future Revolution.
- Short quotes: "Design can be the difference between having a great experience or having a bad one." "Failing is part of any creation process."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- Apple's design process almost always starts from the question "what is the goal of this screen/this component?" before any visual decision: it appears explicitly in Writing for interfaces (wwdc2022_10037, "Purpose" pillar), in Design app experiences with charts (wwdc2022_110342, "consider what the experience needs first") and in Design an effective chart (wwdc2022_110340, "good chart design begins with identifying the chart's goal").
- Accessibility is treated as part of the design process from the start, not as a later addendum: Design an effective chart (wwdc2022_110340) describes VoiceOver and audio graphs as a mandatory step right after choosing the marks; Writing for interfaces (wwdc2022_10037) dedicates the whole "Empathy" pillar to localization and VoiceOver; SF Symbols 4 and Adopt Variable Color in SF Symbols (wwdc2022_10157, wwdc2022_10158) treat contrast and legibility at small sizes as a criterion for choosing the rendering mode; Qualities of great AR experiences (wwdc2022_10131) recommends high contrast and larger type in "screen space" even outside a context of a specific disability.
- There is a strong preference for reusing native system frameworks and components instead of recreating behavior: Design for Arabic (wwdc2022_10034) repeats several times that using SwiftUI resolves RTL directionality automatically; SF Symbols (wwdc2022_10157, wwdc2022_10158) resolves rendering mode and Variable Color through the app's own layer structure; What's new in iPad app design (wwdc2022_10009) describes tables, toolbars and context menus as system controls ready to adopt.
- Progressively revealing complexity is a recurring pattern: Design app experiences with charts (wwdc2022_110342) formalizes this as a principle ("progressively reveal chart complexity"), from the static chart to the interactive one; Design an effective chart (wwdc2022_110340) applies the same principle in the five steps of building a single chart (marks → axes → descriptions → interaction → color); Design App Shortcuts (wwdc2022_10169) uses the same logic in the information-gathering ladder, from assuming a value to asking for intent confirmation.
- Cultural and local context changes decisions that would otherwise seem neutral: Design for Arabic (wwdc2022_10034) shows that even icons that seem universal (magnifying glass) can be kept, while others (text alignment, calendar, numerals) change; Design an effective chart (wwdc2022_110340) cites the example of red/green in stock charts meaning the opposite in the US and in China.
- Teaching through a continuous case study appears as a deliberate pedagogical method: Design app experiences with charts (wwdc2022_110342) and Design an effective chart (wwdc2022_110340) use the same fictional pancake food truck app to tie together, across two separate sessions, the decision of "when" to use a chart and the process of "how" to design it.
