# wwdc2021 (part 1)

## The practice of inclusive design (id: wwdc2021_10275, 34.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 29 of 29 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10275/.

Central thesis: an inclusive app or game is not only usable by different people, but also welcomes people from diverse backgrounds and perspectives; this requires intentional work in content, language, accessibility, localization, color and in the identity fields the app offers.

The design process Apple describes: the presenters (Linda Dong, Design Evangelist, and Sam Iglesias, Prototyper) organize the practice into six steps the team should go through when designing any screen or marketing material: telling diverse stories, avoiding stereotypes, adopting accessibility, localizing for culture, using color with care, and encouraging self-expression. Each step is treated as a question to ask during design (for example: "what stories does this screenshot tell about who the app is for?", "am I associating an enemy with an ethnicity that is already marginalized?", "does this color have the same connotation in another culture?"). The recommended method for validating these decisions is talking with people outside the designer's immediate circle, since most stereotypes are unconscious.

Principles stated and why:
- Diversity of stories in marketing material (App Store, screenshots): because names, scenarios and photos communicate who the app was made for even before the download.
- Avoiding binary gender stereotypes and ableist language ("ableist language"): because assuming only two genders or using words like "insane"/"crazy" in a pejorative way excludes people without the team noticing.
- Adopting accessibility as part of design, not as an extra: the assumption that a person with a disability "won't want" to use the app is cited as the root error.
- Localizing for culture beyond literal translation: idioms and metaphors do not always have a direct equivalent.
- Using color with cultural awareness: the same color can mean opposite things in different cultures.
- Encouraging self-expression without forcing fixed categories (name, gender): so as not to exclude name formats or gender identities outside the Western standard.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Typography and Dynamic Type: using the correct layout APIs in SwiftUI/UIKit so text automatically adapts to different sizes, avoiding cut off or truncated text at larger sizes.
- Bold Text: non-textual elements that have a legibility function (such as indicator circles) should also get bolder when the user turns on Bold Text.
- VoiceOver: using a custom descriptor on buttons represented only by a glyph (e.g.: instead of "Gear shape two, button", using "Account settings, button"); controlling VoiceOver's reading order when the visual order is not the logical one (e.g.: the "Post" button in the footer should be readable first).
- Color and contrast: aiming for a minimum contrast of 4.5:1 when "Increase Contrast" mode is on; this is cited as a "rough rule", since some combinations that pass this number are still hard to read in practice.
- Color blindness: cited as affecting almost 5% of the world's population; the interface should not depend only on color to convey state (example given: "unread" and "flagged" dots that use color also need a different shape/glyph).
- Name and forms: not imposing character limits or prohibiting hyphens/accents in name fields; considering regional variations in name and surname (e.g.: two surnames in Latin America, surname first in China, absence of a surname in Malaysia and Afghanistan); a single "full name" field is recommended, along with the option to ask for the preferred name.
- Gender in forms: avoiding fixed options like "Male/Female/Other"; offering a broad spectrum and the option to make the choice public or private.
- Stocks and regional color: example cited that, when setting the region to mainland China, stock market gains are shown in red (associated with luck and fortune), unlike the Western green/red standard for gain/loss.

Examples cited:
- Screenshots of a social app and a to-do list app: show how to replace "familiar" names and scenarios (John, shopping, Diwali vs. a generic birthday) with more diverse options without losing the story.
- SFSymbols: cited as a source of gender-neutral human figure glyphs.
- Bumble: cited as an example of an app that offers a wide range of gender options at sign-up, with the choice to make it public or private and change it at any time.
- "Say No! More": game cited as an example of character creation without asking for gender, with a wide variety of age, skin color, body type, hair and clothing, and voices not tied to gender.
- Stocks (Apple's app): example of color adapted by region (red for gain in mainland China).

Short quotes: "Inclusive design is welcoming and respectful, and inclusive design is great for everyone." "Nine cows and one hair," used as an example of a Chinese idiom with no direct literal translation.

## Tap into virtual and physical game controllers (id: wwdc2021_10081, 15.0 min)

Basis: transcript and 12 of 12 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10081/.

Central thesis: the Game Controller framework abstracts input hardware (physical controllers, keyboard, mouse) behind a common API, and this year gains a new type of on-screen virtual controller, plus support for adaptive triggers and automatic gameplay capture, so that input code can be written once and work on all devices.

The design process described is more technical than visual interface: the presenter (Nat, from the Game Technologies team) recommends reacting to GCController, GCKeyboard and GCMouse connection/disconnection notifications, and choosing between value-change handlers or polling the controller's state, keeping the same pattern for all input types.

Principles stated:
- Always use the correct glyph of the connected physical controller (Y on Xbox/MFi, triangle on PlayStation) instead of fixed button names, because user feedback showed strong discomfort with wrong glyphs in the UI.
- Support the option to show the button's positional location instead of the name, for new or young players who recognize the position faster than the label.
- When querying the glyph, always request the sfSymbolsName of the mapped button (not of the physical button pressed), so that the UI reflects remappings the player made in the system preferences.
- In the new on-screen virtual controllers, manual positioning of elements is not supported: the layout is determined by the chosen configuration (zero to four buttons per side, plus a thumbstick, d-pad or touchpad), which guarantees grip and responsiveness tested by Apple.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- "Controller Supported" badge on the App Store, activated by adding the "Game Controllers" capability in Xcode (tags the Info.plist).
- Media capture integrated into the controller's Share button: double tap takes a screenshot, long press starts/stops recording; this year "15-second highlights" was added, with automatic background buffering.
- Code example: create GCVirtualControllerConfiguration, configure elements (two thumbsticks and two buttons), call connect() and handle GCControllerDidConnectNotification; customize button appearance with BezierPath and hide/show elements via config.hidden.
- DualSense adaptive triggers: check whether the controller's physical profile is DualSense, apply a resistance effect (feedback effect) proportional to how much the trigger was pulled, and always turn off the effect at the end of the action (example of the slingshot in the game).

Examples cited: SteelSeries Nimbus+, Razer Kishi, Backbone One, Sony DualShock 4, Xbox One, Xbox Elite V2, Xbox Adaptive Controller, Sony DualSense and Xbox Series X (support added in iOS 14.5 and macOS 11.3); the game "Fox2" used to demonstrate screenshot and recording via the Share button.

Short quote: "So whether your player is on team circle-square-triangle-cross or on team A-B-X-Y, your game UI will look right for them."

## SF Symbols in SwiftUI (id: wwdc2021_10349, 11.0 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10349/.

Central thesis: SF Symbols integrates into SwiftUI through Image, Label and string interpolation in Text, with modifiers for color, size, variants and four rendering modes (monochrome, hierarchical, multicolor, palette), allowing symbols to be customized with little code and good automatic accessibility.

The design process described is technical/API: the presenter (Jacob) recommends using Label instead of isolated Image whenever possible, because Label combines title and icon and automatically adapts the presentation to context (showing only the symbol, only the title, or both, depending on the component).

Principles stated and why:
- Prefer Label over Image because it gives automatic accessibility support (VoiceOver) via the title; for system symbols without an explicit title, SwiftUI already generates an accessibility description based on the symbol's content, but for symbols without a standard meaning or custom ones, you need to provide accessibilityLabel or a localized string in the Localizable.strings file.
- Do not over-specify the symbol's variant (e.g.: do not hardcode ".fill" manually): use the base version and let the component (like tab bar) apply the correct variant automatically per platform (fill on iOS, outline on macOS), which also makes the code more reusable.
- Choose the rendering mode according to the communicative intent: monochrome for consistent colors within a set; multicolor to represent the intrinsic identity/color of each symbol; hierarchical to emphasize key parts with opacity; palette for maximum control of up to three colors per layer.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- foregroundStyle: sets the color of the symbol and the text (specific color, current tint, or secondary); it can receive a Material to blur the background behind the symbol.
- font (with text styles like body/caption) scales the symbol and the text together with Dynamic Type; a fixed size keeps a constant size.
- imageScale: a separate axis that adjusts the symbol's size relative to the text, without changing the font size.
- symbolVariant: a modifier that propagates a variant (fill, slash, wrapping shapes like .circle.fill) throughout a view hierarchy via environment.
- symbolRenderingMode: accepts .multicolor, .hierarchical, .palette; automatic rule: a single foregroundStyle with no mode specified becomes monochrome; more than one foregroundStyle with no mode specified automatically becomes palette.
- A symbol embedded in Text via string interpolation: recommended when the symbol should flow together with the text's line break (example given: a chevron).

Examples cited: a card game list used as a connecting thread to demonstrate multicolor (colors associated with each suit), hierarchical (emphasis on parts of a shuffle button) and palette ("undo" button with a colored circular background to match the cards' colors).

Short quote: no literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted as central in this video; the content is mostly instructional/technical without notable catchphrases.

## Create custom symbols (id: wwdc2021_10250, 25.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 19 of 19 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10250/.

Central thesis: creating a custom symbol requires drawing on an SVG template (now at version 3.0) that preserves typographic alignment, optical margins, multiple weights and scales, and that can be annotated to support the multicolor and hierarchical rendering modes, in addition to, optionally, being drawn for automatic interpolation between weights and scales.

The design process described by the presenter (Mike), step by step:
1. Check whether the library of more than 3,000 symbols (SF Symbols 3) already covers the need; a custom symbol is only necessary for a very specific case in the app (example used: a crown with a heart to represent the "Queen of Hearts" in a card game app).
2. Open the SF Symbols app, duplicate an existing symbol as a starting point (Duplicate as Custom Symbol) to have a reference for stroke weight, scale and alignment, and rename it.
3. Export the 3.0 template (File > Export Template or Command+E) for editing in Sketch, Illustrator or Figma; choose between static setup (27 sets of paths, a single explicit pair of margins) or variable (three design sources: Ultralight-Small, Regular-Small, Black-Small).
4. Draw the necessary design variant(s), always converting strokes into paths before finalizing.
5. Reimport into the app and, if you want to support multicolor/hierarchical, annotate: create layers of paths, assign color (multicolor) or hierarchical group (primary/secondary/tertiary).
6. Export the final template (2.0 for compatibility with iOS 14 or earlier, monochrome only; 3.0 for iOS 15+, with all the annotation data) directly to Xcode or back to the SF Symbols app.

Principles stated and why:
- Avoid "live" strokes (live strokes) in the final drawing: always convert to closed paths, because only closed shapes can receive color or hierarchical group.
- Avoid open paths (without connected start and end points): open paths do not have a fill area and cannot receive color.
- Avoid special fills with more than one color (gradients, shadows): they override any multicolor or hierarchical data defined afterward.
- Preserve the same number of paths, in the same order, between design variants: it is a mandatory requirement to generate coherent multicolor or hierarchical data; the text shows the example of two drawings with four paths each, but in a different order, which makes the color of the "badge" (green circle with "plus") get applied wrong, to the folder instead of the "+" sign.
- For interpolable symbols (variable setup), avoid adding or removing points when adjusting Ultralight-Small and Black-Small: only reposition existing points, copied from Regular-Small, to maintain point-to-point compatibility.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Layer annotation: a "layer" is a collection of paths with associated rendering data; layers have an explicit z-order (the ones on top hide the ones below).
- Multicolor mode: each layer receives a color (system colors are recommended, which adapt to light/dark/high-contrast/vibrancy mode); the same path can belong to more than one layer (e.g.: use a layer with reduced opacity to simulate an outline/fill effect).
- Hierarchical mode: layers receive the primary, secondary or tertiary group; a "clear behind" toggle per layer controls whether transparent layers blend with the ones below (default) or "clear" what's behind them, avoiding unwanted visible overlap.
- Palette mode: uses the same hierarchical data; with two colors, the first goes to the primary group and the second to tertiary; with three colors, each one goes to its corresponding group (primary, secondary, tertiary).
- Interpolation requirements: three design sources (Ultralight-Small, Regular-Small, Black-Small) with the same number and order of paths, and the same number of points in corresponding paths; interpolation generates any intermediate weight and the remaining 24 variants automatically from just 3 drawn ones.
- Distribution: template 2.0 is a "lossy" conversion (it loses custom annotations and margins), used only for backward compatibility with iOS 14; template 3.0 should not be used as an editing source file in design tools (it should go back to the SF Symbols app to continue editing).

Examples cited: the "Queen.heart" symbol (crown + heart) built from start to finish as the throughline; example of a folder symbol with a badge (circle with "+") to illustrate the problem of divergent path order between variants; "Teacup.circle" cited as an example of a custom symbol imported from earlier versions of the template.

Short quote: no notable literal quote highlighted; content predominantly technical-tutorial.

## Design great actions for Shortcuts, Siri, and Suggestions (id: wwdc2021_10283, 22.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10283/.

Central thesis: an "action" represents a task that a person can complete in an app, and well-designed actions (useful, modular, multimodal, clear and discoverable) allow the app's functionality to be used outside the app, via Shortcuts, Siri and Suggestions, multiplying the possible use cases from a few building blocks.

The design process described by the presenters (Ari and Cameron) follows five principles, each treated as a decision filter:

1. Useful: the action should complete a task that would normally be done inside the app, avoiding bringing the app to the foreground unless it is truly necessary; when information is missing, the action should ask a follow-up question instead of opening the app (opening the app should be the last resort). Immersion criterion: very visual and complex tasks (the example given is customizing a Watch face) do not make good actions; simple, repeatable tasks (switching between already configured faces) do. Frequency criterion: prioritize actions for the most used features (the example compares "follow friends" versus "play music" in Apple Music, prioritizing the second one).

2. Modular: actions should each do one thing, not one monolithic action that does everything. The comparative example uses two fictional task apps, "Dog Do's" (a single, vague action "Clean up old tasks") and "Shortcats" (two combinable actions, "Get tasks" + "Archive", each useful alone or combined). "Get" actions should expose detailed, named outputs (ex.: Reminder, Attachment, Parent task, Deadline, Notes, List) so that other system actions (such as Show Result or Save File) can reuse them. Common recommended action types: Create, Edit, Delete, Get, Thing (lets the person choose a specific item) and Open.

3. Multimodal: the same action must work by tapping/clicking, by voice with Siri, and when configured in the Shortcuts editor. Prompts should always be phrased as a question (ex.: "When is the deadline?") to work both in the compact UI and when spoken by Siri; the negative example is the label "Deadline:", which works visually but makes no sense when Siri reads it aloud. Parameters without a suitable system type can use a custom type with its own UI, called "Dynamic Enumeration" (example cited: the built-in action "Set Playback Destination", which shows visuals of the available audio devices). For permanent actions (delete, purchase), it is recommended to show a preview "snippet" before execution, different from the confirmation snippet afterward.

4. Clear: the "parameter summary" (format introduced in iOS 13) should start with a verb, be written as a sentence with no final punctuation, summarize only the most important parameters (optional parameters stay in the "Options" UI, behind a chevron), and the action title should share as many words as possible with the parameter summary, starting with the same verb. A default value can be offered to avoid an unnecessary question (example: 15 minutes of default breathing in the fictional app "Awebreath"); any parameter can become a question via "Ask Each Time".

5. Discoverable: provide a suggested invocation phrase ("suggested invocation phrase") so that, on tapping "Add to Siri", the shortcut is added instantly with that name; without this suggested phrase, the person has to type the shortcut's own name, a process described as less direct and one that reduces the chance of the shortcut being added and used successfully.

Concrete interface-building techniques: output variables connect actions in the editor (one action uses another's output as a parameter, ex.: the "Cat Tasks" variable feeding the Archive action); the Shortcuts editor shows parameters as editable blue rectangles; the appearance of actions in the editor was redesigned this year to remove app names from the action body, integrate the app icon in line, and make better use of the parameter summary, allowing more actions to fit on the screen.

Examples cited: Calendar app (actions to add, search for and edit events, combined into multi-step shortcuts); Reminders app ("Add New Reminder" action with configurable parameters); Apple Watch app (switching watch faces as a good action, customizing a watch face as a bad action for being immersive); Apple Music (following friends vs. playing music); fictional apps "Dog Do's" and "Shortcats" used throughout the video to illustrate modularity; "Awebreath" (fictional breathing app) to illustrate parameter summary and default value; "Set Playback Destination" (real Shortcuts action) for Dynamic Enumeration; ordering Starbucks coffee cited as an example of voice invocation and of the order preview snippet.

Short quotes: "What makes an action great?" "Great actions are useful, modular, multimodal, clear, and discoverable."

## What's new in SF Symbols (id: wwdc2021_10097, 20.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10097/.

Central thesis: SF Symbols 2021 expands the library to more than 3,000 symbols, broadens coverage of localized scripts and introduces two new rendering modes (Hierarchical and Palette), making symbols a more versatile design tool by systematically integrating typography, vector structure and color.

The design process described by the presenter (Thalia) is more conceptual than step by step, organized around the "anatomy of a symbol": every symbol starts with a vector point, from which straight segments or curves are drawn (Bézier control points); the "design source" is a closed path; when drawing additional compatible fonts at points (same number of vector points), the system interpolates these fonts to generate the intermediate weights and scales. This justifies why closed paths and compatible points are so important: without them there is no interpolation or reliable color annotation.

Principles stated and why:
- Symbols as a language-independent form of communication: they help with quick, intuitive navigation because most do not depend on the user's language.
- Automatic vertical alignment to the cap height of the San Francisco font at all scales and weights, and centered horizontal alignment (some symbols have negative margins for badge variants), ensuring a consistent appearance when different symbols appear side by side.
- The default variant (outline) is the choice "in the absence of context," because it relates typographically to the text (the same imaginary "tool" drawing symbol and text); the fill variant is recommended when more surface area is wanted to saturate with color, as in selection.
- Contextual use of variants: fill in iOS swipe actions and tab bars (consistency and level of emphasis in selection areas); symbol inside a filled circle for better legibility at small sizes; outline in toolbars, navigation bars and lists where symbols appear next to text.
- Layer annotation (primary, secondary, tertiary): shapes that do not touch or that have a gap between the first and second element are considered secondary; shapes that touch and end up encapsulated one inside the other (e.g.: filled circle, screens) become tertiary.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Scales: small, medium and large keep the same size in points, varying only the stroke weight, optically optimized and paired to the weight of the text.
- About 600 new symbols this year, covering Apple products and devices, video game controls, health, communication, "inset" symbols for interface layout and window management, watchOS, and other objects, totaling more than 3,000 symbols.
- Localization: expanded coverage for Arabic, Hebrew and Devanagari, in addition to Thai, Chinese, Japanese and Korean; variants automatically adapt to the device language, including right-to-left systems; in some cases a new script-specific design is necessary because automatic mirroring does not come out correct.
- Hierarchical: uses a single color with varying opacities per layer, reducing visual noise without removing essential elements; it is applied automatically to all hierarchy layers using the base color.
- Palette: uses two to three independent colors, with opacity controls; if only two colors are applied, the tertiary group replicates the secondary group's annotation.
- Multicolor: represents the "intrinsic" or native color of a symbol (example given: the "Add" concept linked to the color green and "Remove" to the color red); a symbol can be fully colored, partially colored (part uses multicolor, part uses the accent color) or, without multicolor data, renders in monochrome.
- Monochrome: opacity applied to the entire symbol without using layer data; it is the most unified mode, indicated when uniformity is a priority.
- Color updates: Brown, previously only available on macOS, now on all platforms; Teal was redefined with a greener tone; Cyan is a new name that inherits Teal's old values (anyone who used Teal needs to switch to Cyan to keep the same appearance); Indigo and Purple got more consistent hue definitions across platforms.

Examples cited: cloud/sun/rain symbol as an example of a symbol with all three hierarchy levels complete; share symbol (arrow + square) as an example of combining two shapes with differentiated emphasis; SF Symbols app (tool) with its color picker and updated color library.

Short quote: "One symbol in nine weights with three scales each in four rendering modes."

## Accessibility by design: An Apple Watch for everyone (id: wwdc2021_10308, 10.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 14 of 14 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10308/.

Note: the id wwdc2021_110142 is the ASL (American Sign Language) version of the same video, with identical text transcript; see entry below.

Central thesis: Apple approaches accessibility on the Apple Watch from four categories (vision, cognitive, motor, hearing) and treats accessibility as part of the product's "core mission" from the start, not as an additional feature; several solutions designed for specific needs (contrast, Taptic Time, Mickey Mouse voices, one-handed gestures) ended up becoming features useful to all users.

The design process described is reported by engineers and designers (not individually named in the transcript, lines attributed by dash) in testimonial/documentary format, not as a formal step-by-step. The recurring method described is: identify the product's main function (for the Watch, reading the time quickly and sometimes discreetly), ask how that specific function can be made accessible for each type of disability, and test with real users from the affected communities (a collaborator with Usher syndrome, a colleague with low muscle tone in the arms) to refine the solution before generalizing it.

Principles stated and why:
- "Accessible by design" (accessible from conception): features designed for a specific need tend to become mainstream over time (example cited: large-type watch face, created for low vision, now available to everyone).
- Testing with real people from the target community reveals needs that the team would not have imagined on its own (example: a user with Usher syndrome who preferred the Watch's small screen because her central vision was more efficient there than on a large screen).
- Gestures should prioritize the lowest possible energy expenditure: when designing complete gestural control for the Watch, the team started with "coarse" gestures (raising the arm, turning the wrist) and moved to minimal gestures (pinch, double pinch, clench, double clench), combining accelerometer, gyroscope and heart rate sensor in a machine learning model to distinguish subtle muscle movements in the arm.
- Accessibility is not just a technical solution: the case of the Mickey Mouse watch face shows that the lack of a spoken voice (only generic text-to-speech) made the experience incomplete for blind or low-vision users, and the response was not an engineering adjustment, but a partnership with Disney to record character voices in more than 30 languages.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Taptic Time: lets people feel the time through haptics, solving the case of a blind user who needed to take her hand off the cane to touch the device; the final solution was to allow raising the arm and hearing the time, without needing to stop or touch the screen.
- High contrast (black background, legible letters) originally designed for quick reading of the time, which also benefits low-vision users.
- Dedicated glyph in SF Symbols to represent all the Apple Watch's hardware and software inputs (accelerometer, gyroscope, blood flow sensor), developed together with the font designers.
- Mickey Mouse voices: recorded in more than 30 languages; the frequency of Mickey's laughs adjusted differently depending on whether VoiceOver is on (more laughs) or off (fewer laughs), calibrated through team testing and discussion.
- Gesture recognition: pinch, double pinch, clench and double clench offer complete access to the Apple Watch without needing another hand to touch the screen.

Examples cited: "large-type" watch face; Taptic Time; Mickey Mouse watch face with localized voices; one-handed gesture feature (pinch/clench) for complete access to the Watch, described as a combination of accelerometer, gyroscope and health sensors processed by machine learning.

Short quotes: "If you can create not just a good accessibility experience, but a great accessibility experience, you'll have a fan for life." "Accessibility is really about allowing more people to use your thing."

## Discoverable design (id: wwdc2021_10126, 32.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 27 of 27 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10126/.

Central thesis: an interface is "discoverable" when a person can look at a screen, before touching anything, and know what they can do and how; this replaces lengthy onboarding tutorials (which the presenters themselves say they skip as users) with five fundamentals applied to the screen's own design.

The design process is illustrated with a fictional example app, "Toasty" (a toast recipe generator), built throughout the video by presenters Jiabao Li and Mylène Dreyer. The method consists of listing all of the app's features, ranking them by importance from the user's point of view (not the developer's), and then deciding which ones remain immediately visible versus which ones may require additional navigation.

The five fundamentals, with principles and whys:

1. Prioritize important features: not all pixels have the same value, and neither do all features. Negative example cited: a "hamburger" menu tested with users showed that, closed, the three lines communicate nothing about the content inside; the team replaced it with a tab bar, which leaves the main features visible right away. Example of the other extreme: a tab bar with six tabs reduces the tappable area of each tab and increases complexity, making it harder to locate information. The recommendation is to decide by frequency of use and centrality of the function (in Toasty: "For You" and "Recipes" stayed in the tab bar; taking a photo was featured at the top of the recipes screen; search stayed visible but farther from the thumb, since it is used less frequently; personal preferences went to the profile icon).

2. Use visual cues based on what the audience already knows: first understand who the audience is (Toasty is for "toast lovers" in general, not professional chefs, so the tolerance for learning the app is low) and what these people already know, whether from real-world physical experience (a toast icon recognizable by the shape of the bread) or from common digital patterns (a chevron in the top left corner to go back, already conventional on iOS). Text labels must accompany ambiguous icons (an icon that could be "book" or "newspaper" needs a caption). The "blank page problem": an empty search bar, with no examples, creates indecision; the solution was to suggest search examples (by ingredient, place, friends). Animation and interactivity also teach in context: in the feature that scans toast with the camera, the outline on screen changes appearance and there is haptic feedback at the right moment to take the photo, instead of upfront instructions in onboarding.

3. Hint at gestures: always use the platform's standard gesture; if it is necessary to invent a new gesture, it must imitate a real-world interaction (example cited: Apple Pencil Scribble, where scratching out text to delete it reproduces the physical scratching-out gesture). Gestures must be a shortcut, never a substitute: there must always be a primary, legible way (a button) to perform the same action, because gestures are invisible by nature. Animated transitions must reinforce the direction of the gesture (example: tapping the back chevron triggers the same downward slide animation that the swipe down gesture would, teaching the gesture by association); additional visual cues (toast thumbnails on the sides) signal that it is possible to drag sideways between recipes.

4. Organize by behavior: group content into categories that match people's real motivation and behavior, not a technical taxonomy. In Toasty, categorizing by ingredient was discarded for not scaling (too many ingredients); the team chose categories like "restaurants" and "friends" (how people discover toast in real life) plus a personalized section called "Tasty Picks", powered by a machine learning recommendation engine. Categories must be visually reinforced with clear divisions and headers, so the person can skip one section and explore another.

5. Convey a sense of control over personalized content: offer explicit feedback (an intentional action by the person, such as rating an item) clearly about the consequence. The example shows three iterations tested with users: (a) a heart icon alone was misread as "add to favorites"; (b) the thumbs-up/thumbs-down pair (iOS's standard for recommendation feedback) still caused confusion about whether the action was public or private; (c) the text label "Suggest toast like this" solved it, because "suggest" is associated with recommendation engines. It is also recommended to disclose implicit feedback (the reason a recommendation appears, such as "Because you added avocado toast") and to allow editing/correcting recommendations with an easy-to-find action (in the final example, a green text button "edit suggestion" offering "Suggest Less" and "Stop Suggesting", preferred over hiding that option behind a "More Options" menu).

Concrete interface-building techniques: tab bar instead of a hamburger menu for main features; capture button featured at the top of the recipes screen; camera outline that changes state (active/inactive) as it recognizes toast, with haptic feedback at the ideal photo moment; swipe down as a shortcut to go back (with an explicit button also present); pinch to show more recipes (the same pattern as the Photos app); double tap to like versus single tap, coexisting with an explicit "like" icon so as not to confuse it with the zoom pattern of Photos/Maps.

Examples cited: Photos app (pinch to show more, swipe down to go back); Maps app (double tap to zoom); social networks (double tap to like); Apple Pencil Scribble (scratching out to delete).

Short quote: "Your app doesn't get a second chance at a first impression."

## Design for spatial interaction (id: wwdc2021_10245, 18.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 17 of 17 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10245/.

Central thesis: spatial interactions between two or more devices (enabled by the U1 chip and new iOS 15 APIs) remove layers of abstraction from the interface, allowing the person to interact directly with the physical environment around them, as long as the design considers distance and skill, offers continuous feedback, and embraces physical action.

The design process is presented by a team (Peter Tsoi and colleagues Arian Behzadi, Taylor Carrigan, Linus Persson and Pedro Mari) through real case studies (AirTag, HomePod mini, Share Sheet) instead of an abstract generic methodology; each case study illustrates one of the three central principles.

Principles stated and why:

1. Consider distance and skill: the design must adapt to the scale of the interaction. In the AirTag example, the same button that shows direction on the map when the item is far becomes, as the person gets closer, a more precise guide; the tolerance angle for considering that the iPhone is "facing" the AirTag is more generous at a distance and narrower near the target, because the team found that it is hard to hold an exact direction from far away. When within arm's reach, the visual arrow gives way to haptic feedback, considered more effective at that small scale.

2. Provide continuous feedback: spatial interactions need constant feedback (visual, audible and haptic) because the device becomes a more literal extension of the body. In the HomePod mini's case, two distance limits (zones) around the speaker trigger progressive feedback: the first zone invites the person to move closer or cancel, the second confirms and transfers the music; the banner's position, scale and background blur on the screen change fluidly and proportionally to the hand's real distance, and the haptic increases in intensity the closer it gets. The body's natural withdrawal serves as a cancel gesture, with no need for an extra button. In the AirTag's case, the points on the interface rotate smoothly as the person turns the phone, form an arrow upon connecting, reinforced by subtle haptic and sound; a point that pulses before the arrow speeds up its pulse as the person gets closer, and disappears (arrow "disassembles") if the person moves too far away, signaling loss of signal.

3. Embrace physical action: replace abstract lists of options (negative example cited: the AirPlay list, which shows all devices equally, near or far) with a direct bodily interaction (bringing the iPhone close to the HomePod to transfer the music, without opening any list on the screen); the HomePod's own light modulates intensity as it gets closer, and the final confirmation is the sound itself playing on the speaker.

Concrete interface-building techniques: large, bold typography for text read "at the periphery" during a physical task, citing as precedents the Calculator (large digits for checking a restaurant bill), the TV Remote (large touch area, attention directed at the TV, not the remote) and turn-by-turn navigation (next command text larger and bolder than other notifications); in the AirTag's case, the type size used to show distance is cited as "much larger" than in other parts of the system, with the entire UI revolving around a large central arrow; sound used to reinforce key moments (e.g.: when aligned to the correct direction) but avoided in the final search phase within arm's reach, where haptic alone works better.

Examples cited: Share Sheet (introduced in iOS 13, prioritizes sharing suggestions based on who is nearby, using Bluetooth and Wi-Fi on all devices, with additional priority on iPhones with the U1 chip); AirTag (location with visual, audible and haptic guidance according to distance); HomePod mini (music transfer by proximity); Calculator, TV Remote and turn-by-turn navigation (cited as precedents for typography legible at the periphery).

Short quote: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted as central; the content is driven by description of interface behavior.

## Design for Group Activities (id: wwdc2021_10184, 8.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 7 of 7 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10184/.

Central thesis: the Group Activities framework connects an app to SharePlay using FaceTime's network infrastructure, and designing a good shared experience involves discovering which activities in the app make sense to share, enhancing them for the context of a call, giving context about what is being shared, and automating entry into the experience.

The design process described by the presenter (Ian Baird, manager of the FaceTime team): first discover, within the app, which activities are "things people love to do together" (the text notes that, before SharePlay, most apps were designed around a single person); then enhance these activities for sharing using three tools (screen sharing, synchronized media playback, and the Group Activities messaging protocol for custom data); next make the experience contextual; finally, automate the path to the activity to minimize interaction during the call.

Principles stated and why:
- When sharing the screen, everything is shared by default, except secure entry fields (passwords); it is possible to use UIKit APIs to further restrict the visibility of sensitive information during screen sharing on iOS.
- Audio is shared automatically, but protected content (music, movies) is not transmitted via screen sharing; for that it is necessary to implement Group Activities, which synchronizes playback (simultaneous start, speed changes, fast-forward/rewind) without transmitting the media from one device to another (each device plays its own copy in sync).
- The messaging protocol (Messenger) is private and end-to-end encrypted, like the FaceTime call itself, and it serves to drive custom UI when the app does not fit screen sharing or media.
- Context: when launching the app during a call, immediately show whether it supports SharePlay; when sharing content, display an "activity preview" with title, subtitle and thumbnail (like a rich link), with text that clearly corresponds to the action underway, so that everyone understands what they are about to watch/do.
- Automation and minimizing interaction: since the person is multitasking during a call, the app should, whenever possible, open automatically in the background via Picture in Picture; when some interaction is indispensable (e.g., login, subscription), the app should request foreground presentation via API, but with restraint, because the other people on the call are left waiting; every interaction should be questioned for its real necessity, and deferred or automated when possible.

Concrete interface-building techniques: continuous upgrade from screen sharing to rich media playback when starting SharePlay; if the app is not installed, tapping the notice takes the person to the App Store to install it; SharePlay button inside the video content itself to start sharing; drawing custom views (as in the example given of a colleague drawing "Hello World" collaboratively) via the messaging protocol.

Examples cited: TV app (screen sharing to discover what to watch together); Music app (sharing favorite songs and artists); fictional scenario of watching a house for sale over FaceTime with a friend using screen sharing.

## Design for Safari 15 (id: wwdc2021_10029, 33.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 27 of 27 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10029/.

Central thesis: Safari 15 removes the browser's traditional frame (a tall toolbar, separate from the content) and extends each site's design to every edge of the window, giving web developers new tools (theme-color, aspect-ratio, safe area environment variables, rich links, visual intelligence) to design that experience with intention.

The design process described by the presenter (Jen Simmons) and her colleague (Myles, who demonstrates live with a fictional site called "Dog Dog DC") follows the logic of "what the developer can control and how": first understand how the tab bar color is automatically chosen by Safari when the site defines nothing (sometimes it uses the page's background color, sometimes the header's); then show how to take control of that via HTML; then solve, with a practical example, the problems that come up in practice (a gray newsletter bar interrupting the color flow, the color not changing when entering Theater Mode).

Principles stated and why:
- Put the theme color in the HTML head, not only in the web manifest: the browser can apply it immediately on page load, even before downloading other resources, because it is simple declarative code.
- Support Light and Dark Mode with two theme-color meta tags, each with the corresponding media attribute (prefers-color-scheme: light / dark); put the fallback value first, not last, because browsers that do not support the media attribute use the first declaration and ignore the rest.
- Safari refuses to apply theme colors that harm the experience or accessibility (the example shows Safari forcing white when the chosen color "gets in the way too much"); if the chosen color is not being applied, the advice is to adjust it, not insist on it.
- Tab title: put the content title first and the site name after, because tab space is limited and this helps the person identify which tab is which when there are several tabs of the same site open.
- Favicon icon with a transparent background and high resolution, because, with many tabs open, the favicon becomes the main visual identifier of each tab.

Concrete interface-building techniques:
- Theme meta tag: `<meta name="theme-color" content="...">` in the HTML head, with `media="(prefers-color-scheme: light)"` and a second equivalent tag for dark; it can also be defined in the web manifest, but the color in the HTML head takes priority if both exist; as of June 2021, there was still no way to declare different colors for light/dark in the web manifest.
- JavaScript to change the theme color dynamically in reaction to user behavior (demonstrated when entering and leaving Theater Mode in the Dog Dog DC example), manipulating the meta element directly.
- CSS aspect-ratio: a new property that defines a preferred ratio for any element (e.g.: `aspect-ratio: 1 / 1` for a square); if the content does not fit, the box grows by default; to force the ratio even with overflow, min-height: 0 is used, or overflow: scroll to handle the excess another way; it can be written as a single number (`aspect-ratio: 1`) equivalent to "over 1"; especially useful for embedded video iframes (YouTube, Vimeo, Wistia), which previously required padding hacks or JavaScript to keep a responsive ratio.
- CSS environment variables for safe area: `env(safe-area-inset-top/right/bottom/left)` provide, in pixels, the distance from the safe area to the edge of the viewport; they work like the var() function for custom properties, but the value is set by the browser, not the developer; they can be used inside calc() and in any length property (border, margin, padding, line-height); on iOS, the tab bar sits at the bottom of the screen (to stay under the thumb) and minimizes when the page scrolls, so using `padding-bottom: 1rem + env(safe-area-inset-bottom)` keeps fixed footer content from being covered by the bar.
- `viewport-fit=cover` in the viewport meta tag allows content to extend to the edges of the screen (including the sensor housing area when the iPhone is in landscape); when using this, the developer takes on the responsibility of applying safe area margins manually so as not to lose content under the rounded area of the screen.
- Rich links (Open Graph protocol): meta tags to define title, image (ideally the "hero" image of the page content) and even video (which can autoplay without sound, with a tap to enable audio) when a link is shared, for example, in Messages.
- Visual intelligence: recognizes text inside images (e.g.: a screenshot on Twitter) and allows that text to be selected, copied, translated and searched as if it were normal page text; the recognized text is injected into the image's Shadow DOM (it is not affected by JavaScript, but it is affected by z-order: a transparent layer placed over the image prevents recognition); the session stresses that this does not replace good accessibility practices: text that is mostly textual should continue to be real HTML, and images still need alternative text (alt text), because not every browser has visual intelligence.
- Flex gaps: support for the gap property in Flexbox containers (since Safari 14.1) for minimum spacing between items without needing margins on the outer edges.
- New form controls redesigned in iOS and iPadOS 15 (including in Dark Mode), a Date and Time control on macOS (since Safari 14.1) with built-in client-side validation, and a new color picker with three panels plus an eyedropper for `input type="color"`.

Examples cited: fictional site "Dog Dog DC" (dog video streaming), used live by Myles to demonstrate theme-color, removal of a newsletter bar that broke the color flow, adjusting theme-color when entering Theater Mode, and the use of safe-area-inset-bottom to keep the comments area from covering the tab bar when scrolling.

Short quote: no isolated literal quote of up to 15 words was highlighted as central; the content is predominantly technical and demonstrative (code and browser behavior).

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

- SF Symbols appears as a central, recurring system in Apple's visual language in 2021, covered in three complementary videos from the same year (ids wwdc2021_10097, wwdc2021_10349, wwdc2021_10250): the symbol's vector anatomy (compatible points, closed paths, interpolation) is taught once in depth (10097 and 10250) and then applied at the API/SwiftUI layer (10349), showing a deliberate separation between "how the symbol system works" and "how to use it in code".

- Accessibility is treated, in these videos, not as a separate category of features, but as a design principle that runs through nearly every topic: it appears explicitly in content inclusivity (id wwdc2021_10275), in the game controller framework (id wwdc2021_10081, input remapping and customization), in the Apple Watch case (ids wwdc2021_10308 and wwdc2021_110142, also published in an ASL version), and it is one of the six official categories of that year's Apple Design Award (id wwdc2021_113). The pattern described repeatedly is that solutions designed for a specific accessibility need end up adopted by the whole audience (Taptic Time, the large-type watch face, QuickPath Swipe Keyboard cited in 10275, tab bars with an accessible Label by default in 10349).

- Several videos describe a design methodology centered on iterative testing with real users and adjusting labels/icons based on confused reactions: the case of the heart/thumbs-up icon for recommendation feedback in Toasty (id wwdc2021_10126) and the case of the Apple Watch accessibility gestures tested with a colleague with low muscle tone (id wwdc2021_10308) show the same logic of prototyping, showing it to real people, and iterating until the label communicates the action's real consequence.

- A pattern of "modularity over monolith" appears in different domains: Shortcuts actions should be small and combinable rather than a single action that does everything (id wwdc2021_10283); custom symbols must preserve a consistent path structure across variants to allow color and hierarchy recombination (id wwdc2021_10250); and app onboarding itself is discouraged in favor of cues spread through the interface, discovered in context (id wwdc2021_10126).

- The group mixes videos strictly about interface design with videos of a different nature within the same conference catalog: the awards ceremony (id wwdc2021_113) and the biographical account about NASA (id wwdc2021_10317) show that WWDC uses the same technical video channel for institutional and representation content, without necessarily teaching interface technique in these two cases.
