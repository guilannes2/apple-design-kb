## How to Pick a Custom Font (id: wwdc2017_815, 14.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/815/.

Central thesis: choosing a custom font requires answering two questions (what is the intended use and what impression you want to make) and understanding two typography concepts, structure and contrast, instead of choosing by name or by fashion.

The design process Apple describes: the speech starts from calligraphy to explain structure (the path the writing tool travels, also called ductus) and contrast (the modulation of stroke weight, demonstrated with a pointed pen). It compares two structures of lowercase "n", one cursive with a single stroke and another built with several strokes, to show how high or low contrast changes readability. After fixing the vocabulary, the presenter teaches how to evaluate display versus text by comparing Big Caslon (high contrast, meant for large sizes) with Adobe Caslon Pro (lower contrast, looser spacing, meant for reading at small size). Finally, it addresses style pairing as an exercise in conscious practice: first decide the impression you want to convey, then choose the font that activates that cultural association, and only then adjust proportions.

Principles stated and why: for reading text, type contrast should scale well at small size and spacing should stay comfortable, avoiding extremes of weight or style, because "a good reading experience is usually the one you don't notice". For display there is more freedom of weight and style, but spacing still matters, because a headline usually works better with tighter spacing. Choosing a font just because its name matches the app's theme is called LTypI (Lack of Typographic Imagination) and is treated as a bad shortcut, because the choice should come from the typographic style and not from the label.

Concrete interface-building techniques: point size is defined by the height of the "font body", the virtual box where the glyphs are drawn; since this box varies from design to design, two fonts at the same point size can appear to have different scales, so the recommended technique is to place the candidates side by side at the same point size and choose the ones with the most similar proportion, avoiding having to readjust sizes afterward. For pairing text and display, the strategies described in order of intensity are: keep the same typeface varying weight; change the typeface while keeping typographic contrast strong enough to be obvious; or use the system font (SF) as a "typographic workhorse" for the text together with a strong display style.

Examples cited: Big Caslon and Adobe Caslon Pro, to show how contrast and spacing affect legibility at scale. Cochin combined with Snell Roundhand, to show how structure and contrast create an impression of sophistication (elegant, whimsical, elaborate, precious). A fictional sign painter typeface chosen for a sign painters' conference app, used as an example of LTypI. A dictionary app that looks for a serif with proportions close to those of the main text, to introduce typographic hierarchy without needing to readjust point sizes.

Literal quotes: "A good reading experience is usually the one you don't notice." "We call that an LTypI, or Lack of Typographic Imagination."

## Communication Between Designers and Engineers (id: wwdc2017_809, 9.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/809/.

Central thesis: friction between design and engineering drops with four concrete practices, shared terminology, a single source of truth, careful focus on use cases and accessibility, and demonstrating instead of just describing.

The design process Apple describes: the talk proposes team activities for each practice. For terminology, run a 30-minute "app postmortem" focused only on terms, turned into a Pictionary-style game, or produce a report showing breakdowns caused by confusion over terms; use developer.apple.com, both the Human Interface Guidelines and the API Reference, as the standard vocabulary reference. For careful focus, write down, together with the team or client, all the intended use cases and the edge cases, and only then ask whether it is worth the effort of customizing the UI instead of using ready-made SDK patterns. For showing rather than telling, schedule short face-to-face sessions to run prototypes together with the other side of the team, and schedule feedback checks in context before the code check-in.

Principles stated and why: common terminology eliminates the "mental translation" each person does when translating the other's term, and increases credibility with the team and with the client. A single source of truth avoids the situation of pulling mockups from a server, strings from a wiki and assets exchanged by last-minute email, without knowing which is the correct version. Starting from familiar SDK patterns (the speech uses the metaphor of cooking in a nonstick pan before moving on to a tandoor) frees up time to stabilize the implementation and already ensures compatibility with system accessibility. Static specs leave mental gaps that generate frustration on both sides; video or an interactive prototype communicates intent and still opens conversation about constraints and next steps.

Concrete interface-building techniques: the talk corrects four terms used inconsistently between teams, the correct one is UI Alert Controller or, more currently, just Alert (not pop-up, popover or modal); Navigation Bar (not header or top tool bar); Tool Bar (not drawer or bottom navigation bar); Switch (not toggle or on-off slider). As an example of dynamic demonstration, it describes an animation spec for the full-screen transition in the Notes app sketch, including graph curves, related parameter and value pairs, the exact API reference, and a video of the design animation.

Examples cited: prototyping tools recommended for designers, Principle, Flinto and Apple Keynote itself. To gain empathy with the engineering side, it suggests opening Xcode without needing to code, using the asset catalog to see how the app's icons will be implemented, and using storyboards (including importing static mockups) to create interactive flows and see how UIKit already delivers ready-made states and properties for elements like the table view accessory.

Literal quotes: "Or rather show more than tell." "Using the same tool helps build empathy, keeps you informed, and thereby build credibility."

## Get Started with Display P3 (id: wwdc2017_821, 10.1 min)

Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/821/.

Central thesis: Display P3 is an RGB color space 25 percent larger than sRGB, with its own tagging and profile conversion workflow, and should be adopted selectively according to the app's content, not by default.

The design process Apple describes: first ensure color management, which already comes built into macOS and iOS and prevents colors from being represented incorrectly between the source and the device; to do this the designer needs to tag the assets with the correct color profile. Then, decide whether the app should use Display P3 by explicitly asking "should I?", since the color space works well for certain types of content and not for others.

Principles stated and why: Display P3 uses 25 percent more color spectrum than sRGB, so it represents more colors with more fidelity to what exists in real life; a color space larger than sRGB is informally called wide gamut. Working in Display P3 also allows using a color depth of 16 bits per channel instead of the usual 8 bits, which reduces banding in gradients by closing the distance between each defined color. The recommendation to work on a wide gamut display exists because, without it, the designer is "flying blind" and doesn't actually see the colors they are producing.

Concrete interface-building techniques: the late 2015 iMac Retina was the first consumer computer with Display P3 support, and today (at the time of the talk) the iPhone 7, the MacBook Pro with Touch Bar and the iPad Pro also support it; a recommended working display is the MacBook Pro with Touch Bar, the iMac Retina or the LG5K Display. Photoshop workflow, described step by step: create a new document already with the Display P3 color profile; change color depth from 8 to 16 bits per channel; when saving, use Save As in PNG, because it's the only way to embed the color profile and keep the color depth (the Generator doesn't embed the profile and leaves the asset untagged); when opening raw files, tag them for Display P3 and 16 bits; when converting an asset that already exists (like an app icon), always use "Convert to Profile", which does a color match without changing the design, and never "Assign to Profile", which stretches the existing colors and changes the design. As a numeric example, a pure red in sRGB (255, 0, 0) gains more available red amplitude after being converted to Display P3, and the RGB values change.

Examples cited: a landscape image with reds, greens and oranges, showing colors that existed in the camera capture but fell outside sRGB and appear fully in Display P3. Recommended cases for Display P3: UI details that need to grab attention, like a VU meter or the red line of a tachometer; smoothing an icon gradient; apps with a lot of nature, landscape or travel imagery; textile or apparel apps, where the real color of the piece matters. Not recommended case: apps with a calm, desaturated color scheme, with little imagery.

Literal quotes: "Take a second and ask the question, should I?" "Makes sure to tag your assets properly."

## Express Yourself! (id: wwdc2017_820, 8.6 min)

Basis: transcript and 7 of 7 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/820/.

Central thesis: iMessage apps that are very different from each other, from Stickers to sharing and collaboration apps, work well when they share three qualities, they are convenient, personal and fun.

The design process Apple describes: instead of trying to fit the entire app inside Messages, the recommendation is to extract only the slice of the experience that is already conversational by nature and carve it out as an iMessage app. The speech shows this case by case, the Tumblr GIF generator came from a photo-sharing app, the Dunkin Donuts gift cards came from an app full of functions (rewards, menu, orders), and the Tinder poll came from the "Stacks" image function of the dating app.

Principles stated and why: convenience, because the flow needs to be fast, light and easy, like the expandable App drawer that switches between half screen and full screen to find the right sticker. Personality, because the person customizes what they send, whether choosing color, font, scale and rotation of a GIF, or choosing the right gift card, or sharing their own images in a poll. Fun, because sending and receiving this kind of content needs to be enjoyable in itself, "handcrafted messages are fun to send and receive".

Concrete interface-building techniques: Stickers interaction described step by step, tap and hold to "peel" the sticker, position, scale, rotate, drop it onto an existing bubble (something that was not possible before Stickers), and optionally send with a full-screen effect. In the Tumblr GIF, the controls cited are playback speed, text color, font, scale and rotation. Platform resources cited as material available for this type of app: peel-and-place stickers, the Apple Pay sheet (allows completing a financial transaction without leaving Messages) and access to microphone, camera and photos.

Examples cited: Apple's own Stickers, including animated ones, as the basis of the argument; the Tumblr GIF generator, which teaches how video trimming, playback speed and text customization make a robust editor lightweight inside Messages; the Dunkin Donuts gift card (the US$5 example sent via the Apple Pay sheet), which teaches how a function for giving something physical becomes a personal gesture inside the conversation; the Tinder image poll via "Stacks", which teaches how to turn a group decision (which sneakers to wear) into something simpler than sending loose images and asking for votes in text.

Literal quotes: "Handcrafted messages are fun to send and receive." "Being kind is fun."

## Designing for a Global Audience (id: wwdc2017_819, 9.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 7 of 7 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/819/.

Central thesis: design for a global audience is not an all-or-nothing problem; targeted, well-prioritized changes to language, symbols and cultural associations already make an app more useful to more people.

The design process Apple describes: first make a plan, defining strategic goals, which countries and languages enter the roadmap, and checking iTunes Connect app analytics for information that is already available and sometimes surprising. Then, identify user groups and decide whether small adjustments are enough or whether it is time for a serious localization effort, preparing translations of key terms in advance and synchronizing the production of alternative artwork when necessary.

Principles stated and why: when there are terms that are practically interchangeable in the app's main language, it is worth choosing the one that translates more similarly across languages (the example given is "picture" versus "photo", much closer across languages than "picture" alone). Instructions, titles and error messages need to stay clear even when they are not fully translated. Slang and idiomatic expressions give the app character but require checking that they do not offend and, if used, need context clues that help those who do not understand the literal meaning. Symbols and gestures vary culturally, so the relevant question is how specific versus universal the symbol should be; detailed icons of people can exclude part of the audience, while simple silhouettes tend to be more general (and a glyph of this type already comes included in UIKit). Cultural associations also change the meaning of a symbol even without words.

Concrete interface-building techniques: an example of iconography from the Maps app to represent a post office, the Japan Post logo is used only in Japan; the postal horn is used in Sweden, Turkey, Luxembourg and 28 other countries; in all other countries a simple envelope silhouette is used, illustrating a spectrum from the most local to the most global iconography. An example of cultural association, the owl evokes wisdom in the English-speaking world but can evoke bad luck or death in much of the Arab world. At WWDC itself that year, 75 different countries were represented, cited as context for the real size of this audience.

Examples cited: the Maps app and its three levels of postal iconography (Japan, 29 countries with the postal horn, the rest of the world); the exercise of counting to three with fingers, used to show the variation of gestures across cultures.

Suggested resources: traveling to where customers live when possible; formal focus groups (selected by language, culture or other characteristics) or informal ones (friends and family trading feedback over snacks); web research on language learning, translation and travel; books and articles on global design and cross-cultural communication; libraries with collections of maps and documents.

Literal quotes: "This is not an all or nothing design problem." "Smart changes can go a long way."

## Rich Notifications (id: wwdc2017_817, 10.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 7 of 7 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/817/.

Central thesis: a well-designed notification is a self-contained package of information, structured into Short Look, Long Look and Quick Actions, that lets the person solve the task without needing to open the app.

The design process Apple describes: first of all, decide whether the notification is worth sending, sending it only when it is relevant and never to inflate traffic or engagement; when asking for permission to notify, clearly explain what the notifications will be about and what value they bring. From there, the recommended structure has three layers with one question each, the Short Look answers "what is this about" using the API's correct fields and labels (updated in iOS 11 at the time), the Long Look, accessed with 3D Touch, delivers relevant additional context, and the Quick Actions at the end offer actions related to the content, so the person can act and go back to what they were doing.

Principles stated and why: notifications should not be treated as just a hook to open the app, because, if the interaction already resolves something on its own, it creates a real bond between the person and the app; sending too much or without value has a real cost, the person can turn off notifications or delete the app.

Concrete interface-building techniques: if the content is truncated in the Short Look, show the rest in the Long Look (Mail example, which shows sender, subject and preview, and expands to the full message). Adding an image does not necessarily mean a photo, it can be the app's own custom UI, its own typography, unique brand elements, charts and flow diagrams for apps with data, conditions in a graph for weather apps, or stock charts for financial apps. If UI animation is a central part of the app, it can appear in the notification (Tips example playing a tip video in the Long Look); if traditional video is part of the app (news clips, sports highlights), it can also be included. A Long Look can also have a custom view that updates in real time (maps, delivery, flight tracking, stock prices, sports scores, social feeds). At the limit, the notification can become fully custom, as in the Messages example, which draws the conversation itself with bubbles, effects, images, attachments and a live "typing" indicator, making it possible to reply directly from there.

Examples cited: Calendar (Long Look shows the day's detail and availability, with a Quick Action to accept the invite without opening the app); Photos (thumbnail in the Short Look, large photo in the Long Look); Tips (video played in the Long Look); a hypothetical turn-by-turn game (show the last move by whoever played); Missed Call and Voicemail (listen to the audio or read the transcript without opening Phone); Find My Friends (map that updates live); Messages (full conversation rendered, typing indicator, inline reply); Mic (news story with large photo, title and full text); Castro (podcast: title, duration, description and thumbnail typeset like the app); Kuna, smart door camera (video played in the Long Look and Quick Action "Can I help you?").

Literal quotes: "A self-contained package of information that allows you to complete a specific task." "If your app updates in real time, your notifications should update in real time."

## 60-Second Prototyping (id: wwdc2017_818, 10.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 10 of 10 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/818/.

Central thesis: prototyping fast, with the tools already used day to day and not with code or static visuals, makes it possible to test and discard ideas before committing real development time.

The design process Apple describes: the cycle is "make prototypes, show them to people, learn from the feedback", repeated continuously until you have confidence that you are building the right thing. Anyone can prototype by repurposing the tool they already use at work, developers with Xcode and Swift or HTML, CSS and JavaScript, designers with Sketch or Illustrator, and people who neither code nor design with Keynote or even a word processor; the rule is, if the tool displays images and responds to interaction, it can be used to prototype.

Principles stated and why: prototypes serve to test ideas, some seem great and are not, others seem weak and hide valuable insight, and this saves time and money by ensuring that code and assets are only committed to what is really worth it; prototypes also serve to generate new ideas, because the very act of showing something to people brings new input. When collecting feedback, the rule is not to argue, defend or dismiss what the person says, and to always test in context, on the real device, in a realistic situation, because "you can't test things in a vacuum".

Concrete interface-building techniques: a demonstration of how to build, in under 60 seconds, a timer prototype in Keynote using only basic shapes (not refined assets); the trick cited to simulate the hand spinning is to copy and paste the hand itself, move the copy to the bottom and make it invisible, group the two and then rotate the group, since Keynote rotates around the center; the animation goes to the 5-second mark on the first tap with no easing (to look like a real clock), then to the 10-second mark, and finally spins back to the 0 mark, with the Start button disappearing when the animation begins. After testing with people, the iteration applied was, the Start button became a larger tap area (the entire bottom part of the screen) because it was too small, the hands turned bold red and white for contrast, and a final "Timer Done" screen was added.

Examples cited: a colleague testing the timer prototype to measure the pH of a water sample, used as a test in the context of a simple situation; a friend (David) who used to be good at solving the Rubik's cube testing whether he could still do it in under 10 seconds, whose feedback revealed three concrete problems, the Start button was small and hard to tap, there was no countdown when pressing Start for the person to get in position, and there was no way to show the exact time to compare with the personal record; other timer prototypes made in Keynote for specific use cases, developing photographic film, grilling a steak, and a prototype made by a colleague to help his own son brush his teeth.

Literal quotes: "You've just learned something very valuable about your app." "Don't argue, defend, or dismiss the feedback you get."

## Design Tips for Great Games (id: wwdc2017_811, 13.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 11 of 11 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/811/.

Central thesis: ten design tips, from first launch to accessibility, put the player's experience first even in elements that seem like mere gameplay support.

The design process Apple describes, tip by tip: (1) First Launch, design the first screen within the game's visual style even when it needs to display Accept Terms or technical notices. (2) Loading, use loading time to entertain or educate, and always prefer a progress bar over an activity indicator because it communicates better what is happening and how much is left. (3) Quick Start, let the person play quickly, without requiring choices whose meaning they don't understand yet, and keep the main call-to-action (play, start, battle) as the most dominant element even in busier menus. (4) Interface, seek consistency (predictable menu layout, tested screen by screen side by side) and clarity (tappable items must look visually different from non-tappable ones, and every control needs three visual states, Normal, Press and Disabled, because hiding a disabled control confuses when it reappears). (5) to (7) Tutorials, teach gradually through progressive disclosure, structuring the tutorial in three basic parts aligned with the game's core loop, without overloading it with what isn't essential (upgrades, tournaments, store come later), giving short and clear direction (including through a guide character) and never repeating an instruction already given. (8) Skip, reveal a discreet Skip button on tap and always allow the tutorial to be relaunched after being skipped. (9) implicit in the closing of the teaching tip, prefer teaching through the game itself over using a bouncing arrow or floating hand, which make the person "play the tap-the-arrow game" without actually learning and still hide the interface. (10) Inclusion, incorporate Closed Captions, Dynamic Type, left- and right-handed mode, and color blind mode.

Principles stated and why: consistency improves usability because it helps the person mentally map the interface and spend energy playing instead of looking for where the Settings button is. Clarity keeps the person from having to "think too much" about what is tappable, which would pull them out of immersion in the game. Teaching in context and through interaction works better than prior instruction because it uses the act of playing itself as the lesson. Giving the Skip option respects that the person has different skill levels and different amounts of time available.

Concrete interface-building techniques: minimum tap targets of 44 by 44 points on iOS for any interactive element. A numeric example of accessibility, the app Blackbox was cited as a reference for offering a sonic interface for people with low vision or no vision, in a context where the speech cites 285 million blind or low-vision people worldwide.

Examples cited: Pokemon Go (edge to edge illustration on the first screen); Don't Starve Pocket Edition (animation that disguises the loading); Clash Royale (gameplay tip during loading, without repeating a tip already seen in the same session); INKS, Apple Design Award (only two screens before starting to play, large turquoise Play button); Splitter Critters (teaches the swipe mechanic right on the initial screen); Reigns (removed the floating hand for a more immersive animation); Mars, on Apple TV (learning by trial and error within the game itself); Icycle: On Thin Ice (Skip appears discreetly on tap, preserving the opening sequence for those who want to watch it); Zombie Gunship (Skip Tutorial button visible in the top left corner); Mushroom 11 (left- and right-handed mode); KAMI 2 (color blind mode); Blackbox (sonic interface for low vision).

Literal quotes: "Respect your players, include everyone, and put their experience first." "Kids are smart."

## Love at First Launch (id: wwdc2017_816, 10.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/816/.

Central thesis: the first experience of an app should be as open and frictionless as a store with its facade fully open to the street, leading with content, teaching through interaction, and only asking for permissions once their value is already clear.

The design process Apple describes: the talk uses three analogies to establish the starting point, a landlord who demands a passport, birth certificate and pay stubs before letting someone just look at the apartment (exaggerated and hostile), a fortified military base ("stay out" messaging), and the Apple Union Square in San Francisco, whose entire facade opens onto the sidewalk and invites people to come in and handle the products without obstacle. From there, the recommended process is to lead with content, teach through interaction only when something truly needs prior instruction, and time permission requests for the moment when the benefit is already obvious.

Principles stated and why: a first impression can also be the last one, because the person has access to millions of apps and has already passed the first barrier by installing yours. Before adding any opening instruction, it is worth asking whether it is really necessary, because ideally the interface should be intuitive enough to make it unnecessary (the example given is the Phone app, whose interface is already obvious). Asking for private data permission without context repeats the excessive landlord's behavior; the rule is to make sure the person understands the value of sharing the data before asking, reinforce that value after the permission is granted, and only ask for what is necessary when it is necessary.

Concrete interface-building techniques: a direct comparison between two travel apps, one that opens by asking for registration without showing almost anything of the product, and Jetsetter, which opens with large images and browsable content or a direct search, and only asks for registration once the person is already ready to book the trip, a point at which they have already seen the product's value. Example of teaching through interaction without formal onboarding, Lara Croft GO introduces the swipe mechanic and climbing/descending walls within the game's own first two levels, with no tooltip or text instruction; Streaks teaches that "completing a task fills a ring" by inviting the person to fill rings already during the app's introduction. Example of permission timing, Strava only asks for location access when the person is about to start a ride, only asks for photo access at the moment they tap "add a photo" when saving the ride, and only asks for contacts when they search for friends; Transit, on the other hand, which delivers no value at all without location, asks for the permission right at first launch, but immediately shows the benefit by zooming into the map and listing nearby routes and times as soon as the permission is granted.

Examples cited: Jetsetter versus a second, unnamed travel app, for content-first; the Phone app, for questioning the need for prior instruction; Lara Croft GO and Streaks, for teaching through interaction; Strava and Transit, for the timing of permission requests.

Literal quotes: "First impressions matter." "Always lead with great content."

## Designing Glyphs (id: wwdc2017_823, 11.4 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/823/.

Central thesis: an effective glyph is simplified, conceptually universal and quick to read in context, and a set of glyphs only works well when it is designed to feel like a family, with consistent optical weight, stroke width and positioning.

The design process Apple describes: first differentiating glyph from icon, an icon is a broad set that ranges from simple, low-fidelity shapes to illustrated, colored assets, while glyphs are, in most cases, monochromatic and must be colorized programmatically (the example is the Wallet app icon, colored, versus its monochromatic glyph). Then, a conceptualization exercise to arrive at a universal glyph, trying to represent "delicious": cheeseburger, pizza and ice cream fail because they are culturally specific; an expressive face is relatable but risky, because using a face, mouth or body part can be seen as inappropriate in some cultures; a heart wins for being the most universal association among the options (love as an approximation of "delicious"). Finally, the process of making a set of glyphs "feel like a family," by looking at optical weight, stroke width and positioning.

Principles stated and why: glyphs need to be highly simplified, conceptually universal and quickly legible as to function, because they rarely appear alone, so they need to add up to a coherent system. Optical weight, stroke width and optical (not geometric) positioning are treated as the three axes that make a set of glyphs look designed together.

Concrete interface-building techniques: optical weight correction, when two shapes with less surface area (an ellipse and a narrow shape, in the examples given) end up optically smaller than the rest of the set, the solution is to scale them up instead of constraining them to the same vertical limits as the others. Stroke width correction, normalizing the stroke width across the glyphs in the set, because inconsistent strokes make some look more emphasized than others and break the sense of family. Optical positioning correction, a Play glyph perfectly centered geometrically inside a circle looks, perceptually, off-center, and the adjustment is to move it a few pixels to the right; likewise, a share glyph centered geometrically looks "seated" too low, and the adjustment is to raise it a few pixels; this adjustment can be built into the asset itself, so that, when integrating it, it is enough to center the asset in the destination space. Relationship between typography and glyph, if the text style is light, the glyph's stroke width should match that weight, if the style is heavy or more filled, a heavier or filled glyph works better. For system spaces, such as the Touch Bar on the Mac or the Home screen's quick action menus on iOS, the core resource should be the system's existing assets, and system glyphs tend to be a bit bigger and heavier than their in-app equivalents.

Examples cited: the Wallet app icon and its monochrome glyph, to illustrate the difference between icon and glyph; the Phone app icon, showing that the glyph is part of what makes up the icon; a road sign in Germany pointing to the airport, to show how a universal symbol removes the need to know the local language; Airbnb, cited twice, as an example of glyphs in a list whose line weight matches the font used, and as an example of consistency between the app's custom glyphs and the platform's shared glyphs in the Home screen quick action menu.

Literal quotes: "Build them as sets." "Test them in context, and always preview them on device."

## Writing Great Alerts (id: wwdc2017_813, 11.2 min)

Basis: transcript and 9 of 9 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/813/.

Central thesis: a good alert answers three questions, what happened, why the person is seeing this, and how to proceed, and it should only exist when truly necessary, because alerts are disruptive and lightweight by design.

The design process Apple describes: before creating an alert, ask whether it is really the best way to reach the goal. Correct uses, fix errors; ask for access to the person's data (contacts, location, financial data), using Apple's standard format, which concisely explains what is being requested and how it will be used; notify about truly important updates (relevant features, critical security fixes), sparingly. Incorrect uses, alerts are not settings screens or multiple-choice questions, and if the alert occupies almost the entire viewport it is a sign that part of the content should go to a larger element; alerts are not technical diagnostic tools, they should not expose error codes; alerts are not a "patch" for avoidable problems, like password rules that should be visible on the main screen (with invalid characters blocked while typing) rather than appearing only afterward, as an error.

Principles stated and why: "alerts are disruptive by design", so they should only carry essential content, always asking whether the person really needs to know that, and needs to know it now. "Alerts are lightweight by design", so they should not be overloaded with long text or too many options, tasks like that belong to larger UI elements.

Concrete interface-building techniques: recommended alert structure, the title should state the main point in one sentence or less; the message should bring brief additional information only when necessary (cause of the error, reason for the request); the actions should be easy to understand and execute, and if they take the person out of the app or to another state, it is necessary to navigate there, without leaving that responsibility to the Back or Home button; ideally the title and actions, read alone, are already enough to understand the situation. Two rewrite examples are shown, a generic alert saying "app name cannot open this file", with vague instructions and actions that are hard to understand ("No"/"Yes"), is corrected by specifying the file name, reducing the instruction text and making the actions explicit about what each one does; and an apparently correct alert, "Cancel download? If you cancel now, you will not receive this file.", which is still ambiguous about which file is at stake, and is corrected by including the file name both in the title and in the message. Style and quality, avoid platitudes, jargon and filler; keep formatting, spelling and punctuation correct; consider the audience (the talk cites, as extremes, medical professionals, students with special needs, or "a very smart cat who just learned how to use an iPad").

Literal quotes: "Alerts are disruptive by design." "Alerts are lightweight by design."

## Size Classes and Core Components (id: wwdc2017_812, 8.7 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/812/.

Central thesis: a three-piece framework, size classes, Dynamic Type and standard UIKit components, simplifies designing for every iOS screen size and frees up time for what makes each app unique.

The design process Apple describes: every iOS screen size falls into one of two size classes, compact or regular, narrow width behaves like iPhone, wide width behaves like iPad, and this applies to both width and height, in portrait and landscape. The recommendation is to start the design from the iPhone and only then adapt for the iPad, keeping the same level of navigation hierarchy, toolbars and tab bars between the two layouts.

Principles stated and why: flexible layouts, with elements that adapt to the relative size of the screen, avoid having to redesign each device from scratch; the iPhone 7 Plus in landscape falls into regular width, so it can use Split Views like the iPads, while smaller iPhones in landscape become compact in both width and height, receiving bars shorter than the standard portrait height. Readability margins exist because, if the text runs to the edge of the standard UIKit margin, the lines become too long for the eye to follow; these margins are flexible and vary with text size, smaller text calls for a narrower readability length, larger text accepts a greater length. Dynamic Type exists because a lot of people find the default text too small, and scaling automatically without breaking the layout also makes it easier to enable Accessibility for even larger type sizes, as well as adjusting lettering and line height as localization changes the characters. Using standard UIKit components frees up the designer's time for what really differentiates the app, instead of rebuilding tasks people already know.

Concrete interface-building techniques: in that year's Dynamic Type chart, the weight of the Title sizes was adjusted from light to regular; the recommendation is to use two to three text styles per screen, out of ten styles available in total, with Body as the default style used in the system, from which hierarchy is created with smaller, larger or bolder styles. Custom fonts can also be Dynamic Type (using that year's API), but it may be necessary to assign different point sizes per text style, since not every font has the same proportions, to keep the optical size consistent. TableViews are cited as a component that already scales with Dynamic Type, inherits readability margins and brings ready-made standard interactions, such as swipe actions and edit mode; the components described were scaled for iOS 7, recommended as a starting point for a new design. In the adaptation to iPad with Split View, the left side is called the Master View and the right the Detail View (the same screen that, on iPhone, would require drill-down); apps with multitasking transition between compact and regular as the available screen width changes.

Examples cited: Etsy, shown at the default text size and at a much larger Dynamic Type size, still looking good, as proof that the layout holds up at scale.

Literal quotes: "Will make your app look amazing regardless of what device people are using." "There was only one phone."

## App Icon Design (id: wwdc2017_822, 10.5 min)

Basis: transcript and 8 of 8 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/822/. The duration of 59.9 min is as it appears in the header of the source file.

Central thesis: an effective app icon is born from four principles, metaphor, simplicity, connection and lineage, applied through an iterative process of sketching, testing in context (Home screen, folder, small size) and patience.

The design process Apple describes: the talk revisits classic Mac OS icons to show how simple metaphors survived decades and created an emotional connection with the people who use the system; some were abandoned when the technology they represented (the floppy disk) became obsolete, when they were too aggressive (the bomb), or when they required learning a new concept (the alert hieroglyph, which lost its face over time and became just the exclamation point). From there, it describes the practical process of creation: start with sketching on paper even without knowing how to draw well, because that takes off the weight of "getting it right" too early; show drafts to colleagues and collect reaction; test the icon constantly on the Home screen, inside a folder, and in the Settings app; have patience, keeping iterations, because often the simplest direction, or an idea abandoned at the start, ends up being the one chosen in the end.

Principles stated and why: metaphor, finding an easily identifiable object or graphic mark, to anchor an abstract idea in something recognizable (the detailed example is the classic Mac paint bucket, which evokes pouring real paint, with consistent line thickness, movement implied in the angle, a subtle indication of perspective and good black and white balance). Simplicity, the design needs to be simple and clear, without clutter, to be read quickly. Connection, creating an emotional bond, since the icon is the app's "face" as much as the UI itself. Lineage, changes to the icon should be significant and deliberate, not with every update, because the familiarity accumulated over time is part of the icon's value.

Concrete interface-building techniques: Keynote case, the podium identity has held since version 1 in 2003; the metaphor evokes presenters using podiums for generations, reinforced by details such as a warm wood surface for notes and a microphone; across versions the style migrated from classic to modern, but the idea of the podium remained, and on iOS it was simplified until it became a glyph, keeping visual connection across platforms. App family case (iWork/iLife), Pages uses the pen as a writing metaphor, with the paper indicated by a line on iOS; Numbers uses a flattened 2D representation of a chart and table on iOS; GarageBand and iMovie use a guitar and a staff; the color palette and the metaphor run across the platforms to reinforce the idea of family; finishing details cited include colors that harmonize with iOS's vibrant palette, simple glyphs that share the same visual mass, white and high contrast, with no unnecessary detail, sharing the same "ground plane", with corners drawn in custom curves to stay smooth at large and small sizes. Music Memos case, the circle evokes the sound hole of an acoustic guitar, the strings vibrate in a graphic way, and the circle uses the same iOS icon grid, at the same relative size as, for example, the Safari icon. Case study of the News icon, built from the question "if News were the only app on the Home screen, what would the icon need to communicate to be clear even without the name underneath?": a first sketch with the corner of a sheet of paper was ambiguous (it wasn't possible to tell whether it rested on a newspaper or a white box) and hid a good part of the red background; a second sketch added a second sheet of paper behind it, but visible edges were still missing; the final version shows all the newspaper's edges, high contrast white, visible red background, and a simple, bold and graphic rendering of the logo; the final test was to reduce the three sketches to small size, place them in a folder next to other icons, and squint to check what remained legible.

Examples cited: Quick, Instagram and Periscope, as three video apps with colors and shapes quite distinct from each other, used to argue in favor of being unique within its own category; Instagram is cited specifically as an example of an icon that became synonymous with the brand (the white camera glyph over a gradient background); Clear, cited as a good example of representing the app's own interface inside the icon without losing legibility at small size, because of a strong visual style and recognizable colors.

Literal quotes: "Your icon is the face of your app." "Think of your icon as an investment."

## Essential Design Principles (id: wwdc2017_802, 59.9 min)

Basis: transcript and 44 of 44 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/802/.

Central thesis: Apple prefers the term "human interface" to "user interface" because a well-made design serves the human needs for safety, meaning, fulfillment and beauty, and not abstract goals like "being pretty" or "being simple" for their own sake; the design principles (wayfinding, feedback, visibility, consistency, mental models, proximity, grouping, mapping, affordance, progressive disclosure, symmetry) are the universal truths behind these needs.

The design process Apple describes: the session uses a fictional trip to Hawaii as a structure, each stage of the trip (airport, rental car, hotel room, dinner, diving) serves to introduce a principle starting from a real-world object, and only then translate that principle to app screens; the Human Interface Guidelines documentation is cited as dating back to 1978.

Principles stated and why, with the translation to app:

Wayfinding (airport signage): signage systems help navigate complex environments by answering where am I, where can I go, what will I find there, what is nearby and how do I get out; they become more specific as you move deeper into the system. In apps, the navigation bar title and the item selected in the tab bar say where the person is; the tab bar and content area say where they can go and what is nearby; simple glyphs and understandable labels suggest what they will find; the Back button offers an exit route. The practical recommendation is to go through the app screen by screen asking whether each one answers these questions.

Feedback (car dashboard): cars give status feedback (gear shown at the same time on the shifter and the instrument cluster, fuel level, speed), completion feedback (sound and vibration of the engine starting, tactile feedback when shifting gears, automatic door lock sound), warning feedback (low fuel, low brake pad, low oil) and error feedback (trying to start the car without gasoline). In apps, unread indicators in Mail give status feedback; indicators in Calendar warn about a schedule conflict; the Camera app uses three simultaneous elements to indicate recording, the red dot, the incrementing timecode and the state of the record button; the sound of a locked iPhone and the animation of an email marked as unread or deleted are completion feedback; the animation and sound of a successful Apple Pay transaction are cited as confirmation feedback that is hard to ignore; inline form validation prevents an error before it happens; and the Things 3 app is cited correcting a nonexistent date on its own, "June 31st", to "July 1st", instead of showing an error. The suggested technique for designing good feedback is to ask someone who has never used the app to use it while narrating out loud what they are thinking, then explain how the app actually works, and compare what was said with what the interface actually communicates.

Visibility: information and control need to be visible for feedback to work; the car's instrument cluster is visually heavy but necessary, hiding that information would harm safety; in apps, badges in Mail deliver status without needing to open each message, and hiding the Clock app's navigation in a hamburger menu would make it harder to see what other functions exist; there is a recognized trade-off, very dense interfaces can overload and slow down the decision, especially for someone new to the app.

Consistency: the symbols and control positions of a car (door lock, window, fuel, brake on the left, accelerator on the right) do not need to be relearned between different cars; in apps, this means paying attention to platform conventions, iconography, terminology, navigation schemes and common flows. The detailed example is the "sharrow" glyph (an arrow pointing up coming out of a box, used on iOS for the share action), against a generic share icon common on websites and other platforms; even though it is a good glyph, it is not the symbol that the iOS user already recognizes, so the right choice for consistency is the "sharrow". Internal consistency, keeping a single visual style across the app's glyphs and a limited number of fonts, sizes and colors, gives the sense that the choices were deliberate.

Mental models (hotel bathroom faucet): a mental model is the simplified representation each person has of how a system works (system model, for example two water sources, hot and cold, that mix, with variable latency until the temperature changes) and of how they interact with it (interaction model, adjusting temperature and flow through the levers). When the real system matches this model, it feels intuitive; when it does not match, it feels unintuitive. The illustrative story is that of a fictional faucet designer, Mortimer, who swaps the traditional logic (one hot water lever, one cold) for one temperature lever and one flow lever, an objectively rational change, but one that breaks the expectation of the person using it, because the faucet looks identical to a common one but behaves differently; labels and small shape variations are described as weak remedies for this kind of mismatch. The recommendation for apps that already exist is that changing the mental model of something established is risky, it gets riskier the more familiar the person is with the product, and it is only worth moving forward when there is certainty, proven by testing, that the change is a clear win.

Proximity (bedroom light switches): the closer a control is to the object it affects, the more a connection between the two is assumed, which also makes sense from an ergonomic point of view; in interfaces, proximity also expresses the relationship between controls, for example separating a curtain switch from the light switches makes it easier to remember which is which.

Grouping: grouping helps understand relationships between elements and gives structure to the design. Keynote example, proximity associates the View menu with the slide navigator and the canvas area, and the object creation tools are positioned right above the canvas. Sketch example, the grouping controls, the Transform and Edit tools, the Pathfinding operations and the Layer Ordering actions appear grouped together. The need for proximity and grouping grows with the size of the interface, but remains relevant on small screens, iPad, iPhone and Apple Watch.

Mapping: controls should resemble the objects they affect, the control for a curtain that goes up and down should mimic the curtain's actual movement; the order of controls should mirror the physical layout of the objects they affect, such as light switches organized in the same arrangement as the ceiling lights they control. The presence of labels is described as a sign that the mapping is not clear, but not as a good solution, because reading takes time. In UI, adjusting a horizontal property maps better to a horizontal slider, and adjusting rotation maps better to a dial; the most direct mapping of all is direct manipulation, with pointer on macOS or gestures on iOS.

Affordance (dinner plate): the physical characteristics of an object suggest the actions possible with it, a smooth plate suggests turning or sliding, its edge suggests picking up or lifting; affordance is not an attribute of the object itself, it is a relationship between the person and the object, it varies according to each person's physical ability, and it tends to be perceived more strongly when linked to an action more likely to be taken. In UI, sliders afford dragging a knob along a track, and a subtle shadow around the knob suggests that it moves independently; dials afford rotation; buttons afford tapping or clicking, and rounded corners create the visual connection to an actual physical button; animation can also communicate affordance, as in the Weather app example, which slides slightly when tapped, suggesting that the content can be scrolled.

Progressive disclosure (ordering a cheeseburger at a restaurant, step by step, meat doneness, cheese type, extras, side): manages complexity by revealing options from simple to complex, hiding what is less essential for basic tasks. The rule explicitly cited is the 80/20 rule, "80 percent of a system's effects come from 20 percent of its causes"; applied to an app, this means that 80 percent of the benefit comes from 20 percent of the actions offered, or that 80 percent of people use only 20 percent of the functions, which justifies hiding the rest behind progressive disclosure. The example is the print dialog, which immediately shows only the printer, number of copies and page range, leaving more options one click away, which reduces clutter and reduces the chance of someone changing a setting they don't understand.

Symmetry (snorkeling): reflective or bilateral, radial or rotational, and translational symmetry are described as ubiquitous in nature and associated, for evolutionary reasons, with health, stability, balance, order and beauty; symmetrical elements, even when not physically connected, are perceived as a single coherent object. In apps, the Weather app uses reflective symmetry (key elements centered on an axis, counterbalanced with each other), a pattern repeated in Camera, Clock and Phone; translational symmetry gives a sense of structure through repetition, like the list of cities and times in the Clock app or of locations in the Weather app.

In closing, the talk acknowledges that the principles themselves can come into conflict with each other, and that design is, in large part, resolving those differences; it also warns that too much of any principle becomes a problem (too much feedback is annoying, too much visibility is distracting, too much progressive disclosure makes flows inefficient), and that the relevance of each principle varies according to platform, screen size, use case and the experience level of whoever will use the app.

Literal quotes: "They tell us why we should do those things." "Good feedback is like having a conversation with the person who designed it."

## Designing Sound (id: wwdc2017_803, 34.8 min)

Basis: transcript and 28 of 28 frame sheets viewed, codes checked. Source: https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2017/803/.

Central thesis: sound is a design layer as relevant as the visual, an almost universal language that gives the product a "voice", and every sound, whether a notification or a UI element, must go through the same iterative process of recording, testing and refinement as a graphic element.

The design process Apple describes: first recognize the role sound already plays outside apps, church bells marking time and place for centuries, the sound of brakes as an early warning system, the click of a seatbelt as quick confirmation, the sound of someone's footsteps revealing emotional state. From there, an exercise in creating a notification sound for a fictional app, Toast Modern (originally created in a prototyping session called "Fake It Till You Make It", about finding expensive toast in San Francisco), following starting questions: what is the essence of what the app does; what emotion should be evoked on receiving the notification; how should the sound match the app's aesthetic; and then record inspiring sound sources from there.

Principles stated and why: when an app does not design its own notification sound, it gets stuck with the system's default sound, which says nothing about where it comes from or what it means, which is described as a lost opportunity for brand identity. A good notification sound needs to be distinguishable (recognizable as belonging to that specific app), match the app's aesthetic (a "cartoony" app calls for different sounds than a minimalist app), be discreet and repeatable (not annoying even when playing several times a day, tested by living with the sound for a week before validating it), and be clear and clean (cutting through ambient noise without being abrasive). A good UI sound is used with restraint, seeking balance between no sound and too much sound, has a lower volume than a notification sound (because the phone is usually in hand), and helps and delights when combined with haptics and animation.

Concrete interface-building techniques: exercise with the Toast Modern app, recording real sounds (toaster ejecting toast, butter being spread, toast breaking, bite of toast), later complemented by a timer sound and a chime that were missing from the initial palette; testing of candidates discarding "harsh" sounds (like the pure sound of the toaster ejecting) until arriving at a combination of timer with chime; the process is described as a base of inspiration that a professional sound designer can later turn into something more unique and abstract, like the real example of the Dark Sky app, cited as a sound that works well for being recognizable, conveying the message through a "liquid" quality without being a literal recording of rain, sounding friendly, and being simple and clean. iOS 7 message sound (played live during the session), designed to cut through street or airport noise, be clean, simple and neutral, since it can accompany any content (unlike Dark Sky, which only means one thing); a cited figure on volume of use, at one point during the Super Bowl as many as 380,000 messages per second were sent. Calendar's default sound, recorded from a kalimba, an uncommon instrument that gives the sound character while keeping it simple. Apple Watch sounds, recorded from the watch's real materials, stainless steel, aluminum and gold, physically striking the case, to create the illusion that the sound comes from the device itself; used in the notification and in the watch's tap, combined with haptics. iOS keyboard, inspired by the typewriter (the satisfaction of the letter's impact on paper) and by the computer keyboard (a "live" sound, not every key sounds the same); the space bar, modifier keys and backspace have their own sounds, and the sound's volume drops subtly as the person types faster. iPhone 7 Home button, which does not physically move (it is solid and waterproof) and uses the Taptic Engine; the precisely synchronized combination of sound and haptics creates a multisensory experience, with a subtle, quiet click added to the Taptic Engine's tap, allowing two configurable click styles; the cited finding is that the same tactile vibration, synchronized with a "sharp and precise" sound, is perceived as more precise, and synchronized with another sound is perceived as less precise, even though it is exactly the same vibration. Apple TV transitions, designed to convey a sense of depth (through the sound's special character) and of movement (the sound's envelope similar to an object passing nearby), with two different sounds indicating forward or backward direction.

Final process tips: think about sound from the start of the project, asking whether the app sends frequent notifications, whether sound can play a role in the brand, whether the UI benefits from an audible component, and how the app would be understood with no graphical interface at all; "silence is golden", illustrated with the hypothetical exaggeration of Maps making a sound for every element in 3D; games can justify a complete sound world, but that should not apply to every app; always offer the option to turn off UI sounds; synchronize sound, haptics and animation with precision, offsetting the sound relative to the haptic by just ten milliseconds already changes the perception of the experience; use a spectrogram to inspect silence at the start, background noise and an abrupt cut at the end of a recording; GarageBand's "iChat Voice" preset, with a denoiser function, works even on sounds that are not voice; the iPhone's speaker has a physical limitation at low frequencies, which justifies filtering out unused frequencies; always test on the final target device (iPhone and iPad separately, and also with headphones); after the prototyping phase, bring in a sound designer (general concept) and a sound engineer (cleanup and preparation for everyday use).

Simple tools suggested for recording and editing: Voice Memos (any sound), Music Memos (musical ideas, integrates with GarageBand), GarageBand on Mac (recording and editing), Logic Pro (more advanced users).

Literal quotes: "Sound has this magical ability to calm us down." "Silence is golden."

## What this group reveals about the Apple way

Putting the person before the task is treated as the root criterion, not as a nice adjective: video 802 goes as far as preferring the term "human interface" to "user interface" and uses that to justify all the other principles; the same logic reappears in not overloading with permissions or sign-up right at the opening (816), in restricting alerts to the essential (813), and in "respect the player" as the closing of 811.

Platform consistency beats personal design preference, even when the alternative is objectively good: the example of the "sharrow" glyph versus a generic sharing icon in 802 is the most explicit case, but the same discipline shows up in the standardized terminology (Alert, Navigation Bar, Tool Bar, Switch) of 809, in the minimum tap target of 44 by 44 points of 811, and in the common skeleton of size classes and Dynamic Type of 812.

Progressive disclosure, in the broad sense of "revealing less first", cuts across very different domains: the cheeseburger and print dialog example in 802, the three-part game tutorials with Skip in 811, the permission timing in 816, the criterion for when to use an alert in 813, and the layered structure itself (Short Look, Long Look, Quick Actions) of a notification in 817.

Rapid prototyping and testing with outside people is described as a mandatory step of the process, not an optional luxury: the timer prototype made in Keynote in 60 seconds (818), the technique of asking an outside person to narrate app use out loud (802), and the insistence on showing more than telling with video or an interactive prototype (809) point to the same habit.

Sound and haptics get the same iterative rigor as the visual, record, test, discard, refine: the Toast Modern process in 803 mirrors the Keynote timer process in 818 and the optical weight adjustment process for the glyphs in 823; the precise synchronization between sound and haptic on the Home button in 803 echoes the centrality of multisensory feedback described in 802.

Craft at the level of optical detail is treated as part of the design work, not as last-minute polish: the optical positioning nudges on glyphs in 823, the fine-tuning of spacing and typographic contrast for reading at small size in 815, and the custom corner curves on icons in 822 show the same pattern of attention.

Cultural sensitivity is treated as a design responsibility, not just a text translation one: gestures, mail iconography and the owl association in 819 follow the same caution as the "delicious" glyph exercise in 823, where even an expressive face is discarded over the risk of reading as inappropriate in some cultures.
